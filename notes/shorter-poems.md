# Shorter Poems: A Lover's Complaint, The Passionate Pilgrim, The Phoenix and the Turtle

## What happens

**A Lover's Complaint.** A narrator overhears a weeping woman by a river tearing up letters and throwing rings and love-tokens into the water. An old man who once knew the court sits by her and asks what happened. She tells him: a young man of extraordinary beauty and eloquence, whose charm made everyone who saw him imagine themselves his, courted her; she knew his record ("This man's untrue"), held out, and was finally taken by a speech in which he said that all his previous conquests were errors of the blood, not the mind, laid their gifts at her feet as tribute, told her he had even seduced a nun, and wept. She yielded; he left. She ends by saying that if he came back with the same tears, she would fall again. The old man never replies.

**The Passionate Pilgrim.** A miscellany of twenty short poems: variants of two sonnets (138 and 144), four small poems about Venus failing to seduce Adonis, poems on inconstant mistresses, on youth and age, on beauty's brevity, a cynical manual on how to win a woman, the invitation "Live with me and be my love" with its refusal, and a closing poem on the nightingale, flatterers, and the one friend who stays in misery.

**The Phoenix and the Turtle.** A summons to birds for a funeral; an anthem declaring that love and constancy are dead because the phoenix and the turtle-dove, who loved so that two were one, have burned together; Reason, confounded by their union, composes the threnos; the threnos declares that beauty, truth, and rarity lie in the urn, that the pair left no posterity, and that truth and beauty are now buried. Those who are "either true or fair" are invited to sigh a prayer.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **(Complaint) Knowing a man is false is no protection against him.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "For further I could say, 'This man's untrue', / And knew the patterns of his foul beguiling; / Heard where his plants in others' orchards grew, / Saw how deceits were gilded in his smiling; / Knew vows were ever brokers to defiling" (st. 25). "Who ever shunn'd by precedent / The destin'd ill she must herself assay ... when we rage, advice is often seen / By blunting us to make our wills more keen" (st. 23). "O appetite, from judgement stand aloof!" (st. 24).
   - *What the action does with it:* She had the knowledge, used it as a shield ("With safest distance I mine honour shielded"), and fell. The poem's last stanza goes further: all that "borrowed motion" of his "Would yet again betray the fore-betrayed, / And new pervert a reconciled maid" (st. 47). She does not say she has learned; she says she would do it again. The poem observes that experience does not immunise.

2. **(Complaint) The seducer's best instrument is a confession.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* His speech: "All my offences that abroad you see / Are errors of the blood, none of the mind: / Love made them not" (st. 27); "Harm have I done to them, but ne'er was harmed; / Kept hearts in liveries, but mine own was free" (st. 28); "My parts had pow'r to charm a sacred nun" (st. 37). And then the tears: "what a hell of witchcraft lies / In the small orb of one particular tear!" (st. 42).
   - *What the action does with it:* He does not deny his history; he displays it as tribute and offers the other women's gifts to her. The poem shows that admitting everything, with tears, is the technique, and that she knew it was a technique ("his passion, but an art of craft", st. 43) and that the knowing did not help. "His poison'd me, and mine did him restore."

3. **(Complaint) The one who preaches chastity most is the one most burning.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Against the thing he sought he would exclaim; / When he most burned in heart-wish'd luxury, / He preach'd pure maid, and prais'd cold chastity" (st. 45).
   - *What the action does with it:* The observation is confirmed by the whole of his speech, which claims love's power is holy and beyond vows ("O most potential love! Vow, bond, nor space, / In thee hath neither sting, knot, nor confine", st. 38) while asking her to break hers. The frame lets no one answer him; the old man who was asked for counsel says nothing. The poem presents a problem with no adviser.

4. **(Pilgrim) Mutual lying is a working arrangement, and the poem that says so appears twice in the corpus.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "When my love swears that she is made of truth, / I do believe her, though I know she lies ... Therefore, I'll lie with love, and love with me, / Since that our faults in love thus smother'd be" (I). "Two loves I have, of comfort and despair ... I guess one angel in another's hell" (II).
   - *What the action does with it:* These are variants of Sonnets 138 and 144 and have the same shape: a speaker who knows the deception, names it, and continues. In this miscellany they sit beside "Fair is my love, but not so fair as fickle ... Was this a lover, or a lecher whether? / Bad in the best, though excellent in neither" (VII), so that the collection as a whole takes the mistress's falseness as given and asks only what the lover does with it.

5. **(Pilgrim) Desire runs away from what it cannot have; the refused always flees.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Four poems repeat the Venus and Adonis situation in miniature: "Then fell she on her back, fair queen, and toward: / He rose and ran away; ah, fool too froward!" (IV); "'O Jove,' quoth she, 'why was not I a flood?'" (VI); "She showed hers: he saw more wounds than one, / And blushing fled" (IX); "as she fetched breath, away he skips, / And would not take her meaning nor her pleasure" (XI).
   - *What the action does with it:* Each poem ends with the boy running. The collection returns to this image four times, and the last version turns it into the speaker's own wish: "Ah, that I had my lady at this bay, / To kiss and clip me till I run away!" (XI). The man imagines being the one pursued and still imagines running.

6. **(Pilgrim) Youth and age cannot share a life; beauty is a glass broken in an hour.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Crabbed age and youth cannot live together: / Youth is full of pleasance, age is full of care ... Age, I do abhor thee; youth, I do adore thee" (XII). "Beauty is but a vain and doubtful good ... A doubtful good, a gloss, a glass, a flower, / Lost, vaded, broken, dead within an hour" (XIII).
   - *What the action does with it:* These are set as songs without situation, and the collection places them beside I, in which the speaker is "past the best" and lies about it. The miscellany's structure makes the claim that age and youth cannot live together into the speaker's own problem rather than a maxim.

7. **(Pilgrim) Cynical advice about women is undercut by the fear of the woman hearing it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* XVIII: "A woman's nay doth stand for nought"; "Think women still to strive with men, / To sin and never for to saint." Then: "But soft, enough,—too much,—I fear / Lest that my mistress hear my song: / She will not stick to round me on th' ear."
   - *What the action does with it:* The poem's confident doctrine ends in the speaker ducking. The one who claims to know how women yield is afraid of one. The collection lets the swagger and the cringe sit in one poem.

8. **(Pilgrim) An invitation to love is answered by its own refutation.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Live with me and be my love, / And we will all the pleasures prove" (XIX). "Love's Answer: If that the world and love were young, / And truth in every shepherd's tongue, / These pretty pleasures might me move / To live with thee and be thy love."
   - *What the action does with it:* The reply is conditional and the condition is false. The collection prints the two together as one poem, so the offer and the knowledge that offers are not kept become a single utterance.

9. **(Pilgrim) Friends in prosperity are many; the friend in misery is the only one.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Words are easy, like the wind; / Faithful friends are hard to find. / Every man will be thy friend / Whilst thou hast wherewith to spend ... But if Fortune once do frown, / Then farewell his great renown" (XX). "He that is thy friend indeed, / He will help thee in thy need: / If thou sorrow, he will weep; / If thou wake, he cannot sleep."
   - *What the action does with it:* The poem begins with the nightingale singing alone while "All thy fellow birds do sing, / Careless of thy sorrowing," and the speaker recognising himself. The test of friendship it proposes is sleeplessness on another's account, which is the condition the sonnets' speaker describes in 27-28 and 61. The miscellany closes on this plain measure.

10. **(Phoenix) Perfect love abolishes number and confounds reason.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "So they lov'd, as love in twain / Had the essence but in one; / Two distincts, division none: / Number there in love was slain." "Either was the other's mine." "Property was thus appalled, / That the self was not the same; / Single nature's double name / Neither two nor one was called." "Love hath reason, reason none, / If what parts can so remain."
   - *What the action does with it:* Reason, "in itself confounded," is the one who composes the funeral song. The poem shows reason defeated by the sight of such a union and then given the last word over its ashes. The thing that cannot be understood is mourned by the faculty that cannot understand it.

11. **(Phoenix) Perfect love leaves nothing behind, and that is not a failure.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Threnos: "Leaving no posterity:— / 'Twas not their infirmity, / It was married chastity." "Death is now the phoenix' nest."
   - *What the action does with it:* This is the exact opposite of the argument of Sonnets 1-17 and of Venus — that beauty must breed or waste. Here childlessness is the mark of the union's purity. The corpus contains both claims, and this poem states the second without hedging.

12. **(Phoenix) After such a death, truth and beauty are only seeming; the invitation to mourn is addressed to people the poem has just said do not exist.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Truth may seem, but cannot be; / Beauty brag, but 'tis not she; / Truth and beauty buried be. / To this urn let those repair / That are either true or fair; / For these dead birds sigh a prayer."
   - *What the action does with it:* The threnos declares truth and beauty buried, then invites those who are "either true or fair" to the urn. Taken strictly, no one qualifies; taken loosely, only those partly so. The poem ends by asking for mourners it has defined out of existence, and lets the reader decide whether to come.

## What it teaches

A Lover's Complaint is the clearest statement in this group, and what it states is unwelcome: that knowing how you will be deceived does not stop it, and that the person who has been deceived will not necessarily be wiser for it. The woman is not stupid; she lists his crimes before he speaks. She is beaten by a confession and a tear, and she tells the old man that the same confession and tear would beat her again. The old man was asked for counsel and gives none, and the narrator who overheard everything adds nothing. If there is a lesson, it is that there is no counsel for this, and the poem is honest enough to leave the chair empty.

The Passionate Pilgrim is a collection rather than a sequence, but its arrangement makes an argument: the mistress is false, the lover knows it and lies back, the boy runs from the goddess, age cannot keep youth, beauty breaks within the hour, the manual for seducers ends in fear of the seduced, the shepherd's invitation is refused on the ground that shepherds lie, and the last word is that the only friend is the one who cannot sleep when you cannot. Read together, these poems say that love's words are cheap and that the test of any bond is what it costs the other person in the night.

The Phoenix and the Turtle says the opposite of almost everything else in the corpus, and says it as if there were no argument: two can be one, reason is wrong about this, the union leaves no child and that is its perfection, and when it is over, truth and beauty are gone from the world. The poem does not defend any of this. It is the one place in this group where love is not a predator, a lie, or a wound, and it is a funeral. What a reader is left with is the thought that the corpus can imagine love without deceit only as something already dead, and that the mourners it calls for are people it has just said no longer exist.

## Preoccupations

- knowledge of deceit as no defence against it
- confession and tears as the seducer's tools
- mutual lying as the ordinary condition of love
- the refused always fleeing; the pursuer never satisfied
- beauty and youth as a glass broken in an hour
- friendship measured by what it costs in the night
- perfect union as the death of number and reason
- love without deceit imaginable only as already dead
