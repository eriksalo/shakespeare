# The Life of Timon of Athens

## What happens

A rich Athenian lord gives everything away: he pays friends' debts, dowers his servant, buys poems and paintings, hands out jewels and horses at feasts, and will not hear his steward tell him the money is gone. When creditors close in, he sends to the friends he has enriched for loans; each refuses with a different excuse. Timon invites them to one last feast, serves them covered dishes of warm water, throws it in their faces, and leaves Athens cursing the whole city. In the woods, digging for roots, he finds gold, which brings him exactly the visitors he hates: a rebel general and his whores, the cynic Apemantus, thieves, the poet and painter, and finally senators begging him to come back and save the city from the general he has just funded. Only his steward Flavius follows him out of love. Timon writes his own epitaph and dies by the sea. Alcibiades, whom the senate had banished for pleading mercy for a friend, takes the city on terms, reads Timon's epitaph, and promises to make "war breed peace."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Giving that claims to want nothing in return is often a way of buying people, and the giver does not know it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Timon, I.2: "I gave it freely ever, and there's none / Can truly say he gives if he receives." In the same speech: "what need we have any friends if we should ne'er have need of 'em?... what better or properer can we call our own than the riches of our friends?" Timon, II.2: "I am wealthy in my friends." Later, to Flavius, IV.3: "Is not thy kindness subtle, covetous, / A usuring kindness and as rich men deal gifts, / Expecting in return twenty for one?"
   - *What the action does with it:* Timon's two sentences about friendship contradict each other within a single speech, and the action takes the second one seriously. He had treated his friends as a bank, and when he draws on it they behave like a bank: "this is no time to lend money, especially upon bare friendship without security" (Lucullus, III.1). His own later suspicion of Flavius describes exactly what his own bounty had been. Flavius says it plainly: "Undone by goodness!... bounty, that makes gods, does still mar men" (IV.2).

2. **People's ears are open to flattery and closed to counsel, and they cannot learn until they feel.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Apemantus, I.2: "O, that men's ears should be / To counsel deaf, but not to flattery!" Flavius, II.2: "He will not hear till feel." Flavius to Timon: "You would not hear me, / At many leisures I proposed."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play shows Timon refusing the accounts ("you would throw them off / And say you found them in mine honesty"), putting off the steward ("Near? Why then, another time I'll hear thee," I.2), and swearing not to listen to Apemantus ("an you begin to rail on society once, I am sworn not to give regard to you"). The claim is confirmed exactly; and when Timon does finally feel, he does not learn moderation but swings to the opposite extreme.

3. **Friendship that depends on feasting ends when the feasting ends.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Flavius, II.2: "Feast-won, fast-lost; one cloud of winter showers, / These flies are couched." Apemantus's grace, I.2: "Grant I may never prove so fond / To trust man on his oath or bond... / Or my friends if I should need 'em." Timon, III.6, aside: "such summer birds are men." The First Stranger, III.2: "Men must learn now with pity to dispense, / For policy sits above conscience."
   - *What the action does with it:* Act III is a catalogue of refusals, each with its own reasoning: Lucullus because lending is bad business, Lucius because he has (he says) just spent his money, Sempronius because he was asked last and his honor is slighted. The variety is the point: ingratitude does not need a single motive, it will find whichever excuse is at hand. The Stranger who has never received anything from Timon is the one who says he would have helped him.

4. **Gold reverses every value; it is a "visible god" that makes anything true.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Timon, IV.3: "Thus much of this will make / Black white, foul fair, wrong right, / Base noble, old young, coward valiant"; "thou visible god, / That solder'st close impossibilities / And mak'st them kiss." Timon, V.1: "What a god's gold, / That he is worshipped in a baser temple / Than where swine feed!"
   - *What the action does with it:* The gold Timon digs up proves his own speech. As soon as it is rumored, every kind of person comes to the cave: the general, the whores, the thieves, the artists, the senators. Apemantus predicts it ("Thou wilt be thronged to shortly") and is right. Timon's hatred of mankind is heard only because he is rich again; the senators offer him "absolute power" not for his virtue but because a man with gold and a grievance is dangerous.

5. **Promising has replaced performing; saying has become the deed.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Painter, V.1: "Promising is the very air o' th' time... To promise is most courtly and fashionable; performance is a kind of will or testament which argues a great sickness in his judgment that makes it." Timon to Alcibiades, IV.3: "Promise me friendship, but perform none." Timon, I.2: "ceremony was but devised at first / To set a gloss on faint deeds, hollow welcomes."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Poet and Painter arrive with nothing but a promised poem and a promised picture, and the play lets them explain their theory of promising before Timon beats them off. The senators' offer of "special dignities" is another promise made only once they need him. Against this, Timon's early line about ceremony is the one time he saw clearly, and he saw it while being flattered.

6. **Hating all mankind is a position no man can hold without exception, and the play produces the exceptions.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Alcibiades, IV.3: "Is man so hateful to thee / That art thyself a man?" Timon, IV.3: "I am Misanthropos and hate mankind." Timon, on Flavius, IV.3: "I do proclaim / One honest man, mistake me not, but one; / No more, I pray, and he's a steward." The First Bandit, IV.3: "There is no time so miserable but a man may be true."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play keeps presenting Timon with evidence against his thesis: Flavius weeping and asking to stay, the servants sharing their last coins "for Timon's sake" (IV.2), a bandit talked out of theft by a speech meant to encourage it. Timon acknowledges Flavius and then immediately conditions his gift on Flavius becoming a hater too: "Hate all, curse all, show charity to none." He would rather keep the thesis than the man.

7. **Everything steals; law is theft with a license.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Timon to the thieves, IV.3: "The sun's a thief... the moon's an arrant thief... Each thing's a thief. / The laws, your curb and whip, in their rough power / Has unchecked theft." Timon, IV.1: "Large-handed robbers your grave masters are, / And pill by law." Alcibiades, III.5: "Banish your dotage, banish usury, / That makes the Senate ugly."
   - *What the action does with it:* The senate is shown exactly as described: it lets "coin upon large interest" while refusing Alcibiades's friend mercy and Timon a loan, and it banishes the one soldier who has defended it. So the charge has evidence. But the thieves' reaction undercuts the rhetoric: "Has almost charmed me from my profession by persuading me to it." Universal accusation produces either despair or its own opposite; it does not produce justice.

8. **Whether bearing wrongs is valor or cowardice depends on who is speaking and whether they are in power.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* First Senator, III.5: "He's truly valiant that can wisely suffer / The worst that man can breathe... To revenge is no valour, but to bear." Alcibiades, III.5: "who is man that is not angry?"; "Soldiers should brook as little wrongs as gods." Alcibiades, V.5: "Now the time is flush, / When crouching marrow, in the bearer strong / Cries of itself, 'No more!'"
   - *What the action does with it:* The senators preach patience when they hold the sword and beg for mercy when they do not ("Like a shepherd / Approach the fold and cull th' infected forth, / But kill not all together"). The play then separates two kinds of revenge: Timon's, which wants everyone dead and achieves nothing but his own grave, and Alcibiades's, which stops once the guilty are named ("Fall, and no more"). The senators' doctrine is exposed as self-interest, but the play nonetheless ends with the limited form of forgiveness they asked for.

9. **Art sees the truth about Fortune and does nothing with it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Poet, I.1, describing his allegory: "When Fortune in her shift and change of mood / Spurns down her late beloved, all his dependants... let him slip down, / Not one accompanying his declining foot." The Painter: "'Tis common." Timon, I.1: "The painting is almost the natural man, / For since dishonour traffics with man's nature, / He is but outside."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Poet's poem predicts the plot of the play precisely, and the Poet is one of those who abandon Timon and return when they hear of gold, now planning "a satire against the softness of prosperity" to sell him. Timon's aside is the right judgment: "Must thou needs stand for a villain in thine own work? Wilt thou whip thine own faults in other men?" Knowing the moral is no protection against being its example.

10. **A person who has only known the extremes of fortune never learns the middle, and so cannot be content in either.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Apemantus, IV.3: "The middle of humanity thou never knewest, but the extremity of both ends." Apemantus: "Willing misery / Outlives incertain pomp, is crowned before; / The one is filling still, never complete, / The other, at high wish." Timon, V.1: "My long sickness / Of health and living now begins to mend / And nothing brings me all things."
    - *What the action does with it:* The play makes the two phases of Timon's life mirror each other: in both he hands out gold to anyone who comes; only the words change ("One day he gives us diamonds, next day stones," III.6). Apemantus's diagnosis is accurate, and he is also, as Timon says, not a disinterested witness: "Thou flatter'st misery." Timon never reaches content; he reaches "nothing," which he calls all things, and dies.

11. **Loyalty survives among the low when it has disappeared among the great, and it is paid in sorrow.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Third Servant, IV.2: "Yet do our hearts wear Timon's livery... We are fellows still, / Serving alike in sorrow." Flavius: "Thus part we rich in sorrow, parting poor"; "Whilst I have gold, I'll be his steward still." Flavius, IV.3: "I'd exchange / For this one wish, that you had power and wealth / To requite me by making rich yourself."
    - *What the action does with it:* The servants' parting scene is the one place in the play where people give to each other with no calculation. Flavius is the only person Timon's curse cannot cover, and the play lets him keep his master's company to the end even while Timon orders him away. It is a small answer to a very large accusation, and the play does not pretend it is more than that.

## What it teaches

This is a play about a man who is wrong twice in the same way. In Athens Timon believes everyone loves him; in the woods he believes everyone is a villain. Both are claims about all people at once, and the play is careful to show that both are false by producing particular people who do not fit: the friends who fail him, and then the steward who does not. What Timon cannot do is see one person at a time. Apemantus's line is the key: "The middle of humanity thou never knewest, but the extremity of both ends." The teaching is not that generosity is foolish or that cynicism is wise; it is that a judgment about "mankind" is always a way of avoiding a judgment about someone in front of you.

The harder lesson concerns giving. Timon's bounty looks like the opposite of usury and turns out to be a kind of it. He had not noticed that he wanted anything back until he needed it, and then what he wanted was everything. The play does not say that his friends were right to refuse him; it goes to great lengths to show their refusals as contemptible. But it also shows, through Flavius's wish that Timon had "power and wealth / To requite me by making rich yourself," what a gift that wants nothing actually sounds like. It sounds like a servant, not a lord.

There is a question the play raises and does not answer: whether Timon's hatred is a real insight or a tantrum. His speeches on gold and on universal theft are true in detail (the senate really is a house of usurers; the artists really do come for money), and yet the thieves' reaction is to be "charmed" from their profession, the servants part in fellowship, and Alcibiades, given Timon's gold to destroy Athens, chooses instead to "use the olive with my sword." Everyone who hears Timon's total verdict declines to act on it, including those who agree with it. The play leaves you with an accurate description of a corrupt city and the clear sense that the description, however accurate, is not a way to live. Timon's epitaph tells passers-by to curse and keep walking. The last word, Alcibiades's "faults forgiven," is a quiet refusal to do what the epitaph says.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between what people say and what they do (promising versus performing)
- flattery as the common language of a whole society
- money as the thing that decides what is true and who is loved
- ingratitude, and the many excuses it finds
- generosity that is secretly a demand
- the swing from one extreme to its opposite, with no middle
- faithful service from the low and betrayal from the high
- whether to bear wrongs or avenge them, and who gets to decide
