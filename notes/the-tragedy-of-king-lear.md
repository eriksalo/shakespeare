# The Tragedy of King Lear

## What happens

An old king decides to retire while keeping the name of king, and stages a contest in which his three daughters must say how much they love him in exchange for a share of the kingdom. The two who speak most win everything; the one who refuses to perform gets nothing and is married off to France without a dowry. The two winners immediately strip their father of his retinue, then of shelter, and he goes out into a storm and loses his mind. In parallel, the Earl of Gloucester is tricked by his bastard son Edmund into hunting his legitimate son Edgar, who hides as a mad beggar; Gloucester, caught helping the king, has his eyes torn out and is turned loose, and is led unknowingly by the son he wronged. Cordelia returns with a French army to rescue her father and is reconciled with him, but loses the battle; the two elder sisters, both in love with Edmund, destroy each other; Edmund is killed in a trial by combat with his brother but not before his order to hang Cordelia is carried out. Lear enters carrying her body and dies looking at her lips. The survivors are left to "the gor'd state."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Love cannot be measured by speech, and demanding that it be measured destroys it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lear, I.1: "Which of you shall we say doth love us most? / That we our largest bounty may extend / Where nature doth with merit challenge." Cordelia, I.1: "I love your majesty / According to my bond; no more nor less." France, I.1: "Love's not love / When it is mingled with regards that stands / Aloof from the entire point."
   - *What the action does with it:* The two daughters who speak best ("Dearer than eyesight, space, and liberty") turn out to have nothing behind the words; the daughter who says "Nothing" is the only one who comes back for him. The play confirms Cordelia's position completely, but it does not let her off: her refusal to give even a little, and Kent's blunt intervention, trigger a catastrophe that kills everyone including her. Being right about love is not the same as handling it well.

2. **You cannot give away the substance of power and keep its honors; the name without the thing is nothing.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lear, I.1: "Only we shall retain / The name, and all the addition to a king." Fool, I.4: "thou hast pared thy wit o' both sides, and left nothing i' the middle"; "Now thou art an O without a figure... I am a fool, thou art nothing." Regan, II.4, when Lear says "I gave you all": "And in good time you gave it."
   - *What the action does with it:* The retinue goes from a hundred knights to fifty to twenty-five to "What need one?" within two scenes. Every dignity Lear expects (a servant unstocked, a daughter who comes when called) is denied on the simple ground that he no longer has the power to require it. The Fool's riddles are exactly right and are the only thing the play offers as an alternative to learning it the hard way.

3. **What people need is more than what keeps them alive; superfluity is what makes a human life different from a beast's.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lear, II.4: "O, reason not the need: our basest beggars / Are in the poorest thing superfluous: / Allow not nature more than nature needs, / Man's life is cheap as beast's."
   - *What the action does with it:* Lear is then reduced precisely to the beast's condition, and in the storm he looks at the naked Edgar and reverses himself: "unaccommodated man is no more but such a poor, bare, forked animal as thou art" (III.4) and tears off his own clothes. But the play finds something at the bottom that the speech about need did not anticipate: stripped of everything, he feels pity for the first time ("Poor naked wretches... O, I have ta'en / Too little care of this!"). The claim about need is true, and losing everything is also the only thing that teaches him what others lack.

4. **Comfort blinds; a person only sees what they feel.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lear, III.4: "Take physic, pomp; / Expose thyself to feel what wretches feel." Gloucester, IV.1: "I stumbled when I saw"; "Let the superfluous and lust-dieted man, / That slaves your ordinance, that will not see / Because he does not feel, feel your power quickly." Gloucester, IV.6: "I see it feelingly."
   - *What the action does with it:* Both old men genuinely learn this. Lear in madness sees through office and robes (IV.6); Gloucester blind recognizes the son he refused to hear. But the knowledge comes after it could do any good and costs them eyes, wits, and lives. The play lets the insight stand as true while refusing to let it be a consolation.

5. **"Nature" names two opposite things: the bond that ties child to parent, and the appetite that ignores every bond.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Edmund, I.2: "Thou, Nature, art my goddess; to thy law / My services are bound." Lear, II.4: "The offices of nature, bond of childhood, / Effects of courtesy, dues of gratitude." Lear, III.6: "Is there any cause in nature that makes these hard hearts?" Albany, IV.2: "That nature which contemns its origin / Cannot be bordered certain in itself."
   - *What the action does with it:* Edmund's nature, followed consistently, produces exactly what Albany predicts: the two sisters poison and stab themselves over him, and Edmund dies acknowledging "The wheel is come full circle; I am here" (V.3). So the appetite-nature destroys itself. But the bond-nature does not protect anyone either: Cordelia, who honored the bond exactly, is hanged. The play does not let either sense of the word win.

6. **Flattery is a lie about invulnerability, and plain speech is punished for telling the truth about it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Kent, I.1: "To plainness honour's bound / When majesty falls to folly." Lear, IV.6: "They flattered me like a dog... they told me I was everything; 'tis a lie, I am not ague-proof." Cornwall's counter-view, II.2: "These kind of knaves I know which in this plainness / Harbour more craft and more corrupter ends / Than twenty silly-ducking observants."
   - *What the action does with it:* Kent is banished, then stocked; Cordelia is disinherited; the Fool is threatened with the whip for speaking true and would be whipped for lying. Yet the plain speakers are the only ones who stay with Lear, and the play's last line is Edgar's "Speak what we feel, not what we ought to say." Cornwall's suspicion of performed bluntness is not foolish (Kent's railing at Oswald is excessive), but the action makes clear where the real craft lies.

7. **People keep asserting that the gods are just, and the play keeps declining to confirm it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Gloucester, IV.1: "As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods, / They kill us for their sport." Albany, IV.2, hearing Cornwall is dead: "This shows you are above, / You justicers." Edgar, V.3: "The gods are just, and of our pleasant vices / Make instruments to plague us." Lear, V.3: "Upon such sacrifices, my Cordelia, / The gods themselves throw incense."
   - *What the action does with it:* Every one of these is followed by evidence against it. Albany says "The gods defend her!" and Lear walks in with Cordelia dead. The reprieve is sent and arrives too late. Kent asks "Is this the promis'd end?" and Edgar answers "Or image of that horror?" The one place something like justice happens, the combat with Edmund, cannot undo the hanging. The play leaves the question open and makes the cost of its openness as high as possible.

8. **There is no bottom; as long as you can name the worst, you are not there yet.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Edgar, IV.1: "To be worst, / The lowest and most dejected thing of fortune, / Stands still in esperance, lives not in fear"; then, seeing his blinded father: "The worst is not / So long as we can say 'This is the worst.'"
   - *What the action does with it:* The play confirms this with unusual deliberateness. Edgar's speech is refuted within ten lines. In V.3 he himself says his father's death "would have seem'd a period / To such as love not sorrow; but another... would make much more, / And top extremity," and the play then does exactly that with Cordelia. Each time it could stop, it goes further.

9. **The only available stance toward suffering is to endure it; endurance is required, not rewarded.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Edgar, V.2: "Men must endure / Their going hence, even as their coming hither; / Ripeness is all." Gloucester: "And that's true too." Lear, IV.6: "Thou must be patient; we came crying hither"; "When we are born, we cry that we are come / To this great stage of fools." Gloucester, IV.6, after the false fall: "henceforth I'll bear / Affliction till it do cry out itself / 'Enough, enough,' and die."
   - *What the action does with it:* Gloucester's heart "Burst smilingly" between joy and grief; Lear dies not reconciled but staring at Cordelia's lips; Kent asks that no one "upon the rack of this rough world / Stretch him out longer." The play honors endurance by giving it to its best people, and shows that what endurance buys is only the chance to see more loss.

10. **Authority is mostly costume, and justice depends on who wears the robes.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Lear, IV.6: "There thou mightst behold / The great image of authority: a dog's obeyed in office"; "Robes and furr'd gowns hide all. Plate sin with gold, / And the strong lance of justice hurtless breaks." Cornwall, III.7: "our power / Shall do a courtesy to our wrath, which men / May blame, but not control." Goneril, V.3: "the laws are mine, not thine: / Who can arraign me for't?"
    - *What the action does with it:* Cornwall's blinding of Gloucester proves his own claim; so does Goneril's. The only correction comes from below: a nameless servant who has served Cornwall "ever since I was a child" draws on him and gives him his death wound. The mad trial Lear holds in the farmhouse (III.6) convicts a joint-stool. The formal trial by combat does kill Edmund, but the law arrives in time to punish, not to save.

11. **Faithfulness survives at the bottom of the social order when it has vanished from the top.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Kent, I.4: "to serve him truly that will put me in trust." Fool, II.4: "But I will tarry; the fool will stay, / And let the wise man fly." First Servant, III.7: "better service have I never done you / Than now to bid you hold." The Old Man, IV.1, tenant "these fourscore years."
    - *What the action does with it:* The servant is stabbed from behind by Regan; the Fool disappears without explanation after III.6; Kent, after following "your sad steps" through everything, is not even recognized by Lear at the end ("I'll see that straight"). Fidelity is the one steady good in the play and the play pays it nothing.

12. **Reason and madness trade places; the sane are fools and the mad see.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Fool, I.5: "Thou shouldst not have been old till thou hadst been wise." Lear, I.5: "O, let me not be mad, not mad, sweet heaven!" Edgar, IV.6: "O, matter and impertinency mix'd! / Reason in madness!" Gloucester, IV.6: "Better I were distract: / So should my thoughts be sever'd from my griefs."
    - *What the action does with it:* Lear's sharpest judgments about justice, lust, and flattery are delivered crowned with weeds. When sleep and Cordelia restore him (IV.7: "I am a very foolish fond old man"), his sanity lasts just long enough for him to feel the full weight of her death. The play does not romanticize madness; it shows that clarity and the capacity to be hurt arrive together.

## What it teaches

The play is built on a simple error that almost anyone could make: an old man wants to be loved and wants to hear it, and he confuses the saying with the thing. Everything that follows is the world's answer to that confusion. What is hard is that the answer is so disproportionate. Lear is vain, rash, and cruel to Cordelia for a few minutes; he is then stripped, driven mad, and made to carry his daughter's body. Gloucester believes a forged letter; he loses his eyes. The play does not pretend the punishment fits. "I am a man / More sinn'd against than sinning" is a mad king's line, but the action does not refute it.

What the play does offer is a precise account of how people come to know things. Nobody in it learns by being told. Kent tells Lear in the first scene exactly what is true and is banished for it; the Fool tells him in rhymes for two acts and is threatened with the whip. Lear learns only when the rain wets him and the thunder will not stop at his bidding, and Gloucester learns only when he can no longer see. "I see it feelingly" is the play's theory of knowledge: feeling precedes seeing, and the comfortable cannot feel. If you take this seriously it is a hard thing to be told, since it implies that your own understanding of other people's lives is limited by what you have been spared.

The question the play will not settle is whether any of this means anything. Characters keep offering theories (the gods are just, the gods are cruel, the stars govern us, the wheel comes full circle) and the play places each one next to an event that makes it look foolish. The strongest evidence it gives for meaning is not an argument but a handful of people: Cordelia saying "No cause, no cause," Kent following in disguise, a servant drawing his sword on his master. The strongest evidence against is that all three are dead or disregarded by the end. A reader has to decide whether goodness that gets no reward and changes nothing is worth something anyway. The play seems to think so, and seems to know how little that settles.

The last lines insist on one modest discipline: "Speak what we feel, not what we ought to say." That is where the play began, with a daughter who would not say what she ought. It ends by endorsing her, at a price that makes the endorsement hard to take as comfort.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between what people say and what they feel (love as performance vs. love as bond)
- ingratitude of children toward parents, and parents' injustice toward children
- the stripping away of rank, clothes, reason, and sight until only the bare human is left
- suffering as the only real teacher, and knowledge that comes too late
- whether the gods, nature, or anything at all is just
- faithful service from the low and betrayal from the high
- madness as a form of seeing
- "nothing" as a word that keeps turning into everything
- the difference between need and bare survival
