# Love's Labour's Lost

## What happens

The King of Navarre and three lords sign an oath to spend three years in study, fasting, little sleep and no contact with women, so that their fame may outlast death. Berowne signs under protest, pointing out that the French Princess is due that week on state business. She arrives with three ladies and is lodged in the fields outside the gate; each lord falls for one of the women within the day. A Spanish braggart, Armado, falls for a dairymaid, Jaquenetta, and his love letter and Berowne's sonnet to Rosaline are delivered by the clown Costard to the wrong women. In a single scene each lord overhears the next confessing in verse until all four are exposed, and Berowne argues them out of their oath on the grounds that women's eyes are the true academy. The lords come to woo disguised as Russians; the ladies, forewarned, swap tokens and masks so that each man swears love to the wrong woman, then mock them when they return as themselves. A pageant of the Nine Worthies performed by the schoolmaster, curate, braggart, clown and page is baited by the lords until a messenger arrives: the Princess's father is dead. The women leave; each imposes a year's task on her suitor (the King to a hermitage, Berowne to jest among the dying) before any promise will be heard. Armado, whose Jaquenetta is pregnant, vows three years at the plough. Two songs, of Spring and Winter, close the play.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **An oath sworn against appetite is sworn to be broken.**
   - *Where spoken:* Berowne, I.1: "Necessity will make us all forsworn / Three thousand times within this three years' space; / For every man with his affects is born, / Not by might mastered, but by special grace." And: "I am the last that will last keep his oath." IV.3: "Young blood doth not obey an old decree."
   - *What the action does with it:* All four break their vow within days, Berowne first. Armado, the court's licensed fool, breaks his in the same scene he states it ("I shall be forsworn, which is a great argument of falsehood, if I love," I.2). The play confirms Berowne wholesale and then, in its last scene, has the women refuse to accept any further oath: "Yet swear not, lest ye be forsworn again" (Katharine, V.2). What replaces swearing is a year of doing.

2. **The pursuit of fame as a shield against death is interrupted by death itself.**
   - *Where spoken:* The King, I.1: "Let fame, that all hunt after in their lives, / Live registered upon our brazen tombs, / And then grace us in the disgrace of death; / When, spite of cormorant devouring time, / Th' endeavour of this present breath may buy / That honour which shall bate his scythe's keen edge."
   - *What the action does with it:* The academy never meets. What arrives instead of fame is Marcadé with two words, "Even so," and the Princess's father is dead. The King's attempt to argue that grief should yield to love ("to wail friends lost / Is not by much so wholesome-profitable / As to rejoice at friends but newly found") is answered: "I understand you not. My griefs are double." The play opens with a plan to outwit time and closes with time's plain victory.

3. **Learning that is only words produces people who cannot see what is in front of them.**
   - *Where spoken:* Berowne, I.1: "Small have continual plodders ever won, / Save base authority from others' books," and "Too much to know is to know naught but fame, / And every godfather can give a name." Moth, V.1, of Holofernes and Nathaniel: "They have been at a great feast of languages and stolen the scraps."
   - *What the action does with it:* Holofernes cannot hear that Dull is right about the deer; he can only correct his Latin. Nathaniel echoes him. Their learning is displayed to each other and to no purpose, and when they perform the Worthies, it collapses in front of an audience. But the play does not simply side with Berowne: his own great speech against study (IV.3) is requested as "some salve for perjury" and "some tricks, some quillets, how to cheat the devil," and he supplies it. The play shows two kinds of empty learning, the pedant's and the wit's.

4. **A habit of language cannot be shed by resolution.**
   - *Where spoken:* Berowne, V.2: "Taffeta phrases, silken terms precise, / Three-piled hyperboles, spruce affectation, / Figures pedantical: these summer flies / Have blown me full of maggot ostentation. / I do forswear them ... Henceforth my wooing mind shall be expressed / In russet yeas and honest kersey noes."
   - *What the action does with it:* His next sentence contains "sans," and Rosaline catches it: "Sans 'sans,' I pray you." He admits "I have a trick / Of the old rage ... I'll leave it by degrees." The play lets him renounce ornament in ornamented verse, and then sentences him to a year of using his wit on people who cannot enjoy it. Reform of speech is treated as a long labour, not an act of will.

5. **Praise is a form of sale, and the women price it accordingly.**
   - *Where spoken:* The Princess, II.1: "Beauty is bought by judgement of the eye, / Not uttered by base sale of chapmen's tongues." IV.1, paying the forester for an honest word: "Here, good my glass, take this for telling true," and "A giving hand, though foul, shall have fair praise." Rosaline, V.2, of Berowne's letter: "Much in the letters, nothing in the praise." The Princess, V.2: "in our maiden council rated them / At courtship, pleasant jest, and courtesy, / As bombast and as lining to the time."
   - *What the action does with it:* The men's sonnets, each comparing a woman to the sun or a goddess, are read aloud and found interchangeable; the women swap tokens and the men cannot tell them apart ("wooed but the sign of she," Berowne V.2). The men protest "Our letters, madam, showed much more than jest" and cannot prove it; words are the one instrument they have, and the women have already discounted it. The play leaves open whether the men were sincere, because nothing they say can settle it.

6. **The mocker is caught by his own instrument.**
   - *Where spoken:* The Princess, V.2: "None are so surely caught, when they are catched, / As wit turned fool." Rosaline: "That same Berowne I'll torture ere I go ... That he should be my fool, and I his fate." Berowne, IV.3, before his exposure: "Like a demigod here sit I in the sky, / And wretched fools' secrets heedfully o'er-eye."
   - *What the action does with it:* Berowne steps forward "to whip hypocrisy" and is undone within twenty lines by his own letter delivered by a clown. The lords, having been mocked by the ladies, turn and bait the Worthies; Holofernes says "This is not generous, not gentle, not humble," and the Princess, "Alas, poor Maccabaeus, how hath he been baited!" Rosaline's sentence on Berowne names the mechanism: "A jest's prosperity lies in the ear / Of him that hears it, never in the tongue / Of him that makes it." The play's wittiest man is sent to learn that wit depends on an audience's consent.

7. **Grief stops wit, and the play is honest that this is not a flaw in grief.**
   - *Where spoken:* The Princess, V.2: "A heavy heart bears not a nimble tongue." Berowne: "Honest plain words best pierce the ear of grief." Armado, last line: "The words of Mercury are harsh after the songs of Apollo."
   - *What the action does with it:* Four acts of verbal fencing are ended by a messenger's two-word confirmation. Nobody is clever again after it. The Princess apologises for the "liberal opposition of our spirits," and the matter of the play changes from jesting to tasks. The play does not treat this as the collapse of its comedy but as the arrival of the thing the comedy was made to put off.

8. **Love's claims must be tested by time and hardship, not by words.**
   - *Where spoken:* The Princess, V.2: "A time, methinks, too short / To make a world-without-end bargain in," and "If frosts and fasts, hard lodging and thin weeds, / Nip not the gaudy blossoms of your love, / But that it bear this trial, and last love; / Then, at the expiration of the year, / Come challenge me."
   - *What the action does with it:* The penance the Princess imposes on the King (a hermitage, fasting, austerity) is the very regime he chose for himself in I.1 to avoid women. What he could not keep for fame he is now asked to keep for love. Berowne says what a theatre audience would: "That's too long for a play." The play ends without telling you whether anyone passes.

9. **The low plot has the one consequence that cannot be deferred.**
   - *Where spoken:* Costard, V.2: "she is two months on her way ... She's quick; the child brags in her belly already. 'Tis yours." Costard, I.1: "Such is the simplicity of man to hearken after the flesh." Armado, V.2: "I have vowed to Jaquenetta to hold the plough for her sweet love three year."
   - *What the action does with it:* While the lords negotiate a year's trial in verse, Jaquenetta is pregnant and Armado commits to three years of farm work for her. The three-year vow of the opening, made against women, returns at the end as three years of labour for one. The body settles what rhetoric could not.

10. **Things should be taken in their season, and the play ends in two seasons with nothing idealised in either.**
   - *Where spoken:* Berowne, I.1: "At Christmas I no more desire a rose / Than wish a snow in May's new-fangled shows, / But like of each thing that in season grows." The closing songs, V.2: Spring, where the cuckoo "Mocks married men"; Winter, where "greasy Joan doth keel the pot" and "Marian's nose looks red and raw."
   - *What the action does with it:* After all the sun-and-goddess comparisons, the final images are adultery in spring and chilblains in winter. The songs are what married life, if it comes after the year, will actually be. The play has spent its length on ornament and ends on the plain.

11. **Everyone mocks downward.**
   - *Where spoken:* Holofernes to Dull, IV.2: "O, thou monster Ignorance, how deformed dost thou look!" Nathaniel: "he hath not drunk ink." Costard, V.2, defending Nathaniel after the lords have shamed him: "a foolish mild man; an honest man, look you, and soon dashed ... a little o'erparted."
   - *What the action does with it:* The pedant despises the constable; the lords despise the pedant; the ladies despise the lords. Kindness in the pageant scene comes from the clown and the Princess ("Great thanks, great Pompey"), not from anyone clever. The play arranges its cruelty by rank and puts its generosity where the wits are not looking.

## What it teaches

The play is about people who live in language and what it costs them. The King and his lords are brilliant talkers who take an oath because the words of it sound well, break it because other words sound better, and woo with sonnets that the women can't distinguish from one another. The play is affectionate about this; it gives the lords the best speeches. But it also keeps a ledger. Every verbal victory in the play is paid for: the mocker is mocked, the overhearer is overheard, the man who renounces fine phrases cannot get through a sentence without one. If you are the kind of person who wins arguments, this play will recognise you and then show you Rosaline's prescription, a year among people who cannot laugh, where "a jest's prosperity lies in the ear / Of him that hears it." Wit, it says, is a relationship, not a possession.

The hard thing is the ending, and the play knows it is hard; it says so twice ("Our wooing doth not end like an old play"; "That's too long for a play"). A messenger brings a death, and the whole fabric of jest is put down in a line. The women do not reject the men; they refuse to accept words from them and ask for a year instead. You never learn how the year goes. What the play leaves you with is a feeling that promises made in high spirits are worth exactly nothing until something has been endured for them, and a suspicion that the play's own cleverness was a way of not saying that until it had to.

And then there are the songs, which are the plainest thing in the play and come last. After every comparison of a woman to the sun, you get the cuckoo mocking married men and greasy Joan at the pot. It is not a cynical ending. It is the play telling you what was true under the talk all along: that there is a spring and a winter, that the body gets cold and gets pregnant, and that this is where love, if it survives its trial, will have to live.

## Preoccupations

- oaths made against nature and the speed with which they break
- words as a substitute for things, and the cost of living in language
- wit as a weapon that turns on its owner
- praise and flattery as currency the women refuse to accept
- death arriving in the middle of play and changing everything
- time and endurance as the only test of love that counts
- learning that knows names and not things
- the body (hunger, pregnancy, cold) as what remains when talk stops
