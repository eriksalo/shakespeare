# The Merry Wives of Windsor

## What happens

Sir John Falstaff, short of money in Windsor, sends identical love letters to two married women, Mistress Ford and Mistress Page, on the theory that they have looked at him with interest and that each controls her husband's purse. The wives compare the letters and set out to lead him on and punish him. His discarded followers, Pistol and Nym, tell the husbands; Page laughs it off, Ford is consumed by jealousy, disguises himself as "Master Brook," and pays Falstaff to seduce his own wife so he can catch her. Falstaff is smuggled out of Ford's house in a basket of dirty laundry and dumped in the Thames, then disguised as the fat woman of Brentford and beaten by Ford as a witch, while Ford's searches of his house twice find nothing and make him the town's joke. The wives tell their husbands everything, and the whole town stages a midnight pageant at Herne's oak where Falstaff, wearing stag's horns, is pinched and burned by children dressed as fairies and then unmasked and jeered. In the confusion both parents try to marry off the daughter, Anne, to their own chosen suitor by disguise, and both suitors carry off a boy; Anne marries Fenton, the man she chose. Everyone, including Falstaff, goes home to laugh about it by the fire.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Desire that is really about money reads every ordinary kindness as an invitation.**
   - *Where spoken:* Falstaff, I.3: "I am about thrift. Briefly, I do mean to make love to Ford's wife ... She discourses, she carves, she gives the leer of invitation. I can construe the action of her familiar style," and of the two women, "they shall be my East and West Indies, and I will trade to them both." Mistress Page, II.1: "Why, he hath not been thrice in my company!"
   - *What the action does with it:* The wives see the commercial logic instantly because the letters are identical: "I warrant he hath a thousand of these letters, writ with blank space for different names" (II.1). The venture ends with Falstaff owing twenty pounds to the husband he thought he was cuckolding and his horses seized for it (V.5). The play confirms the women's reading of him and refutes his of them at every turn.

2. **A guilty mind will believe anything, including fairies.**
   - *Where spoken:* Falstaff, V.5: "I was three or four times in the thought they were not fairies; and yet the guiltiness of my mind, the sudden surprise of my powers, drove the grossness of the foppery into a received belief, in despite of the teeth of all rhyme and reason, that they were fairies. See now how wit may be made a Jack-a-Lent when 'tis upon ill employment!"
   - *What the action does with it:* The "fairies" are the parson, a tavern crier, Mistress Quickly and the neighbours' children with candles. Falstaff, who has prided himself on his wit all play, lies face down and lets children burn his fingers. His own diagnosis, that guilt disabled his judgement, is the most accurate thing anyone says about him.

3. **Jealousy is a torment the jealous man builds and finances himself.**
   - *Where spoken:* Ford, II.2: "See the hell of having a false woman: my bed shall be abused, my coffers ransacked, my reputation gnawn at ... God be praised for my jealousy!" And: "I will rather trust a Fleming with my butter, Parson Hugh the Welshman with my cheese ... than my wife with herself." III.5: "If I have horns to make one mad, let the proverb go with me: I'll be horn-mad."
   - *What the action does with it:* As "Brook," Ford pays Falstaff to seduce his wife, then pays again to hear himself described as a "poor cuckoldly knave" and a "mechanical salt-butter rogue." He searches his house twice before the whole town and finds laundry. He beats an old woman who is Falstaff. The wives are as pleased to trick him as to trick the knight: "I know not which pleases me better, that my husband is deceived, or Sir John" (III.3). Jealousy is the one vice the play punishes as hard as lust.

4. **A wife can be merry and honest at once, but she has to prove it by staging the proof.**
   - *Where spoken:* Mistress Page, IV.2: "Wives may be merry and yet honest too. / We do not act that often jest and laugh; / 'Tis old but true: 'Still swine eats all the draff.'" V.3: "Against such lewdsters and their lechery, / Those that betray them do no treachery."
   - *What the action does with it:* The wives run the entire plot; the husbands are let in only at Act IV. The claim is confirmed, but note what it costs: the honest wife has to produce a theatrical demonstration before her husband will say "I rather will suspect the sun with cold / Than thee with wantonness" (IV.4). Page's reply to Ford's conversion, "Be not as extreme in submission as in offence," suggests the play distrusts both extremes.

5. **Parents sell daughters, and the daughter, given the same tools, outplays them both.**
   - *Where spoken:* Page, III.2: "The wealth I have waits on my consent, and my consent goes not that way." Mistress Page, IV.4: "He, none but he, shall have her, / Though twenty thousand worthier come to crave her." Anne, III.4: "O, what a world of vile ill-favoured faults / Looks handsome in three hundred pounds a year!" and "Alas, I had rather be set quick i' th' earth, / And bowled to death with turnips."
   - *What the action does with it:* Father and mother each separately plan to have Anne abducted from the fairy pageant by their own candidate, each using a coloured costume as the signal. Both candidates carry off a boy. The wives who out-tricked Falstaff are out-tricked by their daughter with the same device of disguise. Fenton: "Th' offence is holy that she hath committed." Ford: "Money buys lands, and wives are sold by fate." Page: "What cannot be eschewed must be embraced." The parents yield, not because they were wrong but because they lost.

6. **Even the right suitor admits that money came first.**
   - *Where spoken:* Fenton, III.4: "Albeit I will confess thy father's wealth / Was the first motive that I wooed thee, Anne, / Yet, wooing thee, I found thee of more value / Than stamps in gold or sums in sealed bags." Anne, a few lines earlier: "Maybe he tells you true," of her father's suspicion.
   - *What the action does with it:* The play does not pretend that Fenton is above the economy that Falstaff, Slender, Caius and the parents all operate in. He pays the Host "a hundred pound in gold" to arrange the priest. The difference the play allows him is that he can say the money motive out loud and that Anne, hearing it, still chooses him. Honesty about the bargain is what distinguishes the good match from the bad ones.

7. **The man who cannot say what he wants gets what he never looked at.**
   - *Where spoken:* Slender, I.1: "if there be no great love in the beginning, yet heaven may decrease it upon better acquaintance ... I hope upon familiarity will grow more contempt. But if you say 'Marry her,' I will marry her." III.4, to Anne: "Truly, for mine own part I would little or nothing with you. Your father and my uncle hath made motions."
   - *What the action does with it:* He marries "a great lubberly boy," having gone by the colour of the dress and the password rather than the person. His malapropisms ("decrease," "contempt") say what he means better than he does. The play makes the proxy marriage literally blind.

8. **Everyone who sets a trick is caught in one.**
   - *Where spoken:* The Host, III.1, to the duellists he has sent to different fields: "Boys of art, I have deceived you both." Evans, IV.5, when the Host's horses are stolen: "You are wise, and full of gibes and vlouting-stocks, and 'tis not convenient you should be cozened." Falstaff, IV.5: "I would all the world might be cozened, for I have been cozened and beaten too."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Host mocks the parson and the doctor and loses three horses; Falstaff plots on the wives and is washed, beaten and burned; Ford plots on Falstaff and is made "your table-sport"; the parents plot on Anne and get two boys. The play's whole structure is a ledger in which no cleverness goes unrepaid.

9. **"Honour" is the word people use to excuse what they were going to do anyway.**
   - *Where spoken:* Falstaff, II.2, to Pistol who has refused to carry a love letter: "I myself sometimes, leaving the fear of God on the left hand, and hiding mine honour in my necessity, am fain to shuffle, to hedge, and to lurch; and yet you, rogue, will ensconce your rags ... under the shelter of your honour!" Nym, I.3: "I will keep the 'haviour of reputation."
   - *What the action does with it:* Pistol and Nym, having refused to pander on principle, go straight to the husbands to inform out of spite ("humours of revenge," I.3). Their honour turns out to be resentment with a name. Falstaff, who at least admits he hides his honour in his necessity, is more candid than the men who invoke it.

10. **Punishment in this town is public ridicule, and after it the offender is taken home to supper.**
   - *Where spoken:* Mistress Ford, IV.2: "methinks there would be no period to the jest should he not be publicly shamed." Falstaff, V.5: "Well, I am your theme. You have the start of me. I am dejected ... Use me as you will." Page: "Yet be cheerful, knight. Thou shalt eat a posset tonight at my house." Mistress Page: "let us every one go home, / And laugh this sport o'er by a country fire, / Sir John and all."
   - *What the action does with it:* The fairy pageant is a community sentence carried out by children. Falstaff is called "hodge-pudding," "bag of flax," "puffed man," "old, cold, withered," in a round of insults from everyone. Then he is invited to dinner. The play shows a town that disciplines by laughter and then readmits; it does not pretend the laughter was gentle.

11. **The body can be turned into a thing, and a man can keep his wit while it happens.**
   - *Where spoken:* Falstaff, III.5: "Have I lived to be carried in a basket like a barrow of butcher's offal, and to be thrown in the Thames? ... you may know by my size that I have a kind of alacrity in sinking." V.5, in horns: "O powerful love, that in some respects, makes a beast a man, in some other a man a beast! ... When gods have hot backs, what shall poor men do?"
   - *What the action does with it:* He is in turn laundry, an old woman, a stag, an ass, an ox. Each time he describes it better than anyone else could, and each time he comes back. His last generous line, when Anne's marriage spoils the Pages' plans, is "I am glad, though you have ta'en a special stand to strike at me, that your arrow hath glanced." The play humiliates him on the flesh and lets him keep the last good joke.

12. **The town is held together by people who cannot speak each other's English, and the one fluent man is the one it expels.**
   - *Where spoken:* Falstaff, V.5: "Have I lived to stand at the taunt of one that makes fritters of English?" Ford: "I will never mistrust my wife again, till thou art able to woo her in good English." Evans, IV.1, teaching Latin: "hung, hang, hog"; Mistress Quickly: "'Hang-hog' is Latin for bacon, I warrant you."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Welsh parson, the French doctor, the housekeeper who mishears every Latin case as bawdry, the Host with his "bully rook," Slender who says "dissolutely" for "resolutely": these are the people who prevail. Falstaff's language is the best in the play and it does not help him. The play suggests that a community runs on good will more than on good grammar, and that fluency is no protection against being wrong about people.

13. **A go-between who keeps every promise keeps none.**
   - *Where spoken:* Mistress Quickly, III.4: "I will do what I can for them all three, for so I have promised and I'll be as good as my word—but speciously for Master Fenton." I.4: "Never a woman in Windsor knows more of Anne's mind than I do," said after assuring both Caius and Fenton that Anne loves them.
   - *What the action does with it:* She carries Falstaff's messages to the wives who are tricking him, and the suitors' messages to a girl who has already chosen. Her fidelity to all parties is the play's picture of how most messages in a town actually travel: everyone is told what they hope to hear.

## What it teaches

The play is about a town, and what a town does with a man who thinks it is there for him to use. Falstaff arrives with a theory: that women who smile at him want him, that their husbands are fools, that money and pleasure can be got together by a bold man. Every part of the theory is wrong, and the play lets him discover this one humiliation at a time, in the laundry, in the river, under a cudgel, under children's candles. What is instructive is not that he is wrong but why. His own answer, in the last scene, is the right one: "the guiltiness of my mind" made him believe in fairies. The man who wants something badly enough will misread everything, and the play shows that this is true of the jealous husband quite as much as the lecher. Ford pays to hear his fear confirmed; Falstaff pays to hear his hope confirmed; the wives, who want nothing from either, are the only people who see clearly.

The harder thing to carry away is the play's picture of how a community corrects its members. It is not kind. The pageant at Herne's oak is a sentence passed by the whole town and carried out by its children, and the round of insults afterward is a chorus of everyone against one. Then Falstaff is asked to supper, and goes, and the play treats this as a happy ending. A reader has to decide whether this is the healthiest thing in the play, a community that can laugh someone back into line and then feed him, or a glimpse of how cruelty becomes respectable when enough people share it. The text supports both, and Falstaff's dignity under it ("Well, I am your theme") makes the question harder, not easier.

And then the daughter. The wives, who have outwitted a knight and cured a husband, are beaten at the end by Anne, who uses their own device against them and marries the man she wants. The parents yield with proverbs about fate, which is what people say when they have lost. Fenton has already admitted that he came for the money and stayed for her. The play's view of love is not sentimental: it is a market in which the good outcome is the one where someone tells the truth about the price and the other person chooses anyway.

## Preoccupations

- desire that is really greed, and how it misreads other people
- jealousy as a self-inflicted torment the sufferer pays for
- the honest wife having to stage proof of her honesty
- daughters as merchandise and the daughter choosing for herself
- the trickster tricked: every plot repaid in kind
- community discipline by public ridicule, followed by readmission
- the body degraded (basket, beating, horns) and wit surviving it
- "honour" and "reputation" as words that cover what people already wanted to do
