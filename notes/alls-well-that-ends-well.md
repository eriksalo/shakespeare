# All's Well That Ends Well

## What happens

Helena, orphaned daughter of a famous physician and ward of the Countess of Rossillon, loves the Countess's son Bertram, who is far above her in rank and barely notices her. When Bertram goes to the French court, Helena follows with her father's last prescription and cures the King of a fistula his doctors had given up on, having staked her life on it; as her reward she asks to choose a husband, and chooses Bertram. He refuses ("A poor physician's daughter my wife!"), is forced by the King, marries her, and runs away the same night to the Florentine wars with his follower Parolles, a boastful liar everyone but Bertram has seen through. He sends Helena a letter saying she may call him husband only when she has the ring from his finger and a child of his begetting, "but in such a 'then' I write a 'never.'" Helena leaves Rossillon as a pilgrim and lets it be reported that she has died. In Florence Bertram is a war hero and is trying to seduce Diana, a widow's daughter; Helena, lodging with them, pays them to let Diana consent, get the ring, and then let Helena take her place in bed. Parolles, meanwhile, is ambushed by his own side pretending to be the enemy and betrays everything and everyone to save his life; Bertram sees it. Bertram returns home a widower, is forgiven by the King, is about to be married to Lafew's daughter, and is caught with a ring the King gave Helena. Diana arrives to accuse him; he lies, slanders her, and is unravelled by Diana's riddle until Helena enters, pregnant, with his ring and his letter. Bertram says that if she can make him "know this clearly" he will love her "ever, ever dearly." The King says all yet seems well.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Our remedies lie in ourselves, and we credit heaven with them; but what self-help wins may be the form of the thing and not the thing.**
   - Where it is spoken: Helena, I.1: "Our remedies oft in ourselves do lie, / Which we ascribe to heaven: the fated sky / Gives us free scope." To the King, II.1: "Of heaven, not me, make an experiment."
   - What the action does with it: Helena does everything herself: the cure, the choice, the pilgrimage, the purse of gold, the bed-trick. Every obstacle in the play is overcome by her design. And at the end she describes what she has won: "'Tis but the shadow of a wife you see, / The name, and not the thing" (V.3). Bertram answers "Both, both," but his love is still conditional on proof. The play confirms that a resolute person can get almost anything, and leaves open whether a husband so got is a husband.

2. **Honour should come from deeds, not birth; the argument is true and persuades no one.**
   - Where it is spoken: The King, II.3: "From lowest place when virtuous things proceed, / The place is dignified by the doer's deed... The mere word's a slave, / Debauch'd on every tomb, on every grave / A lying trophy."
   - What the action does with it: Bertram's answer is "I cannot love her, nor will strive to do 't." The King then drops the argument and uses force: "My honour's at the stake, which to defeat, / I must produce my power." Bertram submits to the power ("I submit / My fancy to your eyes"), not to the reasoning. The best speech in the play about merit is shown to move exactly nothing; what moves things is a king's threat. Honour by deeds is also what Bertram actually wins in Florence, and it does not make him good.

3. **Love across rank is a hind loving a lion; the hind can win by stratagem, and the play does not say that this is the same as being loved.**
   - Where it is spoken: Helena, I.1: "'Twere all one / That I should love a bright particular star, / And think to wed it, he is so above me"; "The hind that would be mated by the lion / Must die for love." I.3: "I know I love in vain, strive against hope; / Yet in this captious and inteemable sieve / I still pour in the waters of my love."
   - What the action does with it: She does not die; she mates the lion. But the mating is by substitution in the dark, and her own description of it afterward is bleak: "O strange men! / That can such sweet use make of what they hate" (IV.4). The play lets her win and lets her name the cost in the same breath.

4. **A man's soul can be his clothes; the exposed liar is treated more kindly than the lord who believed him.**
   - Where it is spoken: Lafew, II.5: "there can be no kernal in this light nut; the soul of this man is his clothes." Helena, I.1: "these fix'd evils sit so fit in him / That they take place when virtue's steely bones / Looks bleak i' th' cold wind."
   - What the action does with it: Parolles, blindfolded, betrays his general, his comrades, and his master within minutes, and when unmuffled says, "Simply the thing I am / Shall make me live... There's place and means for every man alive" (IV.3). Lafew, who first saw through him, feeds him: "though you are a fool and a knave, you shall eat" (V.2). Bertram, who was fooled by him and then behaves worse than he does in the last scene, lying and slandering under oath, gets no such plain sentence. The play's charity goes to the man who stops pretending.

5. **Our lives are a mingled yarn; virtues and faults keep each other honest.**
   - Where it is spoken: First Lord, IV.3: "The web of our life is of a mingled yarn, good and ill together; our virtues would be proud if our faults whipped them not; and our crimes would despair if they were not cherish'd by our virtues." Also: "As we are ourselves, what things are we!" "Merely our own traitors."
   - What the action does with it: Said about Bertram on the night he is both the Duke's victorious general and a seducer handing away his family ring. The play keeps the two Bertrams in one body and never separates them. The same two lords who engineer Parolles's exposure are the ones who say this, and they know that the "whip" they are about to apply to Parolles is also meant for their lord.

6. **We price what we have at nothing until we have lost it; and the man told this is already lying again.**
   - Where it is spoken: The King, V.3: "love that comes too late, / Like a remorseful pardon slowly carried, / To the great sender turns a sour offence, / Crying, That's good that's gone. Our rash faults / Make trivial price of serious things we have, / Not knowing them until we know their grave." First Lord, IV.3: "How mightily sometimes we make us comforts of our losses!"
   - What the action does with it: Bertram has just claimed that Helen, "since I have lost, have lov'd." Within thirty lines he is denying the ring was hers, inventing a woman who threw it from a casement, and calling Diana "a common gamester to the camp." The King's maxim is true; the repentance it was answering was not. The play puts the wisest speech about remorse into a scene that demonstrates the remorse was performance.

7. **Many oaths do not make truth; all men have the like oaths.**
   - Where it is spoken: Diana, IV.2: "'Tis not the many oaths that makes the truth, / But the plain single vow that is vow'd true"; "My mother told me just how he would woo, / As if she sat in's heart. She says all men / Have the like oaths." Mariana, III.5: "their promises, enticements, oaths, tokens, and all these engines of lust, are not the things they go under."
   - What the action does with it: Confirmed exactly. Bertram swears "My house, mine honour, yea, my life be thine" to get into Diana's bed and in V.3 says of her, "I had that which any inferior might / At market-price have bought." Parolles's testimony, "He lov'd her, sir, and lov'd her not," is called equivocal by the King and is the most accurate thing anyone says about Bertram.

8. **A lawful act can be done with a wicked meaning, and a sinful fact can involve no sin; the person who says so is the one doing it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Helena, III.7: "Is wicked meaning in a lawful deed, / And lawful meaning in a lawful act, / Where both not sin, and yet a sinful fact." Diana, V.3: "he's guilty, and he is not guilty."
   - What the action does with it: The bed-trick is the play's solution and its own author calls it a riddle. Bertram intends adultery and commits marriage; Helena intends marriage and must enact a deception; Diana sells a consent she never gives. The King's last word is not "all's well" but "All yet seems well." The play does not untangle the riddle; it stages it and hands it over.

9. **Youth's rebellion is natural, and the old forgive it in advance, and the old are as volatile as the young.**
   - Where it is spoken: The Countess, I.3: "Even so it was with me when I was young; / If ever we are nature's, these are ours; this thorn / Doth to our rose of youth rightly belong." V.3: "Natural rebellion, done i' the blaze of youth, / When oil and fire, too strong for reason's force, / O'erbears it and burns on." The King: "I have forgiven and forgotten all."
   - What the action does with it: Within a hundred lines of forgiving and forgetting all, the King has Bertram seized on suspicion of murder: "thou didst hate her deadly. / And she is dead." The old people's clemency is sincere and no steadier than the young man's lust. The Countess, who in III.2 said "I do wash his name out of my blood," is in V.3 asking that his crimes be called "natural rebellion." Both are true of her.

10. **Age fills a place and knows it; a cure for the body does not cure time.**
    - Where it is spoken: The King, I.2: "I fill a place, I know't"; "Since I nor wax nor honey can bring home, / I quickly were dissolved from my hive / To give some labourers room." Lafew, I.1: the King "hath abandon'd his physicians... under whose practices he hath persecuted time with hope, and finds no other advantage in the process but only the losing of hope by time."
    - What the action does with it: Helena cures him and he is "of as able body as when he number'd thirty" (IV.5). In V.3 he is still saying, "we are old, and on our quick'st decrees / Th'inaudible and noiseless foot of time / Steals ere we can effect them." Health is restored and the sense of the end is not. The play's one miracle leaves its recipient exactly as mortal as before.

11. **"All's well that ends well" is a hope spoken in the middle, and the play ends before the end.**
    - Where it is spoken: Helena, IV.4: "All's well that ends well; still the fine's the crown. / Whate'er the course, the end is the renown." V.1: "All's well that ends well yet, / Though time seem so adverse and means unfit." The King, V.3: "All yet seems well, and if it end so meet, / The bitter past, more welcome is the sweet." Epilogue: "All is well ended if this suit be won."
    - What the action does with it: The title is a proverb that the characters keep saying as encouragement, and each time it is conditional: "yet," "seems," "if." Bertram's final line is itself an "if." The play stops at the moment where ending well would have to begin. Whether the end justifies the course is exactly the question the course has raised, and the title answers it only as a wish.

12. **Modern knowledge makes trifles of terrors; we would rather explain than submit to an unknown fear.**
    - Where it is spoken: Lafew, II.3: "we have our philosophical persons to make modern and familiar things supernatural and causeless. Hence is it that we make trifles of terrors, ensconcing ourselves into seeming knowledge when we should submit ourselves to an unknown fear."
    - What the action does with it: The cure is presented as "a showing of a heavenly effect in an earthly actor," and Helena, who performed it, says it was heaven's. Then the second half of the play is all earthly contrivance: gold, rings, substitution, a false report of death. The text keeps both accounts in play and lets Parolles parrot Lafew's wonder ("so I say") to show how cheaply wonder can be professed.

## What it teaches

The play tells you that a determined person can get what she wants, and then asks you to look hard at what she got. Helena is the most capable character in any of these comedies: she cures a king, outwits a court, and traps a husband with his own letter. The play admires her and does not pretend her prize is worth it. Bertram's last words are a conditional, and the King's are "seems" and "if." If you want to believe the title, the text makes you supply the belief yourself. That is the first thing to carry away: the difference between winning and being loved, and the possibility that resolve can close the first gap and not the second.

The second thing is about speeches. The King on honour, the King on late remorse, the Lord on the mingled yarn, Diana on oaths: every one of these is wise and true, and every one is followed within the scene by a demonstration that it changed nothing. The King argues merit and then uses force. Bertram hears the finest lines about regret and goes straight on lying. The play is unusually explicit that good sense is not a lever. What does move people here is power, money, and desire, and the one character who grasps this fully is the heroine, which is why she is both admirable and a little frightening.

What the play leaves you with is the mingled yarn. Bertram is a coward and a hero, a liar and a man who fought well; Parolles is a traitor who tells more truth under a blindfold than his master tells before a king; Helena is a saint who buys a bed. The last scene's riddle, "he's guilty, and he is not guilty," is not a trick of plotting; it is the play's account of a person. The old people have been saying all along that youth burns and should be forgiven. The question the play does not answer is whether forgiveness extended to someone who has not yet asked for it is mercy or just another kind of wishing. Helena says, "Will you be mine now you are doubly won?" Won is the word. All yet seems well.

## Preoccupations

- getting what you want by your own devices, and what that leaves you holding
- merit against birth; the argument that is true and does not persuade
- good sense spoken aloud and ignored in the same scene
- oaths as the standard equipment of seduction
- the liar exposed and fed; the lord excused and unreformed
- youth's faults forgiven in advance by the old
- the body cured and age untouched
- endings as a claim made in the middle: "seems," "yet," "if"
