# Pericles, Prince of Tyre

## What happens
Pericles goes to Antioch to win a princess by solving a riddle, solves it, and discovers that the riddle confesses the king's incest with her. He flees, knowing that a tyrant who has been seen through will kill the witness. Fear drives him out of Tyre; he feeds starving Tarsus with corn, is shipwrecked, washes up naked at Pentapolis, wins a tournament in rusty armour, and marries Thaisa. On the voyage home she dies (apparently) in childbirth during a storm and is thrown overboard in a chest; the infant Marina is left at Tarsus with Cleon and Dionyza. Thaisa, revived by the physician Cerimon, takes vows at Diana's temple. Fourteen years on, Dionyza, envious of Marina's eclipse of her own daughter, hires a murderer; pirates interrupt and sell Marina to a brothel in Mytilene, where she talks every customer out of the deed, including the governor Lysimachus. Pericles, told Marina is dead, stops speaking for three months. His ship anchors at Mytilene; Marina is brought to cure him and, telling her story, turns out to be his daughter. Diana appears in a dream and sends him to Ephesus, where Thaisa is found alive. Gower, an old poet raised from the dead, narrates between the acts and closes with a moral.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Fair appearance is no evidence of inner good, and is more often its disguise.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Pericles, having read the riddle, on Antiochus' smooth response: "How courtesy would seem to cover sin, / When what is done is like an hypocrite, / The which is good in nothing but in sight!" (1.1); and to Helicanus, "'Tis time to fear when tyrants seems to kiss" (1.2). Cleon: "Who makes the fairest show means most deceit" (1.4). Gower on Marina's false tomb: "No visor does become black villany / So well as soft and tender flattery" (4.4).
   - *What the action does with it:* The play confirms it repeatedly — Antiochus' daughter "apparell'd like the spring" is "a glorious casket stored with ill"; Dionyza's "I love the king your father, and yourself, / With more than foreign heart" (4.1) is said as she hands Marina to a murderer. But the play immediately undercuts it as a rule for living: Cleon applies the maxim to Pericles' white-flagged ships and is wrong — they carry corn, not soldiers. And Simonides reverses it in the other direction: "Opinion's but a fool, that makes us scan / The outward habit by the inward man" (2.2), proven when the knight in rusty armour wins. The work's position is that surfaces are unreliable in both directions, and that the only test is what a person does over time.

2. **A man is the plaything of forces that owe him nothing; what he has is given and taken "not what they crave."**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Pericles to the fishermen: "A man whom both the waters and the wind, / In that vast tennis-court, have made the ball / For them to play upon" (2.1). At Simonides' feast: "time's the king of men, / He's both their parent, and he is their grave, / And gives them what he will, not what they crave" (2.3). After Thaisa's death: "Could I rage and roar / As doth the sea she lies in, yet the end / Must be as 'tis" (3.3).
   - *What the action does with it:* The action confirms the passivity. Pericles never defeats an enemy, outwits anyone, or earns his recoveries: Antiochus is burned by "a fire from heaven" (2.4) in his absence; Cleon and Dionyza are burned by their own citizens offstage; the armour comes back in a fishing net; Marina is saved by pirates who happen by. The same fortune that stripped him clothes him. But the play qualifies pure fatalism: Cerimon's skill, Helicanus' loyalty, and Marina's speech are human acts that do the actual work of restoration. The gods bring the ship to the right harbour; people do the rest.

3. **Fear, once conceived, feeds itself and outgrows its cause.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "the passions of the mind, / That have their first conception by misdread, / Have after-nourishment and life by care; / And what was first but fear what might be done, / Grows elder now and cares it be not done" (1.2). Also "tyrants' fears / Decrease not, but grow faster than the years" (1.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* Pericles' fear is justified — Thaliard has already been sent with poison (1.1) — so the play does not dismiss fear as neurosis. But the fear becomes the engine of his life: it empties Tyre of its prince for years, and every later disaster follows from the flight. Antiochus' fear is the mirror case: a tyrant who must kill the man who knows. The play treats fear as a thing with its own appetite, in good men and bad alike.

4. **A ruler needs someone who will tell him he is wrong, and flattery is the opposite of love.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Helicanus: "They do abuse the king that flatter him: / For flattery is the bellows blows up sin ... Whereas reproof, obedient and in order, / Fits kings, as they are men, for they may err" (1.2). Pericles: "heaven forbid / That kings should let their ears hear their faults hid!" (1.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* The play confirms it in full. Helicanus, the man who kneels and says "I have ground the axe myself; / Do but you strike the blow," is the one who keeps the kingdom, refuses the crown when the lords offer it ("For honour's cause, forbear your suffrages", 2.4), and is standing beside Pericles at the end. Antiochus by contrast has Thaliard, who says "if a king bid a man be a villain, he's bound by the indenture of his oath to be one" (1.3). The two courts are a controlled experiment in counsel.

5. **The gods give what is lovable and then take it, and a human being has no remedy but endurance.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Pericles at Marina's birth and Thaisa's death: "O you gods! / Why do you make us love your goodly gifts, / And snatch them straight away? We here below / Recall not what we give, and therein may / Vie honour with you" (3.1). Lychorida's only answer: "Patience, good sir; do not assist the storm."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play answers the complaint, but on a fourteen-year delay and with human mediation. Thaisa is not dead — Cerimon's "Death may usurp on nature many hours, / And yet the fire of life kindle again" (3.2) — and Marina survives. Yet the play does not let the complaint be withdrawn: the sailors' superstition that a corpse must go overboard (3.1) is the immediate cause of the loss, and Pericles' sackcloth and uncut hair are fourteen years of a life. What is given back is given back late, and not because the complaint was wrong.

6. **Loss strips a man to what he is, and what he is can be seen most clearly by people who have nothing.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Pericles, wet, to the fishermen: "What I have been I have forgot to know; / But what I am, want teaches me to think on" (2.1). The First Fisherman's moral: "the great ones eat up the little ones" (2.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* The fishermen's cynicism about the great is contradicted by their own conduct: they give the stranger a gown, food, and the armour, and ask only to be remembered. Their world (begging, whipping, misers like whales) is harsh, and their kindness is unforced. Pericles calls it "honest mirth." The play places its clearest instances of decency among people who have least.

7. **Knowledge and charity are the only possessions that cannot be squandered or lost.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cerimon: "Virtue and cunning were endowments greater / Than nobleness and riches: careless heirs / May the two latter darken and expend; / But immortality attends the former, / Making a man a god" (3.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* Cerimon does the one deed in the play that reverses death, and he does it by study ("by turning o'er authorities") and open hands ("your purse, still open"). Pericles at the end calls him "a mortal officer / More like a god than you" (5.3), echoing Cerimon's own claim word for word. The play endorses the idea without irony.

8. **Envy is what parental love becomes when another's child outshines your own.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Gower: "that monster envy, oft the wrack / Of earned praise" (4 Chorus). Dionyza: "She did distain my child, and stood between / Her and her fortunes: none would look on her, / But cast their gazes on Marina's face; / Whilst ours was blurted at and held a malkin" (4.3). She calls the murder "an enterprise of kindness / Perform'd to your sole daughter."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play refuses to make Dionyza merely a villain: it gives her a motive that is a distortion of a good. Cleon's response — horror, then compliance ("I know you'll do as I advise") — shows the second mechanism: weak people who disapprove and do nothing become accomplices. Both burn for it. Pericles' earlier line applies: "One sin, I know, another doth provoke" (1.1).

9. **A good person's speech can change a corrupted mind, but it cannot change an economy.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lysimachus to Marina: "Had I brought hither a corrupted mind, / Thy speech had alter'd it" (4.6). The gentlemen leaving the brothel: "I am out of the road of rutting for ever" (4.5). Marina to Boult: "Do anything but this thou doest."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play confirms the first clause — one customer after another leaves converted — and tests the second. Boult's answer is not refuted: "Go to the wars, would you? where a man may serve seven years for the loss of a leg, and have not money enough in the end to buy him a wooden one?" (4.6). Marina gets out not by argument alone but by gold and marketable skills ("I can sing, weave, sew, and dance") and by making her virtue pay the bawd. The play also quietly questions Lysimachus' "I came with no ill intent," which the audience heard contradicted ("How a dozen of virginities?") a hundred lines earlier.

10. **Grief can close a person entirely, and what reopens him is being addressed by someone whose suffering matches his own.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Helicanus: "for this three months hath not spoken / To anyone" (5.1). Marina: "she speaks, / My lord, that, may be, hath endured a grief / Might equal yours, if both were justly weigh'd" (5.1). Pericles: "thou dost look / Like Patience gazing on kings' graves, and smiling / Extremity out of act" (5.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* Music does not reach him ("No, nor look'd on us"); the claim of equal grief does. The recognition scene is built so that Pericles has to be told the story in pieces and keeps breaking it off, because belief is as dangerous as despair: "O Helicanus, strike me, honour'd sir; / Give me a gash, put me to present pain; / Lest this great sea of joys rushing upon me / O'erbear the shores of my mortality" (5.1). The play observes that joy, after enough grief, is physically unbearable.

11. **A child can restore the parent: "Thou that beget'st him that did thee beget."**
   - *Where it is spoken:* 5.1, Pericles to Marina.
   - *What the action does with it:* Literally enacted. Pericles is passive through the play; Marina is the only member of the family who argues her way out of death twice (with Leonine, with the brothel). The generation that was lost is the one that does the saving. The play's title character is restored by its most active character, and she is his daughter.

12. **Intent is judged as deed.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Gower's epilogue: "The gods for murder seemed so content / To punish, although not done, but meant."
   - *What the action does with it:* Cleon and Dionyza are burned for a murder that did not happen. The play's justice is absolute about intention, which is the same standard Pericles applied to Antiochus in Act 1 (sin "known" is enough to flee from). It is a harsh and clean rule, and the play states it without qualification.

## What it teaches

The play's hardest lesson is about what you cannot do. Pericles solves one riddle in the first scene and is never again the agent of his own fate. He flees, is wrecked, is clothed by fishermen, is married by a king who pretends to oppose it, loses his wife to sailors' superstition, and is given everything back by a sequence of accidents and other people's competence. If you want a story in which virtue earns its reward through action, this is not it. What the play shows instead is that a life can be lived almost entirely in reaction and still be a good life, provided the reactions are the right ones: he does not touch Antiochus' daughter when he understands what she is, he feeds Tarsus when he has the corn, he listens to Helicanus when he would rather be flattered. The play's word for this is patience, and it is careful to show patience as something nearly unbearable — fourteen years of sackcloth and three months of silence — and not as serenity.

What is unresolved is the relation between fortune and merit. Gower's moral says virtue was "preserved from fell destruction's blast, / Led on by heaven," but the text has shown Thaisa preserved by a physician's craft and Marina preserved by pirates. The play keeps two accounts of causation running side by side and does not reconcile them. A reader can take this as sloppiness or as honesty: in a long life you cannot tell which of your rescues were providence and which were luck, and the play declines to tell you.

The brothel scenes carry a second difficulty. Marina's goodness really does convert people, which is a strong claim, but it does not convert the institution; Boult's question about what else a poor man is to do goes unanswered, and Marina leaves by paying. The play lets her moral victory and the bawd's economic argument stand together. The lesson, if there is one, is that goodness works on individuals one at a time and should not expect to work on anything larger.

## Preoccupations

- fortune and the sea as powers that give and take without regard to desert
- the gap between fair appearance and inner truth, in both directions
- patience and endurance as the only available virtue when action is impossible
- the loss and recovery of family — wife and daughter as what makes a life
- flattery versus honest counsel
- envy as the corruption of love
- virtue tested in a market that puts a price on everything
- grief as silence; joy as a danger to the body
