# The Tragedy of Antony and Cleopatra

## What happens
Antony, one of the three men who share the Roman world, has settled into Alexandria with Cleopatra and lets the messengers from Rome wait. Fulvia's death and Pompey's rising at sea pull him back; he patches a peace with Octavius Caesar by marrying Caesar's sister Octavia, and then goes back to Egypt anyway, as Enobarbus has predicted. Caesar disposes of Pompey and Lepidus, and the two remaining "chaps" of the world grind against each other. Against every soldier's advice Antony fights at sea at Actium because Caesar dares him to; Cleopatra's sixty ships turn, and Antony follows her "like a doting mallard." From there the play is a long going-off: kings and officers desert, Enobarbus leaves and dies of shame when Antony sends his treasure after him, Antony wins one day's skirmish and loses the fleet the next, and accuses Cleopatra of selling him. She sends word she is dead; he botches his suicide and is hauled up dying into her monument. Caesar means to lead her in triumph; she plays obedience, learns the truth from Dolabella, dresses as for Cydnus, and dies by the asp with her women. Caesar, "beguiled," orders them buried together.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Lovers claim a love beyond all measure, and the play leaves the claim both exposed as talk and standing as the last truth.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Antony, 1.1: "There's beggary in the love that can be reckoned"; "Then must thou needs find out new heaven, new earth."
   - *What the action does with it:* Cleopatra answers at once, "Excellent falsehood! / Why did he marry Fulvia, and not love her?" and the next scenes show Antony planning to leave, marrying Octavia "for my peace," and saying flatly "I' th' East my pleasure lies" (2.3). The love is shown as evasion, jealousy, blows to a messenger, and the mutual accusations of 3.13 and 4.12 ("Triple-turned whore!"). Yet it is also the thing both die for, and Caesar, who has every reason to belittle it, closes the play with "No grave upon the earth shall clip in it / A pair so famous" (5.2). The play does not decide whether the immeasurable claim was true; it shows that two people made it true by dying on it.

2. **What we throw away becomes precious the moment it is gone.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Antony on Fulvia, 1.2: "What our contempts doth often hurl from us, / We wish it ours again... She's good, being gone." Caesar, 1.4: "the ebbed man, ne'er loved till ne'er worth love, / Comes deared by being lacked."
   - *What the action does with it:* The maxim is spoken about a wife and a rival and then runs the plot. Antony values Enobarbus most after he has deserted (4.5). He kills himself on a false report of Cleopatra's death, so that she becomes "good, being gone" in the most literal way. Caesar, who has pursued Antony across the world, hears of his death and says, "The breaking of so great a thing should make / A greater crack," and Agrippa gives the law of it: "strange it is / That nature must compel us to lament / Our most persisted deeds" (5.1). The action confirms the observation in every direction, including against the man who made it.

3. **A man's judgment follows his fortune; when fortune declines, so does the wit that might have saved him.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Enobarbus, 3.13: "I see men's judgments are / A parcel of their fortunes, and things outward / Do draw the inward quality after them / To suffer all alike." And: "When valour preys on reason, / It eats the sword it fights with."
   - *What the action does with it:* Antony fights at sea "For that he dares us to 't" (3.7), against Enobarbus, Canidius, and a scarred foot soldier. Having lost, he challenges Caesar to single combat, which Enobarbus hears as proof that "Caesar, thou hast subdued / His judgment too." Antony even names the mechanism himself: "the wise gods seal our eyes, / In our own filth drop our clear judgments, make us / Adore our errors, laugh at's while we strut / To our confusion" (3.13). Caesar's side states the counter-rule coolly: "Never anger / Made good guard for itself" (4.1). Every decision after Actium bears this out.

4. **Reason tells a follower to leave a falling master; the heart cannot survive having done it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Enobarbus, 3.13: "The loyalty well held to fools does make / Our faith mere folly. Yet he that can endure / To follow with allegiance a fallen lord / Does conquer him that did his master conquer, / And earns a place i' th' story."
   - *What the action does with it:* Enobarbus chooses reason, goes to Caesar, and is destroyed when Antony sends his treasure after him: "I am alone the villain of the earth" (4.6). He dies in a ditch asking the moon to witness his repentance (4.9). Set beside him are Eros, who kills himself rather than kill Antony ("Thus do I escape the sorrow / Of Antony's death," 4.14), Dercetus, who carries Antony's sword to Caesar for preferment (5.1), and Charmian and Iras, who die with their mistress. The play does not say the loyal are rewarded; Eros and Charmian get nothing but the "place i' th' story" Enobarbus named and then forfeited.

5. **The self is not a fixed shape; it can dissolve like a cloud, and a name can be lost and re-earned.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Antony, 4.14: "That which is now a horse, even with a thought / The rack dislimns and makes it indistinct / As water is in water... Here I am Antony, / Yet cannot hold this visible shape." Philo, 1.1: "sometimes when he is not Antony, / He comes too short of that great property / Which still should go with Antony."
   - *What the action does with it:* Antony has to insist "I am / Antony yet" (3.13) while having a messenger whipped; Cleopatra says, "since my lord / Is Antony again, I will be Cleopatra" (3.13). Identity is shown as something that must be performed and that fails when fortune withdraws its audience. The play then lets it be rebuilt: Antony claims at death to be "a Roman by a Roman / Valiantly vanquished" (4.15), and Cleopatra remakes him as a colossus whose "legs bestrid the ocean" (5.2). Dolabella tells her no such man existed; she answers that imagining one "were nature's piece 'gainst fancy." The dissolved shape is restored by someone else's wanting it.

6. **A woman who performs her feelings may be no less sincere for performing them; the play refuses to separate the two.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Enobarbus, 1.2: "I have seen her die twenty times upon far poorer moment... she hath such a celerity in dying"; and then: "This cannot be cunning in her; if it be, she makes a shower of rain as well as Jove."
   - *What the action does with it:* Cleopatra instructs her women, "If you find him sad, / Say I am dancing; if in mirth, report / That I am sudden sick" (1.3). Her faked death in 4.13 ("word it, prithee, piteously") kills Antony. Her real death is staged to the last detail: "Show me, my women, like a queen... I am again for Cydnus" (5.2). The play puts the lie and the truth in the same gesture. Even Caesar reads her corpse as a performance that still works: "she looks like sleep, / As she would catch another Antony / In her strong toil of grace."

7. **To be lucky is not to be great; the winner of the world may be only Fortune's servant.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cleopatra, 5.2: "'Tis paltry to be Caesar; / Not being Fortune, he's but Fortune's knave, / A minister of her will." Soothsayer, 2.3: "If thou dost play with him at any game, / Thou art sure to lose."
   - *What the action does with it:* The soothsayer is right: Caesar wins every throw, and Antony confirms "The very dice obey him." Caesar is shown as competent, sober ("I had rather fast from all, four days, / Than drink so much in one," 2.7), and cold. The play gives him the world and denies him the show he wanted: "Caesar's beguiled" (5.2). His last speeches are forced to praise what he meant to parade. The claim that luck is paltry is not proven, but the play arranges for the lucky man to have the least memorable lines.

8. **Power is a crowd of followers, and when it goes it goes like sweets melting; what is left is the bounty that made it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Antony, 4.12: "The hearts / That spanieled me at heels, to whom I gave / Their wishes, do discandy, melt their sweets / On blossoming Caesar." Charmian, 4.13: "The soul and body rive not more in parting / Than greatness going off."
   - *What the action does with it:* Six kings yield, Canidius goes, Alexas turns and is hanged by Caesar anyway, Enobarbus goes. Antony's authority "melts" (3.13). But the giving does not stop with the power: he sends Enobarbus's treasure "with his bounty overplus" (4.6), and Cleopatra's dream of him is a dream of bounty: "an autumn 'twas / That grew the more by reaping" (5.2). The play shows that what followers loved was the giving, and that the giving outlasted the ability to command.

9. **Death chosen is the one deed that stops change, and even that deed is hard to perform cleanly.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cleopatra, 5.2: "it is great / To do that thing that ends all other deeds, / Which shackles accidents and bolts up change." Antony, 4.14: "I will be / A bridegroom in my death and run into't / As to a lover's bed."
   - *What the action does with it:* Antony falls on his sword and does not die: "I have done my work ill, friends." His guards refuse to finish it. Cleopatra draws a dagger and is disarmed by Proculeius; she has to get her death from a clown with a basket of figs. The grand Roman gesture is shown as clumsy, delayed, dependent on servants. And yet it works: Caesar's "Bravest at the last" and Dolabella's "That you did fear is done" concede that the only move Caesar could not counter was the one Cleopatra said "bolts up change."

10. **Honourable scruple loses the world to men who have none.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Pompey, 2.7, refusing Menas's offer to cut the cable and kill the triumvirs: "In me 'tis villainy; / In thee 't had been good service... Being done unknown, / I should have found it afterwards well done, / But must condemn it now." Menas: "Who seeks, and will not take when once 'tis offered, / Shall never find it more."
   - *What the action does with it:* Pompey is beaten and killed within an act; Caesar uses Lepidus against Pompey and then "denied him rivality" and imprisons him (3.5). Pompey's scruple is also a hypocrisy (he would have approved the deed done unasked), and the play lets that stand uncorrected. Ventidius states the rule for subordinates: "Who does i' th' wars more than his captain can / Becomes his captain's captain" (3.1), and limits his own victory to stay safe. In this world, honour is an expense and discretion is survival.

11. **The common people love a man only once he is past deserving it, and the great fear the people's eye more than death.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Antony, 1.2: "Our slippery people, / Whose love is never linked to the deserver / Till his deserts are past." Caesar, 1.4: "This common body, / Like to a vagabond flag upon the stream, / Goes to and back, lackeying the varying tide."
   - *What the action does with it:* Both rulers say it; the play then shows Antony himself as the vagabond flag, and Cleopatra choosing death to avoid "mechanic slaves / With greasy aprons" and a "squeaking Cleopatra" boying her greatness "I' th' posture of a whore" (5.2). The spectacle of the triumph is more feared than the asp. What the great call the people's fickleness is the same thing they most want to control.

12. **Rome calls Egypt a place of surfeit, and the play shows Rome drunk on a galley with the world's fate hanging on a cable.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Caesar, 1.4: "to confound such time / That drums him from his sport... 'tis to be chid / As we rate boys." Pompey, 2.1: "Tie up the libertine in a field of feasts; / Keep his brain fuming."
   - *What the action does with it:* The feast on Pompey's galley (2.7) has Lepidus carried off ("'A bears the third part of the world, man") and the three rulers dancing hand in hand while Menas proposes to cut their throats. Caesar leaves early and sober. The contrast Rome draws between itself and Egypt is partly true (Caesar does win by discipline) and partly a story Rome tells; the play's one scene of Roman revelry is as reckless as anything in Alexandria, and the only man who stays sober is the one who will own everything.

## What it teaches

This play makes it very hard to say what Antony's love was worth, and that difficulty is what it teaches. For four acts the lovers are shown as liars, bullies, and fools: Cleopatra beats a messenger for telling the truth, Antony marries a woman to get a treaty and leaves her, both accuse the other of selling them, and the greatest soldier in the world flees a battle because a woman's ship turned. Every sober voice in the play, from Philo's first line to Enobarbus's last analysis, says the same thing: this is dotage, and judgment is a parcel of fortune. Then in the fifth act Cleopatra builds an Antony out of words whose "legs bestrid the ocean," Dolabella says no such man ever lived, and she replies that imagining him is "nature's piece 'gainst fancy." You are left to choose between the play's evidence and the play's poetry, and the play has arranged things so that Caesar, the clearest-eyed man in it, chooses the poetry too. The lesson is not that love conquers; it is that what people make of their lives in the telling may outlast and outweigh what they did.

The second lesson is colder and comes from the reasonable people. Enobarbus is right about everything, and it kills him. He sees that the marriage will be "the very strangler of their amity," that the sea fight is folly, that Antony's challenge is a sign his judgment is gone, and that loyalty to a fool is folly. He acts on his reason, and then cannot bear the self that reason has made. Set against him, Eros simply dies rather than strike his master and gets a line of praise. The play does not say that loyalty pays; it says that the cost of being right about a falling man may be higher than the cost of falling with him, and that this cost is paid in the heart, not in fortune.

What remains unresolved is Caesar. He is temperate, methodical, and victorious; he wins at dice, cards, cocks, and war; he gets "the time of universal peace." The play gives him no vice except coldness and no speech anyone would keep. Cleopatra calls him "Fortune's knave," and the ending denies him his triumph, but he still has the world. If you want to know whether it is better to be Antony or Octavius, the play will not tell you; it only shows that one of them is lamented by the other, and not the other way around. Cleopatra's "He words me, girls" is the whole play's suspicion of the man who wins by words that cost him nothing, set against two people who, for all their lies, finally paid for theirs.

- "There's beggary in the love that can be reckoned." (Antony, 1.1)
- "Who tells me true, though in his tale lie death, / I hear him as he flattered." (Antony, 1.2)
- "What our contempts doth often hurl from us, / We wish it ours again... She's good, being gone." (Antony, 1.2)
- "In time we hate that which we often fear." (Charmian, 1.3)
- "Eternity was in our lips and eyes." (Cleopatra, 1.3)
- "the ebbed man, ne'er loved till ne'er worth love, / Comes deared by being lacked." (Caesar, 1.4)
- "We, ignorant of ourselves, / Beg often our own harms, which the wise powers / Deny us for our good." (Menecrates, 2.1)
- "Age cannot wither her, nor custom stale / Her infinite variety." (Enobarbus, 2.2)
- "He married but his occasion here." (Enobarbus, 2.6)
- "Though it be honest, it is never good / To bring bad news." (Cleopatra, 2.5)
- "Who does i' th' wars more than his captain can / Becomes his captain's captain." (Ventidius, 3.1)
- "Celerity is never more admired / Than by the negligent." (Cleopatra, 3.7)
- "I see men's judgments are / A parcel of their fortunes." (Enobarbus, 3.13)
- "the wise gods seal our eyes, / In our own filth drop our clear judgments, make us / Adore our errors, laugh at's while we strut / To our confusion." (Antony, 3.13)
- "When valour preys on reason, / It eats the sword it fights with." (Enobarbus, 3.13)
- "O, my fortunes have / Corrupted honest men!" (Antony, 4.5)
- "The soul and body rive not more in parting / Than greatness going off." (Charmian, 4.13)
- "Here I am Antony, / Yet cannot hold this visible shape." (Antony, 4.14)

- "He words me, girls, he words me, that I should not / Be noble to myself." (Cleopatra, 5.2)

## Preoccupations

- the gap between what people say about love and how they act on it
- valuing what is lost only after it is gone
- judgment decaying with fortune; passion eating the reason that could save it
- loyalty to a falling master, and what deserting him costs the deserter
- the self as a performance that depends on an audience and can dissolve
- fame and the story told afterward outweighing the deed
- death by one's own hand as the only act that cannot be taken away
- the sober winner versus the reckless loser; luck versus greatness
