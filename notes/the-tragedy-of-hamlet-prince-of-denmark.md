# The Tragedy of Hamlet, Prince of Denmark

## What happens

A dead king's ghost tells his son that the new king, the son's uncle, murdered him and married his widow, and commands revenge. The son, Hamlet, believes and does not believe; he puts on a feigned madness, stages a play to test the uncle's guilt, gets his proof, and still does not strike when he finds the king at prayer. Instead he kills the wrong man (Polonius) through a curtain, is shipped to England under a death warrant, rewrites the warrant so that his two escorts die in his place, and returns to find the girl he loved drowned and her brother sworn to kill him. The king arranges a rigged fencing match with a poisoned sword and a poisoned cup; by the end of it the queen, the brother, the king, and Hamlet are all dead, most of them by weapons meant for someone else, and a foreign prince walks in to take the throne. The shape is a long, crowded delay followed by a rush of accidents; nearly every deliberate plan in the play lands on the head of the one who made it.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Grief is private and cannot be shown; the outward forms of mourning are things "a man might play."**
   - Where it is spoken: Hamlet to his mother, I.2: "Seems, madam! Nay, it is; I know not seems... But I have that within which passeth show; / These but the trappings and the suits of woe."
   - What the action does with it: The play takes Hamlet at his word and then makes him spend four acts performing. He puts on an "antic disposition" (I.5), coaches actors on how to counterfeit passion (III.2), and is shamed by a player who weeps for Hecuba, a fiction, while he with real cause "can say nothing" (II.2). The inner thing that "passeth show" never becomes action until the last scene, and then only by accident. Meanwhile Laertes, whose grief is loud and theatrical ("Hold off the earth a while, / Till I have caught her once more in mine arms," V.1), is the one who actually does something. The play confirms that inward grief is real, and shows that it is also useless.

2. **Thinking about an act too closely dissolves the will to do it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Hamlet, III.1: "Thus conscience does make cowards of us all, / And thus the native hue of resolution / Is sicklied o'er with the pale cast of thought." And IV.4: "some craven scruple / Of thinking too precisely on th'event,— / A thought which, quarter'd, hath but one part wisdom / And ever three parts coward."
   - What the action does with it: Confirmed in Hamlet's own case: he has the king kneeling and unguarded in III.3 and reasons himself out of it ("That would be scann'd"). But the play complicates the diagnosis by showing what thoughtless action looks like: Hamlet's one unreflecting blow kills Polonius ("Is it the King?"), which sets Laertes and Ophelia's ruin in motion. Laertes, who thinks not at all ("To cut his throat i' th' church," IV.7), is used as a tool and dies "as a woodcock to my own springe" (V.2). The play does not endorse either thought or thoughtlessness; it shows that both kill.

3. **A resolution made in the heat of feeling will not survive the cooling of the feeling.**
   - Where it is spoken: The Player King, III.2: "What to ourselves in passion we propose, / The passion ending, doth the purpose lose... Our wills and fates do so contrary run / That our devices still are overthrown. / Our thoughts are ours, their ends none of our own." Claudius repeats it to Laertes, IV.7: "That we would do, / We should do when we would; for this 'would' changes."
   - What the action does with it: The Player King's speech, which Hamlet himself wrote or chose, describes Hamlet exactly: the man who swore "with wings as swift / As meditation" to sweep to revenge (I.5) has to be visited a second time by the Ghost "to whet thy almost blunted purpose" (III.4). The play is a demonstration of the speech. Claudius, who understands the principle, uses it to rush Laertes into murder before his grief cools. So the observation is true, and the people who understand it best are the ones who exploit it.

4. **The people we cannot trust are the ones who most profess devotion; the surface of a smile tells you nothing.**
   - Where it is spoken: Hamlet, I.5: "That one may smile, and smile, and be a villain! / At least I am sure it may be so in Denmark." Polonius, I.3: "do not believe his vows; for they are brokers... Breathing like sanctified and pious bawds, / The better to beguile."
   - What the action does with it: Nearly every relationship is spied on or feigned: Polonius sends Reynaldo to slander his own son to learn the truth about him (II.1), the king hides behind curtains, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern come as friends and are sent as instruments, Ophelia is "loosed" as bait (II.2). Hamlet's counter-move is to be unreadable himself. But the play also gives one man, Horatio, who is exactly what he seems, and Hamlet's praise of him ("Give me that man / That is not passion's slave," III.2) is the only speech of unguarded love he makes. The play affirms the suspicion and then keeps one exception alive so that the suspicion does not become a law.

5. **Guilt knows itself even when it cannot change; a man can see his sin clearly and still choose to keep what it bought him.**
   - Where it is spoken: Claudius, III.3: "May one be pardon'd and retain th'offence?... Try what repentance can. What can it not? / Yet what can it, when one cannot repent?" and after: "My words fly up, my thoughts remain below."
   - What the action does with it: Claudius is given the most honest self-examination in the play and it changes nothing; within a scene he is writing to England for Hamlet's death. The play's judgment is exact: a confession is not a repentance. What complicates it is that Hamlet, spying on the prayer, misreads the posture as success ("Now might I do it pat, now he is praying") and so spares him. The man who cannot pray is saved by looking as if he could. Appearance beats substance even in the one scene where substance is laid bare.

6. **The dead are forgotten fast, and the speed of forgetting is an insult to the dead.**
   - Where it is spoken: Hamlet, I.2: "But two months dead... A little month"; III.2: "die two months ago, and not forgotten yet? Then there's hope a great man's memory may outlive his life half a year." The Ghost's whole demand is "Remember me" (I.5).
   - What the action does with it: Gertrude's quick remarriage is the wound the play opens with, and the Player Queen's vow never to remarry is a mirror held up to her. But the play ends by extending the forgetting to everyone: Yorick is a skull knocked about with a spade; Alexander stops a beer-barrel; Caesar patches a wall (V.1). Hamlet's dying wish is that his story be told so that his "wounded name" does not live behind him, and Fortinbras, who never met him, gives him a soldier's funeral based on what he "was likely" to have been. Memory is shown to be both a sacred duty and a thing that survives only as guesswork.

7. **Everyone ends as the same dirt; rank is a difference among the living only.**
   - Where it is spoken: Hamlet, IV.3: "Your fat king and your lean beggar is but variable service,—two dishes, but to one table." V.1: "To what base uses we may return, Horatio!"
   - What the action does with it: The gravedigger scene confirms it with physical evidence (skulls of politician, courtier, lawyer, jester all handled alike) and the First Clown adds the one social correction: Ophelia gets Christian burial only because "great folk should have countenance in this world to drown or hang themselves more than their even Christian." So the play agrees that death levels, and notes drily that even the funeral is rigged by rank. The final tableau, four royal corpses on the floor, is the thesis acted out.

8. **The young are warned by elders who do not take their own advice.**
   - Where it is spoken: Ophelia to Laertes, I.3: "Do not as some ungracious pastors do, / Show me the steep and thorny way to heaven; / Whilst like a puff'd and reckless libertine / Himself the primrose path of dalliance treads."
   - What the action does with it: Polonius, who tells his son "to thine own self be true" (I.3), spends the rest of his life as a spy and dies behind a curtain. Laertes warns his sister about Hamlet's inconstancy and then is himself inconstant, swearing revenge and then "almost 'gainst my conscience" (V.2) fencing a man who has apologized to him. Ophelia obeys both of them and goes mad. The play's one piece of advice that is actually followed, Polonius's "Give thy thoughts no tongue," is the thing that destroys everyone, since nobody says what they mean.

9. **A plan made in secret tends to kill its maker.**
   - Where it is spoken: Hamlet, III.4: "'tis the sport to have the enginer / Hoist with his own petard." Horatio's summary, V.2: "purposes mistook / Fall'n on the inventors' heads."
   - What the action does with it: This is the plot's engine, and the play states it as its own moral in the last speech. Polonius hides and is stabbed; Rosencrantz and Guildenstern carry a death warrant that is turned on them; Laertes's unbated sword kills Laertes; Claudius's cup kills Gertrude and then Claudius. The complication is that Hamlet's own plans fare no better: the play catches the king's conscience and then Hamlet does nothing with the catch; the sparing of Claudius at prayer gets Polonius killed; the stab through the arras gets Ophelia killed. Hamlet is not exempt from the law he names.

10. **After enough failure of planning, a person can give up planning and call it providence.**
    - Where it is spoken: Hamlet, V.2: "There's a divinity that shapes our ends, / Rough-hew them how we will," and "There's a special providence in the fall of a sparrow... The readiness is all."
    - What the action does with it: The play lets the attitude stand as the nearest thing to peace Hamlet achieves, and in the same scene he walks into a match he half-suspects is a trap ("thou wouldst not think how ill all's here about my heart"). Providence does bring the king to justice, but only after it also brings the queen and Laertes and Hamlet himself to death, and the throne to a foreigner. Whether this is a divinity shaping ends or just the petard going off is left exactly open. The readiness is real; what it is readiness for is not consoling.

11. **Madness and sanity can be told apart by whether the speaker can repeat himself in order; but the sane, under enough pressure, come to resemble the mad.**
    - Where it is spoken: Hamlet, III.4: "Bring me to the test, / And I the matter will re-word; which madness / Would gambol from." Polonius, II.2: "Though this be madness, yet there is method in't."
    - What the action does with it: Hamlet's madness is announced as a disguise, yet in the graveyard he leaps into Ophelia's grave to out-rant Laertes and then apologizes for it as if it were real ("I here proclaim was madness," V.2). Ophelia's madness is not feigned, and the Gentleman's description of it (IV.5) is a description of how the sane interpret nonsense: "they aim at it, / And botch the words up fit to their own thoughts." The play never settles whether Hamlet's "antic disposition" stayed a mask. It does show that a disguise worn long enough becomes hard to take off.

12. **Honour can drive men to die for nothing, and the same impulse that is folly in them may be the thing a hesitating man most lacks.**
    - Where it is spoken: Hamlet, IV.4, watching Fortinbras's army go to fight for "a little patch of ground / That hath in it no profit but the name": "Rightly to be great / Is not to stir without great argument, / But greatly to find quarrel in a straw / When honour's at the stake."
    - What the action does with it: Hamlet calls the war "th'imposthume of much wealth and peace" and twenty thousand men going "to their graves like beds" for "a fantasy and trick of fame," and in the same breath takes it as a rebuke to his own inaction. The play gives the last word to Fortinbras, the man of the eggshell, who comes home with "conquest" and inherits Denmark. The thoughtless man of honour wins; the play does not pretend this is admirable, only that it is what happens.

## What it teaches

The hardest thing in this play is that it will not let you prefer thought to action or action to thought. Hamlet's delay is not a flaw you can simply name and set aside; it is a position, argued with great intelligence, and the arguments are good. The ghost might be a devil. Killing a man at prayer might send him to heaven. Conscience, as he says, does make cowards, and the play shows that the word "coward" is not quite fair, since the alternative, the one time he acts on impulse, is to kill an old fool through a curtain and start the chain that drowns Ophelia. You come out of the play unable to say what Hamlet should have done, because every path shown is a path to the same floor full of bodies. What the play teaches is not a rule but a difficulty: that the gap between knowing and doing is not closed by more knowledge, and that it is not closed by less either.

The second thing the play leaves you with is how much of life is performance, and how little that makes performance false. Hamlet despises "seems" and then does nothing but seem; the player's tears for Hecuba are fake and move everyone; Claudius's prayer is empty and saves his life; Laertes's leap into the grave is a show and is also real grief. The play's ideal, Horatio, is the one who performs nothing and is almost nothing in the plot, a witness. The lesson, if it is one, is that a person who refuses to act a part refuses to act, and that the people who do act are always, somewhat, acting. Hamlet's dying request is for a performance: tell my story.

What is clearest is the play's sense of waste and dirt. Alexander stops a bung-hole; Polonius is eaten by worms; Ophelia is sung over and shoved into a half-sanctified grave. The play's gravediggers are the only people in it with "a property of easiness," and their ease comes from custom, from handling death every day until it means nothing. Hamlet cannot do that, and the play does not ask you to. It asks you to hold both: that death reduces everyone to the same clay, and that the one thing worth fighting for, in a dying man's last breath, is that someone absent himself from felicity awhile to tell the truth about him. That is not a resolution. It is what the play gives you to carry out of it.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between knowing what to do and doing it
- the gap between what people show and what they are
- how fast the dead are forgotten, and the duty to remember
- death as the leveller that turns kings into dirt
- plots that turn back on the plotter
- spying, feigning, and the impossibility of a private life at court
- whether purpose made in passion can survive the passion
- the difference between confessing a sin and repenting it
