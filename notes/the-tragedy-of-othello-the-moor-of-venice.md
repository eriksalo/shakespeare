# The Tragedy of Othello, the Moor of Venice

## What happens

A black general in the service of Venice secretly marries a senator's daughter, defends the marriage before the Duke, and is sent with her to Cyprus to meet a Turkish fleet that a storm destroys before he arrives. With no war to fight, he is worked on by his ensign, Iago, who has been passed over for promotion and who says he suspects the general of sleeping with his wife, though he admits he does not know. Iago gets the lieutenant Cassio drunk and cashiered, has him sue to Desdemona for reinstatement, and then reads her advocacy to Othello as evidence of adultery; a dropped handkerchief, picked up by Iago's wife Emilia and planted on Cassio, becomes the "ocular proof." Othello strikes his wife in public, smothers her in her bed, and learns from Emilia within minutes that the handkerchief was stolen and the whole thing a lie. Iago kills Emilia, Othello kills himself, and Iago is led away refusing ever to speak again. The shape is a marriage built in one act and destroyed, by talk alone, in the next three.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A person's outward conduct can be a complete disguise; "I am not what I am."**
   - Where it is spoken: Iago, I.1: "when my outward action doth demonstrate / The native act and figure of my heart / In complement extern, 'tis not long after / But I will wear my heart upon my sleeve / For daws to peck at: I am not what I am." Also III.3, to Othello: "Men should be what they seem; / Or those that be not, would they might seem none!"
   - What the action does with it: The play confirms this with a brutality unmatched elsewhere: the word "honest" is attached to Iago by nearly every character, repeatedly, and he is the least honest person on stage. Othello's trust rests on the opposite principle ("The Moor is of a free and open nature / That thinks men honest that but seem to be so," I.3, Iago's own diagnosis). The one person who sees through the surface is Emilia, and only after the murder: "I thought so then" (V.2). The play does not offer a way to tell the honest from the seeming-honest; Iago's closing refusal to explain himself ("What you know, you know") leaves the disguise intact even after it is exposed.

2. **The jealous mind does not need evidence; it manufactures it from trifles and from its own existence.**
   - Where it is spoken: Iago, III.3: "Trifles light as air / Are to the jealous confirmations strong / As proofs of holy writ." Emilia, III.4: "jealous souls will not be answer'd so; / They are not ever jealous for the cause, / But jealous for they are jealous: 'tis a monster / Begot upon itself, born on itself."
   - What the action does with it: Confirmed exactly: a handkerchief, a man leaving a room, a dream Iago says he overheard, Cassio laughing about another woman while Othello watches from hiding and supplies the dialogue himself ("Now he tells how she plucked him to my chamber," IV.1). Othello demanded "ocular proof" and accepts a report of a beard being wiped. The complication is that Othello was not a jealous man ("I think the sun where he was born / Drew all such humours from him," Desdemona, III.4), and his own account was that "To be once in doubt / Is once to be resolv'd" (III.3). The play shows that a person who prides himself on not being jealous has no defenses once the doubt is in; he goes from "I'll see before I doubt" to "I'll tear her all to pieces" within a single scene.

3. **Being told you are deceived hurts more than being deceived; ignorance was a kind of possession.**
   - Where it is spoken: Othello, III.3: "What sense had I of her stol'n hours of lust? / I saw't not, thought it not, it harm'd not me... He that is robb'd, not wanting what is stol'n, / Let him not know't, and he's not robb'd at all." And: "I swear 'tis better to be much abus'd / Than but to know't a little."
   - What the action does with it: The play lets this stand as true and then shows the trap in it: Othello is not deceived by his wife, he is deceived by Iago, and the knowledge he says he would rather not have is false knowledge. Everything he has lost (the tranquil mind, the plumed troops, "Othello's occupation") he loses over nothing. The speech's logic is correct about an actual betrayal and fatal when applied to an invented one, and Othello cannot tell which he is in.

4. **Reputation is both the "immortal part" of a person and "an idle and most false imposition," depending on who is speaking and why.**
   - Where it is spoken: Cassio, II.3: "I have lost my reputation! I have lost the immortal part of myself, and what remains is bestial." Iago, same scene: "Reputation is an idle and most false imposition, oft got without merit and lost without deserving." Then Iago to Othello, III.3: "Good name in man and woman... / Is the immediate jewel of their souls."
   - What the action does with it: Iago says both things within a few scenes, each to serve a purpose: to calm Cassio and to inflame Othello. The play shows reputation to be exactly as real as people make it: Cassio does lose his place; Desdemona's "name, that was as fresh / As Dian's visage, is now begrim'd" (III.3) on the strength of nothing; Iago's reputation for honesty is the engine of every death. The play does not resolve whether reputation is substance or air. It shows that it kills either way.

5. **A good quality can be turned into the net that catches its owner.**
   - Where it is spoken: Iago, II.3: "So will I turn her virtue into pitch, / And out of her own goodness make the net / That shall enmesh them all." And of Cassio, V.1: "He hath a daily beauty in his life / That makes me ugly."
   - What the action does with it: This is the method of the whole plot and the play confirms it without qualification. Desdemona's generosity in pleading for Cassio is the thing read as lust; Othello's openness is the thing that makes him "tenderly be led by the nose"; Cassio's courtesy in kissing Emilia's hand is "an index and obscure prologue to the history of lust" (II.1). Even Emilia's wifely obedience, picking up the handkerchief "nothing but to please his fantasy" (III.3), delivers the murder weapon. No one is undone by a vice. The play's darkest claim is that virtue, in the presence of someone who hates it, is a liability.

6. **A person's self is made partly of their work; take away the occupation and the self collapses.**
   - Where it is spoken: Othello, III.3: "Farewell the tranquil mind! Farewell content! / Farewell the plumed troops and the big wars / That make ambition virtue!... Othello's occupation's gone!" And V.2: "That's he that was Othello. Here I am."
   - What the action does with it: The Turks are drowned in II.1, before Othello lands. From that moment he is a general with no war, on an island, with only his marriage to attend to, and the play is explicit that the domestic replaces the military: "Our general's wife is now the general" (II.3). His last speech returns to Aleppo and a Turk he once killed, and he dies re-enacting that stroke on himself. The play shows a man whose identity was built on a public role and who, when that role is removed, has nothing to stand on but a love he cannot trust.

7. **Love that begins in storytelling can be destroyed by storytelling, because it was always a matter of what was believed.**
   - Where it is spoken: Othello, I.3: "She lov'd me for the dangers I had pass'd, / And I lov'd her that she did pity them. / This only is the witchcraft I have us'd." Brabantio, I.3: "I never yet did hear / That the bruis'd heart was pierced through the ear." Iago, II.3: "I'll pour this pestilence into his ear."
   - What the action does with it: Brabantio's line is refuted by the entire play. Desdemona's heart was won through the ear, by tales; Othello's is poisoned through the ear, by tales; Iago "lay with Cassio" and reports a dream he invents. Not one piece of what Othello believes about his wife's adultery is anything but speech. The play confirms that the ear is where people are made and unmade, and shows that a man who knows his whole fortune was built on narrative ("I think this tale would win my daughter too," says the Duke) has no reason to doubt that a counter-narrative could be true.

8. **Men treat women as objects of appetite and then punish them for being thought to have appetites.**
   - Where it is spoken: Emilia, III.4: "They are all but stomachs and we all but food; / They eat us hungerly, and when they are full, / They belch us." And IV.3: "Let husbands know / Their wives have sense like them: they see, and smell / And have their palates both for sweet and sour, / As husbands have... The ills we do, their ills instruct us so."
   - What the action does with it: Emilia's speech is the one sustained argument in the play from a woman's side, and the play places it the night before Desdemona is killed for a sin she did not commit and would not ("Beshrew me, if I would do such a wrong for the whole world"). Othello's rage is specifically at the idea of her having a body with wants ("O curse of marriage, / That we can call these delicate creatures ours, / And not their appetites!" III.3). The play confirms Emilia's charge by its action; what it adds is that the one woman who says she might cheat (Emilia, "for the whole world") is faithful, and the one who says she never would is killed as a whore. Nobody's stated principle predicts what happens to them.

9. **Obedience in a wife is praised and is also what gets her killed; the play cannot tell which it is admiring.**
   - Where it is spoken: Desdemona, III.3: "Whate'er you be, I am obedient." Lodovico, IV.1: "Truly, an obedient lady." Desdemona, IV.3: "My love doth so approve him, / That even his stubbornness, his checks, his frowns... have grace and favour in them."
   - What the action does with it: Desdemona chose her husband against her father and defended the choice in public (I.3); she is not timid. But after he strikes her she says only "I have not deserv'd this," goes to bed when ordered, dismisses her attendant when ordered, and with her last breath lies to protect him: "Nobody; I myself" (V.2). Emilia, by contrast, disobeys her husband to speak ("'Tis proper I obey him, but not now") and is stabbed for it. The play puts both women dead on the same bed. It does not say whether Desdemona's obedience was love or a failure to defend herself, and it gives her final lie the same weight as Emilia's final truth.

10. **A man can commit a murder and call it a sacrifice; the name he gives the act is part of how he does it.**
    - Where it is spoken: Othello, V.2: "It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul... Yet she must die, else she'll betray more men." And: "O perjur'd woman, thou dost stone my heart, / And mak'st me call what I intend to do / A murder, which I thought a sacrifice."
    - What the action does with it: Othello enters the bedroom talking of justice and chaste stars and the need not to kill her soul; he will not shed her blood or scar her skin. Desdemona's denials, which are the truth, are what he experiences as perjury turning his sacrifice into a murder. The play shows that the ceremony is a way of not seeing what he is doing, and it breaks the ceremony with her plain voice: "Kill me tomorrow, let me live tonight!... But while I say one prayer!" "It is too late." The last "honourable murderer... naught did I in hate, but all in honour" (V.2) is the same self-description, and Lodovico's "All that's spoke is marr'd" is the play's verdict on it.

11. **Grief and shame can be given words, and the words can themselves be a kind of self-flattery.**
    - Where it is spoken: Othello, V.2: "Speak of me as I am. Nothing extenuate, / Nor set down aught in malice. Then must you speak / Of one that loved not wisely, but too well; / Of one not easily jealous, but being wrought, / Perplex'd in the extreme."
    - What the action does with it: The speech asks for an unvarnished account and then supplies a varnished one: he was not easily jealous (the play showed him reaching murder in one scene); he loved too well (the play showed him calling her a whore in front of a visitor). Yet the play also lets the speech be beautiful and lets him die on a kiss, and Cassio's last word on him is "he was great of heart." The play does not settle whether this is a man seeing himself truly at last or a man still telling the story that won Desdemona. It makes you hold both.

12. **The injured party can be made to thank, love, and reward the one who injures him.**
    - Where it is spoken: Iago, II.1: "Make the Moor thank me, love me, and reward me / For making him egregiously an ass / And practicing upon his peace and quiet / Even to madness." Othello, III.3: "I am bound to thee for ever." V.1: "O brave Iago, honest and just, / That hast such noble sense of thy friend's wrong!"
    - What the action does with it: Confirmed line by line. The scene in which Othello and Iago kneel together and swear a vow (III.3), with Iago promoted to lieutenant at the end of it, is a marriage ceremony between the victim and the man destroying him. The play's most unbearable moments are the ones where Othello is grateful. What complicates it is that Iago gets nothing he wanted from this: no clear motive ever holds (the promotion, the wife, "I know not if 't be true"), and he ends in custody with torture promised. The play shows the mechanism working perfectly and shows the man who built it with no profit from it.

## What it teaches

The thing this play leaves you with is how little it took. There is no witch, no ghost, no prophecy, no civil war. There is one man who dislikes another and talks to him, and a handkerchief. Everything Othello comes to believe is made of words, and he had just told the Senate that words were how he won his wife. The play is precise about the fact that he never sees anything: he hides and watches a conversation he cannot hear and narrates it to himself; he is told of a dream; he is told of a beard wiped. His demand for "ocular proof" is satisfied by a report. What the play teaches, if it teaches, is that belief does not wait for evidence and that the people who think themselves hardest to move ("not easily jealous") are the ones with no practice at resisting. You cannot read Othello and come away confident that you would have seen through Iago, because nobody did, including Iago's wife.

The second thing is that goodness is not protection. Desdemona's advocacy for Cassio is the kindest act in the play and it is the thing that condemns her; Cassio's good manners are read as seduction; Othello's trust is the instrument of his ruin; Emilia's small marital compliance delivers the handkerchief. Iago says it plainly: he will "out of her own goodness make the net." The play does not pretend that this is a rare case. It gives the hatred of goodness a face and a voice ("He hath a daily beauty in his life / That makes me ugly") and then refuses to explain it further. Iago's last line is a refusal to speak. You are left with the fact of malice and no account of it, which is probably the honest position.

What is hardest to settle is Othello himself. The final speech asks to be judged fairly and then judges itself kindly, and the play allows both the beauty of the speech and the lie in it. He killed his wife with a ceremony of justice over her bed while she begged for half an hour. He also loved her, and when he learned the truth he did not try to live. Lodovico's last instruction is to hide the bed, because "the object poisons sight." The play has spent its last scene making you look at it. The question it leaves is whether the man who could say "the pity of it, Iago" in the same breath as "I will chop her into messes" is a noble nature destroyed or a destroyer who could speak nobly, and the play's honesty is that these are not alternatives.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between what people seem and what they are
- how belief outruns evidence, and how little evidence a mind needs
- goodness as the thing that can be turned against its owner
- the word "honest" and who gets to wear it
- love and ruin both entering through the ear
- a self built on a public role, and what is left when the role goes
- what men assume about women's bodies and loyalty
- the names people give their violence (justice, sacrifice, honour)
