# King Richard the Second

## What happens
Richard, a young king who rules by words and ceremony, stops a trial by combat between his cousin Bolingbroke and Mowbray and banishes both, then seizes the dead Gaunt's estate to pay for an Irish war. Bolingbroke returns "for Lancaster," his own inheritance; the nobles and commons, already bled by taxes and flatterers, flow to him, and Richard, arriving one day late from Ireland, finds his army dispersed. He surrenders at Flint Castle, is made to perform his own deposition in Westminster Hall, is parted from his queen, imprisoned at Pomfret, and killed by Exton, who took a muttered wish of the new king for a command. Henry IV ends the play disowning the murder he wanted and promising a pilgrimage to wash off the blood.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A man's name and reputation can feel more necessary to him than his life.**
   - Spoken: Mowbray, I.1: "The purest treasure mortal times afford / Is spotless reputation; that away, / Men are but gilded loam or painted clay... Mine honour is my life; both grow in one."
   - What the action does: The trial that would have settled Mowbray's honour is cancelled by a king throwing down a baton; he is banished for life, loses even his language ("Within my mouth you have engaoled my tongue," I.3), and dies offstage in Venice (IV.1). In IV.1 a half-dozen lords throw gages at one another and nothing is ever tried. The play treats the honour-code as real to those who hold it and useless as a way of deciding anything: the king's whim, not the lists, determines outcomes.

2. **The king's word can shorten or lengthen a life, but not a single minute of time itself.**
   - Spoken: Bolingbroke, I.3: "How long a time lies in one little word! / Four lagging winters and four wanton springs / End in a word: such is the breath of kings." Gaunt's immediate reply: "But not a minute, king, that thou canst give."
   - What the action does: Richard's breath sends men into exile and seizes estates, and the play shows how quickly that breath stops being "sterling" (IV.1) once armed men stop believing in it. By IV.1 Richard has to ask whether his word can still command a mirror. The power of royal speech is shown to be borrowed from the obedience of others, not inherent.

3. **God anoints kings; no subject can unmake one.**
   - Spoken: Richard, III.2: "Not all the water in the rough rude sea / Can wash the balm off from an anointed king; / The breath of worldly men cannot depose / The deputy elected by the Lord." Gaunt, I.2: "God's is the quarrel." Carlisle, IV.1: "What subject can give sentence on his king?"
   - What the action does: The breath of worldly men deposes him, and Richard himself completes the thought: "With mine own tears I wash away my balm" (IV.1). The play never refutes the doctrine in argument—Carlisle's prophecy of "Golgotha" is answered only with his arrest—but it shows the doctrine has no force without soldiers. Richard's reliance on angels ("if angels fight, / Weak men must fall") is set beside Salisbury's arithmetic: twelve thousand Welshmen gone because he came "one day too late."

4. **Wasted time comes back and wastes the one who wasted it.**
   - Spoken: Richard, V.5: "I wasted time, and now doth time waste me; / For now hath time made me his numb'ring clock." Gaunt, II.1: "His rash fierce blaze of riot cannot last."
   - What the action does: Confirmed with brutal literalness. Richard prays to reach the dying Gaunt "too late" (I.4) and is then himself one day too late to save his crown (III.2). Gaunt's prophecy that the "insatiate cormorant... soon preys upon itself" is what the Gardener later describes as a fact: "waste of idle hours hath quite thrown down" the crown (III.4).

5. **A ruler surrounded by flatterers cannot hear the one thing that would save him.**
   - Spoken: Gaunt, II.1: "A thousand flatterers sit within thy crown." York, II.1: "it is stopped with other flattering sounds." Richard, IV.1, to the mirror: "O flatt'ring glass, / Like to my followers in prosperity, / Thou dost beguile me."
   - What the action does: The play complicates this in a cruel way. Richard curses Bushy, Green and Wiltshire as "Three Judases" for making peace with Bolingbroke, and is told they died for him: "Their peace is made / With heads, and not with hands" (III.2). The flatterers were loyal; the king, habituated to flattery, cannot recognize loyalty when it is given. And at the end Richard notices that the man who deposed him now flatters him with "fair cousin" (IV.1)—flattery changes its object but never stops.

6. **Grief has shadows that are not the thing itself, yet the thing itself can be real before it has a name.**
   - Spoken: Bushy, II.2: "Each substance of a grief hath twenty shadows, / Which shows like grief itself, but is not so." The Queen: "Some unborn sorrow, ripe in Fortune's womb, / Is coming towards me... 'Tis nameless woe, I wot."
   - What the action does: Green enters with the news of Bolingbroke's landing within the same scene. The Queen's "nothing" was a something. Later Bolingbroke tells Richard that "the shadow of your sorrow hath destroyed / The shadow of your face" (IV.1) and Richard accepts it: "my grief lies all within." The play lets both stand: outward laments are shadows, but the inward grief is substance, and foreboding is sometimes knowledge.

7. **You cannot think your way out of real suffering.**
   - Spoken: Gaunt, I.3: "Teach thy necessity to reason thus: / There is no virtue like necessity... Think not the King did banish thee, / But thou the King." Bolingbroke's reply: "O, who can hold a fire in his hand / By thinking on the frosty Caucasus?... the apprehension of the good / Gives but the greater feeling to the worse."
   - What the action does: Bolingbroke does not console himself; he raises ships. Richard, by contrast, spends V.5 trying to "hammer out" a comparison between his prison and the world and finds "no thought is contented." The man who refuses consolation acts; the man who makes consolations into art is killed in the act of making them. Yet it is Richard's thinking that the play gives its best lines to.

8. **Kingship is a role played inside a hollow crown, and the man inside it eats bread and needs friends like anyone.**
   - Spoken: Richard, III.2: "within the hollow crown / That rounds the mortal temples of a king / Keeps Death his court... Allowing him a breath, a little scene, / To monarchize... I live with bread like you, feel want, / Taste grief, need friends." York, V.2: "As in a theatre the eyes of men / After a well-graced actor leaves the stage, / Are idly bent on him that enters next."
   - What the action does: Richard is made to perform his resignation "in common view" (IV.1) so the deposition will look voluntary. Without the office he has "no name, no title." But the play also shows that the man who sees the crown as hollow cannot then stop playing: in prison he is "in one person many people, / And none contented" (V.5). The insight is true and does not help him.

9. **Break the law of inheritance for your own gain and you break the thing that makes you king.**
   - Spoken: York, II.1: "Take Hereford's rights away, and take from Time / His charters and his customary rights... for how art thou a king / But by fair sequence and succession?"
   - What the action does: Confirmed exactly. Richard seizes Gaunt's goods; Bolingbroke comes "for Lancaster"; the same lords who were "pilled" by the king's exactions ride to Ravenspurgh. But the play then doubles it: Bolingbroke swears his coming "hath no further scope / Than for his lineal royalties" (III.3) and within an act is on the throne. The gap between what he says he wants and what he takes is never explained by him—"I come but for mine own" is the last thing he says before accepting a kingdom.

10. **The love of the crowd is purchased and follows the winner.**
   - Spoken: Bagot, II.2: "the wavering commons, for their love / Lies in their purses; and whoso empties them, / By so much fills their hearts with deadly hate." Richard, I.4, watching Bolingbroke "dive into their hearts / With humble and familiar courtesy."
   - What the action does: Confirmed. York's account of the London entry (V.2)—"all tongues cried 'God save thee, Bolingbroke!'" while "dust was thrown upon his sacred head"—is the play's picture of public affection. The one person who seeks Richard out in prison is a groom who once dressed his horse; "love to Richard / Is a strange brooch in this all-hating world" (V.5).

11. **A wish spoken by a powerful man is heard as an order, and he will disown it afterward.**
   - Spoken: Exton, V.4: "'Have I no friend will rid me of this living fear?'... I am the King's friend, and will rid his foe." Henry, V.6: "They love not poison that do poison need, / Nor do I thee. Though I did wish him dead, / I hate the murderer, love him murdered."
   - What the action does: The play rhymes this with its opening: Richard's "Pray God we may make haste and come too late!" (I.4), and Mowbray's confession that for Gloucester's death he "neglected my sworn duty" (I.1). Kings in this play kill at one remove and keep their hands clean in language. Exton's "O, would the deed were good!" is the only honest line on the subject. Henry's pilgrimage is a promise, not a cleansing.

12. **Loyalty is split between oath and blood, and the split cannot be resolved by principle.**
   - Spoken: York, II.2: "Th' one is my sovereign, whom both my oath / And duty bids defend; th' other again / Is my kinsman, whom the King hath wronged... I know not what to do." Duchess of York, V.3: "Love loving not itself none other can."
   - What the action does: York declares himself "neuter," then rides with Bolingbroke; then, to prove himself true to the new king, tries to have his own son executed, while his wife begs on her knees. The scene becomes, in Henry's words, "The Beggar and the King." Mercy wins—but in the same scene Henry sends men to kill the other conspirators, and the next scene brings their heads to London. Pardon and execution are issued by the same mouth within minutes.

13. **Patience is called a virtue in the humble and cowardice in the great, and in anyone it wears out.**
   - Spoken: Duchess of Gloucester, I.2: "That which in mean men we entitle patience / Is pale cold cowardice in noble breasts." Richard, V.5: "Patience is stale, and I am weary of it."
   - What the action does: Richard, who has made a long art of suffering, finally strikes his keeper and kills two men before he dies. York bows to "heaven's hand" and calls it "calm contents" (V.2). The play does not decide whether patience is holy or merely what the defeated call their defeat.

## What it teaches

The hardest thing this play asks you to hold is that it gives the best words to the worst ruler. Richard does nothing well except lose: he farms the realm, seizes a dead man's estate, prays to arrive too late at a deathbed, and returns from Ireland one day after his army has gone home. Yet from the moment he begins losing he sees more than anyone else on stage—that the crown is hollow, that his flatterers were loyal, that a king "needs friends," that he has wasted time. The play does not let you take his insight as compensation. Carlisle says plainly that "wise men ne'er sit and wail their woes, / But presently prevent the ways to wail" (III.2), and he is right, and Richard cannot do it. Seeing the truth and being able to act on it are two different capacities, and the play keeps them in different men.

Bolingbroke is the other capacity. He says almost nothing about himself. He states a limited claim ("I come but for mine own") and then takes everything, and the text never shows him deciding to. What it shows instead is the sequence: an injustice, a landing, a stream of lords, a kneeling at Flint, a throne. The reader is left to decide whether he lied in III.3 or whether power simply flowed to the man who did not refuse it. The play's last scene suggests he knows the price: he wanted Richard dead, got it, and now cannot bear the man who did it. That is the first lesson in a habit this play seems to think belongs to kings—to will an outcome and refuse the deed.

What is unresolved is the doctrine itself. Nobody in the play ever answers Carlisle. The balm is washed off, but by Richard's own tears, and the one who washes it off calls himself a traitor "with the rest." The text lets you believe either that God's deputy was deposed by sacrilege, or that "God's deputy" was a story that held only as long as people were paid to tell it. The Gardener, the one person who speaks about the kingdom as a thing to be tended rather than owned, says the king could have kept his crown if he had pruned "great and growing men" in time. That is a practical sentence, not a sacred one, and the play puts it in the mouth of a servant.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between the authority of words and the force that backs them
- time wasted and time running out
- flattery, and the inability to recognize loyalty
- the crown as a role played by a mortal body
- inheritance and succession as the ground of legitimacy
- the crowd's love following whoever wins
- wishing a death without ordering it, and disowning it afterward
- grief as something with an inside and an outside
