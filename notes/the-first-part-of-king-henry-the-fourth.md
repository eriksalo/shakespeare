# The First Part of King Henry the Fourth

## What happens
Henry IV, "shaken" and "wan with care," cannot get to his crusade because his kingdom will not stay quiet: Mortimer is taken in Wales, and young Hotspur, fresh from beating the Scots, refuses to hand over his prisoners. The Percys, who made Henry king, feel discarded and conspire with Glendower, Mortimer and Douglas to divide England three ways. Meanwhile the heir, Prince Hal, lives in an Eastcheap tavern with Falstaff and a crew of thieves, robs travellers at Gad's Hill, and tells the audience in private that his idleness is a planned foil for a dazzling reformation. Summoned by his father and compared unfavourably to Hotspur, Hal promises to take the rebel's honours for his own. At Shrewsbury the rebels fight short-handed (Northumberland sick, Glendower held back by prophecies), Worcester hides the King's offer of pardon because he cannot believe it, Hal saves his father's life and kills Hotspur, and Falstaff, having played dead, claims the kill. Worcester is executed; the war goes on.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Honour is either the one thing worth dying for or nothing but a word, depending on who is speaking and whether he is about to die.**
   - Spoken: Hotspur, I.3: "By Heaven, methinks it were an easy leap / To pluck bright honour from the pale-faced moon." Falstaff, V.1: "What is honour? A word. What is in that word, 'honour'? What is that 'honour'? Air... Who hath it? He that died o' Wednesday. Doth he feel it? No."
   - What the action does: Both are tested on the same field. Hotspur, dying, says the titles Hal has taken "wound my thoughts worse than thy sword my flesh" (V.4): honour is the last thing he cares about, and it is now someone else's. Falstaff stands over Blunt's body—"There's honour for you"—and lives by lying down. Then he carries off Hotspur's corpse and claims the kill, and Hal lets him: "if a lie may do thee grace, / I'll gild it with the happiest terms I have." The play does not decide between them; it shows that honour is awarded by whoever survives to speak.

2. **A man can plan his own redemption as a performance, making his faults a dark background so his virtue will shine.**
   - Spoken: Hal, I.2: "I know you all, and will awhile uphold / The unyok'd humour of your idleness... like bright metal on a sullen ground, / My reformation, glitt'ring o'er my fault, / Shall show more goodly."
   - What the action does: It works exactly as planned. He promises his father in III.2 to "redeem all this on Percy's head," rescues the King at Shrewsbury ("Thou hast redeem'd thy lost opinion," V.4), and kills Hotspur. Vernon reports him as "England's sweet hope... so much misconstrued in his wantonness." But the play lets you see what it costs: the people on the "sullen ground" are people. When Falstaff pleads "Banish plump Jack, and banish all the world," Hal answers, still inside the game, "I do, I will" (II.4). The plan requires that the companions be a foil, and a foil is not a friend.

3. **What is seldom seen is wondered at; what is seen daily is despised.**
   - Spoken: the King, III.2: "By being seldom seen, I could not stir / But like a comet I was wonder'd at"; of Richard: "Heard, not regarded; seen, but with such eyes / As, sick and blunted with community, / Afford no extraordinary gaze."
   - What the action does: Hal already said this in his own voice in I.2 ("If all the year were playing holidays, / To sport would be as tedious as to work"), so the son has the father's theory before he hears it. But Hal practices the opposite: he goes among drawers and learns to "drink with any tinker in his own language" (II.4). The play lets both strategies produce a king, and leaves open whether Hal's familiarity will cheapen him as his father fears or give him something his father never had.

4. **The man who climbed by breaking an oath has taught the people who helped him that oaths can be broken.**
   - Spoken: Hotspur, IV.3: "He came but to be Duke of Lancaster... He presently, as greatness knows itself, / Steps me a little higher than his vow." Worcester, V.1: "You swore to us... at Doncaster, / That you did nothing purpose 'gainst the state."
   - What the action does: The King's only reply is that rebels always find "water-colours to impaint" their cause (V.1), which does not deny the charge. The lesson of Ravenspurgh is now the rebels' method. The play lets the accusation stand and shows its consequence: nobody in this world can take a promise at face value, including the King's promise of pardon.

5. **Once trust is broken, a pardon cannot be believed, and the fear of punishment produces the crime that earns it.**
   - Spoken: Worcester, V.2: "It is not possible, it cannot be, / The King should keep his word in loving us... Suspicion all our lives shall be stuck full of eyes, / For treason is but trusted like the fox." Earlier, I.3: "To save our heads by raising of a head."
   - What the action does: Confirmed in the cruellest way. The rebellion begins because the Percys expect to be discarded; Worcester hides the King's "liberal and kind offer" from Hotspur because he cannot believe it; the battle follows; Hotspur dies and Worcester is executed for the very thing he lied to prevent. His last word is that "my safety urged me to" it (V.5).

6. **Imagining a great exploit is not the same as attending to its conditions.**
   - Spoken: Northumberland, I.3: "Imagination of some great exploit / Drives him beyond the bounds of patience." Worcester: "He apprehends a world of figures here, / But not the form of what he should attend."
   - What the action does: Hotspur reads a cautious letter and calls the writer "a frosty-spirited rogue" (II.3); reads his father's absence as something that "lends a lustre" (IV.1); hears the King's army is thirty thousand and says "Forty let it be"; insists on fighting "tonight" with tired horses (IV.3). He dies, and the play gives him the realization: "thoughts, the slaves of life, and life, time's fool, / And time... Must have a stop" (V.4). Hal's epitaph is exact: "Ill-weaved ambition, how much art thou shrunk!"

7. **Staying alive is a kind of truth, and a counterfeit death is "the true and perfect image of life."**
   - Spoken: Falstaff, V.4: "The better part of valour is discretion, in the which better part I have saved my life." V.3: "Give me life, which if I can save, so: if not, honour comes unlooked for, and there's an end."
   - What the action does: He survives, which no one else on the field who shares his view of honour does, because no one else holds it. But the play qualifies the triumph: Hal, thinking him dead, says "I could have better spared a better man" and "I should have a heavy miss of thee / If I were much in love with vanity" (V.4). The affection is real and conditional, and the condition is named.

8. **Common lives are cheap to those who spend them.**
   - Spoken: Falstaff, IV.2: "food for powder, food for powder, they'll fill a pit as well as better. Tush, man, mortal men, mortal men." V.3: "There's not three of my hundred and fifty left alive, and they are for the town's end, to beg during life."
   - What the action does: Falstaff has taken bribes from the able and pressed the ragged; the Prince looks at them, says "I did never see such pitiful rascals," and rides on. Nobody is punished. The King sends men "marching in his coats" so that Blunt dies as a decoy: "A borrow'd title hast thou bought too dear" (V.3). And the King's own line, "nothing can seem foul to those that win" (V.1), is the moral the victory obeys. The play's first scene with ordinary people is two carriers at four in the morning, flea-bitten, remembering an ostler who "never joyed since the price of oats rose."

9. **A lie exposed is a pleasure that binds the liar and the exposer together.**
   - Spoken: Poins, I.2: "The virtue of this jest will be the incomprehensible lies that this same fat rogue will tell us... and in the reproof of this lives the jest." Falstaff, II.4: "Is not the truth the truth?"
   - What the action does: Two men in buckram become eleven; caught, Falstaff says "I knew ye" and invents "instinct." No lie costs him anything, and the game is why Hal keeps him. Hotspur, meanwhile, tells Glendower to "tell truth, and shame the devil" (III.1) and is the one person the play has deceived to his death, by his own uncle. Being a truth-teller by temperament is no protection against liars.

10. **A father can envy another man's son and call his own his enemy, and be proved wrong by one deed.**
   - Spoken: the King, I.1: "O, that it could be proved / That some night-tripping fairy had exchanged / In cradle-clothes our children." III.2: "thou... my nearest and dearest enemy."
   - What the action does: Hal drives Douglas off his father in V.4, and the King's response is grudging and precise: "show'd thou mak'st some tender of my life." Hal's reply is that he could simply have let Douglas do it. The play treats love between father and son as something proved by an act, not by words—the King never says he loves him, and the one eye that "hath desired to see thee more" (III.2) is the nearest it comes.

11. **Fellowship among the lawless is real and also the first thing abandoned under pressure.**
   - Spoken: Falstaff, II.2: "A plague upon it when thieves cannot be true one to another!" II.4: "Banish plump Jack, and banish all the world."
   - What the action does: The Prince robs his own gang for a joke; Falstaff hides behind the arras while Hal lies to the sheriff for him; the Prince takes a drawer who has "fewer words than a parrot" and makes sport of him for being trapped between two callers (II.4). The warmth is real—the play spends more time in the tavern than at court—and every instance of it is also a use.

12. **War enters a man's body before he says a word about it.**
   - Spoken: Lady Percy, II.3: "In thy faint slumbers I by thee have watch'd, / And heard thee murmur tales of iron wars... beads of sweat have stood upon thy brow."
   - What the action does: Hotspur answers "Why, my horse, my love, my horse," and tells her she will not "utter what thou dost not know." Mortimer and his Welsh wife cannot speak each other's language and manage with kisses. The play's marriages are affectionate, and the men leave anyway; Lady Percy's last line in the scene is "It must, of force."

## What it teaches

The play puts three theories of how to live next to each other and lets each be tested by the same battle. Hotspur's: live for the thing that outlasts you, and hurry. Falstaff's: nothing outlasts you, so stay alive and eat. Hal's: time everything, including your virtue, for effect. Shrewsbury rewards the third. But the play is careful not to let the reward look like justice. Hotspur dies because his uncle lied to him out of fear; Falstaff lives because he lay down; Hal wins because his opponent arrived with half an army. "Nothing can seem foul to those that win" is said by the King as the sun rises, and the play means it as description, not endorsement.

What is hardest to settle is Hal. The soliloquy in I.2 tells you he is never not calculating, and the rest of the play confirms it: the promise to his father, the challenge to single combat that Vernon calls so modest, the epitaph over Hotspur, the gilded lie for Falstaff. Every act is handsome and every act is also an investment. The text gives you no scene in which he is unguarded. You can read that as the discipline a prince must have, or as the thing that makes "I do, I will" the truest line he speaks. The play does not help you choose; it only makes sure you hear the line.

Underneath the three men is a lesson about trust that the play states without raising its voice. The King got his crown by swearing he wanted something smaller. The men who believed him now cannot believe his pardon, and their disbelief kills the best of them. Nobody lies in this play for the pleasure of it except Falstaff, and his lies are the only ones that hurt no one. The lies that kill are told by sober men for safety.

## Preoccupations

- honour: what it is worth and who gets to award it
- reformation staged for effect; virtue as performance
- oaths broken by the powerful and the distrust that follows
- fear producing the harm it fears
- ambition that imagines the prize and ignores the conditions
- the cheapness of common lives in a lords' war
- lying as play and lying as policy
- fellowship that is real and provisional at once
- fathers and sons measuring each other
