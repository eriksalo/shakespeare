# The Life of King Henry the Fifth

## What happens
A Chorus asks the audience to supply with imagination what the stage cannot. Two bishops, afraid of a bill that would strip the Church's lands, offer the new king money and a legal argument for his claim to France; Henry asks Canterbury to be sure the claim is just, is told "The sin upon my head," and resolves on war when the Dauphin sends tennis balls. At Southampton he exposes three traitors, one of them his closest friend, and sends them to death. In Eastcheap Falstaff dies offstage, and Pistol, Nym, Bardolph and the Boy go to France to steal. Harfleur yields under a threat of rape and infanticide; Bardolph is hanged for robbing a church with the king's approval; the sick, starving English are offered ransom and refuse. On the night before Agincourt Henry walks disguised among his soldiers, argues with one of them about the king's responsibility for the deaths of his men, and prays that God not remember his father's crime. The English win against five-to-one odds; Henry orders the prisoners' throats cut; the dead are counted (ten thousand French, twenty-nine English of whom four have names). Henry woos Katharine, who is already "our capital demand" in the treaty, and the Epilogue says his son lost it all.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A sudden reformation is called a miracle by those who profit from it, and they admit, in the same breath, that miracles have ceased.**
   - Spoken: Canterbury, I.1: "Consideration like an angel came / And whipped th' offending Adam out of him"; then: "It must be so, for miracles are ceased, / And therefore we must needs admit the means / How things are perfected." The Constable, II.4: "Covering discretion with a coat of folly; / As gardeners do with ordure hide those roots."
   - What the action does: The men who praise the change are the men who need the king to invade France so the Commons will not take their lands; the praise and the motive are in the same scene. Henry himself tells the ambassadors he used his wild days ("Not measuring what use we made of them," I.2). The play does not doubt the change; it shows who benefits from describing it as grace.

2. **A ruler can ask whether his cause is just in a way that makes someone else answer for it.**
   - Spoken: Henry, I.2: "take heed how you impawn our person, / How you awake our sleeping sword of war... For God doth know how many now in health / Shall drop their blood in approbation / Of what your reverence shall incite us to." Canterbury: "The sin upon my head, dread sovereign!"
   - What the action does: The Archbishop has already offered money for the French war before the king asks his conscience (I.1), and the Salic-law speech he gives is long, self-interested, and never examined. The question "May I with right and conscience make this claim?" is asked after the answer has been bought. On the night before the battle a common soldier reopens it—"if the cause be not good, the King himself hath a heavy reckoning"—and the king's long answer addresses whether he is responsible for his soldiers' damnation, not whether the cause is good. Williams's "That's more than we know" is never answered.

3. **Threats are phrased so the victim will be guilty of what is done to him.**
   - Spoken: Henry to Harfleur, III.3: "What is it then to me, if impious War... Do with his smirch'd complexion all fell feats... What is't to me, when you yourselves are cause?" To the Dauphin, I.2: "his soul / Shall stand sore charged for the wasteful vengeance." Exeter, II.4: "on your head / Turning the widows' tears."
   - What the action does: Harfleur yields and the king says "Use mercy to them all." The threatened atrocities are not committed there. But at Agincourt, when the French reinforce, the order comes at once: "Then every soldier kill his prisoners" (IV.6)—given before Henry hears that the boys have been slaughtered (IV.7). Gower then supplies the justification afterward: "the King, most worthily, hath caus'd every soldier to cut his prisoner's throat. O, 'tis a gallant king!" The play places the order and its excuse in that sequence and leaves them there.

4. **The king is only a man, and the man who says so arranges for someone else to take the blow meant for him.**
   - Spoken: Henry, disguised, IV.1: "I think the King is but a man as I am. The violet smells to him as it doth to me... His ceremonies laid by, in his nakedness he appears but a man." Alone: "And what have kings, that privates have not too, / Save ceremony, save general ceremony?"
   - What the action does: He exchanges gloves with Williams as an equal, then gives the glove to Fluellen so that the Welshman receives the box on the ear (IV.7-8). When Williams says "Your Majesty came not like yourself... you appear'd to me but as a common man," Henry fills the glove with crowns and does not answer the point. The equality was true for a night and in a borrowed cloak.

5. **Penitence that comes after the profit is not finished, and the one who prays knows it.**
   - Spoken: Henry, IV.1: "Not today, O Lord, / O, not today, think not upon the fault / My father made in compassing the crown!... Though all that I can do is nothing worth, / Since that my penitence comes after all, / Imploring pardon."
   - What the action does: The victory comes and Henry gives it all to God, proclaiming death to any man who boasts (IV.8). But the Epilogue says "Fortune made his sword" and that his son "lost France and made his England bleed." The play ends by taking back what it gave. Whether the prayer was heard is left to the sequel the Chorus says the stage has often shown.

6. **Honour is cheaper when there are fewer to share it, and the dead who have no names are not counted among the brothers.**
   - Spoken: Henry, IV.3: "The fewer men, the greater share of honour... We few, we happy few, we band of brothers; / For he today that sheds his blood with me / Shall be my brother; be he ne'er so vile, / This day shall gentle his condition."
   - What the action does: The English dead are listed as "Edward the Duke of York, the Earl of Suffolk, / Sir Richard Ketly, Davy Gam, esquire; / None else of name; and of all other men / But five and twenty" (IV.8). The common men of the play are Bardolph (hanged), Nym (hanged), the Boy (killed with the luggage) and Pistol, who goes home to steal. The gentling of condition is promised in a speech and tested in a list.

7. **Old men remember "with advantages," and the legend begins its decay the moment the battle ends.**
   - Spoken: Henry, IV.3: "Old men forget; yet all shall be forgot, / But he'll remember with advantages / What feats he did that day." Gower, III.6: such fellows "learn you by rote where services were done... and what a beard of the general's cut and a horrid suit of the camp will do among foaming bottles."
   - What the action does: Pistol provides the parody at once: "patches will I get unto these cudgell'd scars, / And swear I got them in the Gallia wars" (V.1). The play foresees the corruption of its own story and puts it in the mouth of its worst liar.

8. **Mercy is a policy, chosen because it wins.**
   - Spoken: Henry, III.6: "when lenity and cruelty play for a kingdom, the gentler gamester is the soonest winner"—said immediately after approving Bardolph's hanging: "We would have all such offenders so cut off."
   - What the action does: The drunk who railed against the king is pardoned in II.2 so that the traitors can be told "The mercy that was quick in us but late, / By your own counsel is suppress'd and kill'd." Harfleur is spared, the prisoners are not. Each act of mercy or severity is right by its own lights and each is also a calculation; the play never shows mercy done at a cost to the one who grants it.

9. **A friend's treason taints every virtue afterward, because the traitor looked exactly like a good man.**
   - Spoken: Henry, II.2, to Scroop: "Show men dutiful? / Why, so didst thou. Seem they grave and learned? / Why, so didst thou... And thus thy fall hath left a kind of blot / To mark the full-fraught man and best indued / With some suspicion."
   - What the action does: The exposure is staged—papers handed out, faces watched, "Why, how now, gentlemen! / What see you in those papers?"—and the condemned thank God for being caught. The king who complains that appearances can no longer be trusted has just used appearances to trap three men. He is right about the blot and he is one of its makers.

10. **Those who speak most of their courage have the least; the empty vessel makes the greatest sound.**
   - Spoken: the Boy, III.2: "three such antics do not amount to a man." IV.4: "I did never know so full a voice issue from so empty a heart." The Dauphin, III.7, on his horse: "my horse is my mistress." The Constable, IV.2: "A very little little let us do, / And all is done."
   - What the action does: The French are beaten by men they called "island carrions." Pistol takes a prisoner who surrenders to his noise. But the king too notices the vice in himself—"forgive me, God, / That I do brag thus! This your air of France / Hath blown that vice in me" (III.6)—and the boast about the slave who sleeps better than a king is made by a man who will not sleep.

11. **Between people who do not share a language, love is a negotiation already concluded elsewhere.**
   - Spoken: Katharine, V.2: "les langues des hommes sont pleines de tromperies." Henry: "I know no ways to mince it in love, but directly to say, 'I love you'"; "I love France so well that I will not part with a village of it, I will have it all mine." Katharine: "Dat is as it shall please le roi mon père."
   - What the action does: Before the wooing, Henry has named her "our capital demand, compris'd / Within the fore-rank of our articles." The "plain soldier" delivers the longest and most fluent speech of courtship in the play while claiming he has "no cunning in protestation." When she says kissing before marriage is not the custom of France, he answers "nice customs curtsy to great kings... We are the makers of manners." The kiss is taken, the treaty is signed, and the French king's last word is a prayer that the marriage end the wars; the Epilogue says it did not.

12. **The good found in bad things is found by a man who needs everything to be of use.**
   - Spoken: Henry, IV.1: "There is some soul of goodness in things evil, / Would men observingly distil it out... Thus may we gather honey from the weed, / And make a moral of the devil himself."
   - What the action does: This is the habit of the whole character—the wild youth used as foil, the tennis balls turned to gun-stones, the enemy made "our outward consciences." The play admires it and shows its limit: the one thing he cannot turn to use is his father's crime, and his prayer admits it.

13. **A people is a set of quarrelling nations held together by a cause, and the cause is what keeps them from each other's throats.**
   - Spoken: Canterbury, I.2, on the bees: "many things, having full reference / To one consent, may work contrariously." Macmorris, III.2: "What ish my nation? Ish a villain, and a bastard, and a knave, and a rascal? What ish my nation?" Bates, IV.1: "Be friends, you English fools, be friends. We have French quarrels enough."
   - What the action does: Welsh, Irish, Scots and English captains nearly fight each other before Harfleur; Fluellen beats Pistol into eating a leek after the victory. The unity the bishop describes is real on the field and unstable everywhere else.

## What it teaches

The play gives you a man who succeeds at everything, and then shows you, piece by piece, what he does to succeed. He asks whether his war is just in words that make the bishop responsible; he tells Harfleur that its raped daughters will be its own fault; he says a king is only a man while wearing another man's cloak, and lets a Welshman take the blow he promised to take himself; he orders the prisoners killed before he has the excuse Gower supplies for him. None of this is hidden. The text lays the order and the excuse side by side and does not comment. If you admire Henry, the play will not stop you; if you do not, it has given you everything you need.

What the king himself cannot distil into use is the thing he prays about at the bottom of the night. The crown was stolen; he has reburied Richard, paid five hundred poor to pray, built two chantries, and knows that "penitence comes after all." This is the most honest moment he has, and it is also private and in the dark. The victory that follows he gives to God with such insistence—death to any who boast—that you may wonder whether a man so careful to refuse credit is also refusing blame.

The hardest thing the play leaves you is the disproportion between the speech and the list. "We band of brothers... this day shall gentle his condition" is the finest thing anyone says in it. "None else of name; and of all other men / But five and twenty" is the record of what the words meant. Then the Epilogue, in fourteen lines, loses France. A play that asked you at the start to "piece out our imperfections with your thoughts" ends by telling you that your thoughts were the only place the glory ever lived whole.

## Preoccupations

- responsibility shifted onto others: bishops, enemies, the enemy's own choices
- the king as a man and as a ceremony
- the gap between the speech and the list of the dead
- mercy and cruelty as calculated policy
- bragging, counterfeit soldiers, and the decay of legend into lies
- the inherited guilt of a stolen crown
- language, translation, and what is lost or forced across it
- the common soldier's soul and the king's duty
- imagination asked to supply what reality lacks
