# As You Like It

## What happens

Two households have gone wrong in the same way. Duke Frederick has usurped his elder brother Duke Senior, who now lives in the Forest of Arden with a band of loyal lords; Oliver de Boys keeps his younger brother Orlando "rustically at home" and plots his death. Orlando wins a wrestling match before the court, and he and Rosalind, Duke Senior's daughter, fall in love at sight. Within a day Frederick banishes Rosalind, and she flees to Arden disguised as a boy, "Ganymede," with her cousin Celia and the fool Touchstone; Orlando flees there too with the old servant Adam, who gives him his life savings. In the forest the exiled Duke keeps a philosophical court, the melancholy Jaques moralizes, shepherds love unrequitedly, and Orlando hangs bad verses to Rosalind on trees. Rosalind, still as Ganymede, offers to "cure" Orlando of love by having him woo her as if she were Rosalind, which she is. Phoebe the shepherdess falls for Ganymede; Touchstone courts the goatherd Audrey; Oliver arrives, is saved by Orlando from a lioness, converts on the spot, and falls for Celia. Rosalind, promising to make "all this matter even," reveals herself, and four couples marry under Hymen. News comes that Frederick, marching on the forest, met a hermit and renounced the world, restoring the dukedom. Jaques declines the dance and goes to find the convert. Rosalind speaks the epilogue.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Adversity can be made sweet by the style in which it is received; but a style is not a conviction.**
   - Where it is spoken: Duke Senior, II.1: "Sweet are the uses of adversity, / Which, like the toad, ugly and venomous, / Wears yet a precious jewel in his head"; "Finds tongues in trees, books in the running brooks, / Sermons in stones, and good in everything." Amiens: "Happy is your grace, / That can translate the stubbornness of fortune / Into so quiet and so sweet a style."
   - What the action does with it: Amiens's word "translate" is exact. The Duke's contentment is real and his exile really is less perilous than "the envious court." But the moment the dukedom is offered back (V.4) he takes it without a pause: "Meantime, forget this new-fall'n dignity, / And fall into our rustic revelry." The forest was a way of bearing loss, not a preference for it. The song in II.7 says the same thing more sharply: winter's tooth "is not so keen" as "man's ingratitude"; the woods are good because they are not people.

2. **A life is a sequence of parts, and the last part is nothing.**
   - Where it is spoken: Jaques, II.7: "All the world's a stage, / And all the men and women merely players"; "Last scene of all... Is second childishness and mere oblivion, / Sans teeth, sans eyes, sans taste, sans everything."
   - What the action does with it: The speech ends and Orlando enters "bearing Adam," a man near fourscore who has just given away his savings, walked till he dropped, and been carried to food. The stage direction answers the speech. Adam's age is "a lusty winter, / Frosty but kindly" (II.3). Jaques is not wrong about bodies; he is wrong that a body is all there is at the end. The play puts the counterexample on stage without a word of argument.

3. **Love is a madness; nobody has ever died of it; and the person who says so is the one most infected.**
   - Where it is spoken: Rosalind, III.2: "Love is merely a madness, and, I tell you, deserves as well a dark house and a whip as madmen do." IV.1: "Men have died from time to time and worms have eaten them, but not for love."
   - What the action does with it: She says it in disguise to the man she loves, having told Celia the same hour that her affection "hath an unknown bottom, like the Bay of Portugal" (IV.1). When Oliver shows her Orlando's bloody napkin she faints, and insists "Counterfeit, I assure you" (IV.3). The play lets her cynicism and her condition stand side by side and does not pick. Her "cure" for love is to be wooed every day under her own name; the physician is the disease.

4. **Lovers' vows are the least reliable of oaths, and the play tests one.**
   - Where it is spoken: Celia, III.4: "the oath of a lover is no stronger than the word of a tapster. They are both the confirmer of false reckonings." Rosalind, IV.1: "men are April when they woo, December when they wed. Maids are May when they are maids, but the sky changes when they are wives."
   - What the action does with it: Orlando breaks his promise to return within an hour. The reason turns out to be a lioness and a brother's life. The one broken vow in the play has the best excuse a vow could have, and Rosalind's bitter forecast is left standing as a forecast. Jaques's parting line to Touchstone, "thy loving voyage / Is but for two months victualled" (V.4), keeps the question open where the text ends: at the wedding, before December.

5. **Being good can make you hated by those who are not; and hatred can end as suddenly as it began.**
   - Where it is spoken: Oliver, I.1: "my soul—yet I know not why—hates nothing more than he. Yet he's gentle, never schooled and yet learned... of all sorts enchantingly beloved." Adam, II.3: "to some kind of men / Their graces serve them but as enemies." Duke Frederick on Rosalind, I.2: "the people praise her for her virtues / And pity her for her good father's sake."
   - What the action does with it: Confirmed at court, where both villains hate the people the world loves and cannot say why. Then the play dissolves both hatreds without earning it: Oliver is saved from a lioness and says "'Twas I; but 'tis not I" (IV.3); Frederick meets "an old religious man" and is "converted / Both from his enterprise and from the world" (V.4), reported in nine lines by a messenger. The play treats envy as a weather that can clear. Whether that is wisdom or wish is the reader's call.

6. **Faithful service is a thing of the old world and the present does not reward it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Orlando, II.3: "O good old man, how well in thee appears / The constant service of the antique world, / When service sweat for duty, not for meed. / Thou art not for the fashion of these times, / Where none will sweat but for promotion." Adam: "Yet fortune cannot recompense me better / Than to die well and not my master's debtor."
   - What the action does with it: Adam gives five hundred crowns and his remaining strength, is carried to the Duke's table, is welcomed, and is never mentioned again. He does not appear in the last three acts. The play honours him in II.7 and then confirms Orlando's line by forgetting him exactly as "the fashion of these times" would.

7. **Kindness is stronger than revenge, when it comes to the moment.**
   - Where it is spoken: Oliver, IV.3: "Twice did he turn his back and purposed so; / But kindness, nobler ever than revenge, / And nature, stronger than his just occasion, / Made him give battle to the lioness."
   - What the action does with it: This is the hinge of the whole resolution: Orlando's choice converts Oliver, Oliver's conversion frees Celia to love him, their wedding gives Rosalind her occasion to end the game. The play is clear that the choice was not automatic ("Twice did he turn his back"). But it also shows kindness's limit in Rosalind, whose charity does not extend to Phoebe: "Sell when you can; you are not for all markets" (III.5).

8. **The fool may say what the wise do; folly is a licence, and "if" is a peacemaker.**
   - Where it is spoken: Touchstone, I.2: "The more pity that fools may not speak wisely what wise men do foolishly." Jaques, II.7: "O that I were a fool! / I am ambitious for a motley coat... Invest me in my motley. Give me leave / To speak my mind." Touchstone, V.4: "Your 'if' is the only peacemaker; much virtue in 'if.'" Duke Senior: "He uses his folly like a stalking-horse, and under the presentation of that he shoots his wit."
   - What the action does with it: The whole plot runs on "if." Rosalind woos Orlando under "an I were your very, very Rosalind" (IV.1) and marries him in a mock ceremony with Celia as priest; the real wedding then repeats the pretend one. Hypothesis turns out to be the safest way to say a true thing. The Duke's answer to Jaques, that a reformed libertine has no right to "cleanse the foul body of th' infected world," is the play's caution: the licence of the fool is not the same as innocence.

9. **Time has no fixed pace; it goes as the person it carries feels it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Orlando, III.2: "There's no clock in the forest." Rosalind: "Time travels in divers paces with divers persons. I'll tell you who time ambles withal, who time trots withal, who time gallops withal, and who he stands still withal." Touchstone's dial, reported by Jaques, II.7: "from hour to hour we ripe and ripe, / And then from hour to hour we rot and rot."
   - What the action does with it: Court time is brutal and brief: banishment "within these ten days," seizure "within this twelvemonth." Forest time is suspended and conversational, until the last act compresses four weddings and a restoration into "tomorrow." The play treats time as a function of place and company rather than a clock, and then, to end, has to invent a god (Hymen) to stop it.

10. **Manners are local; a creed of contentment is possible, but it is spoken by a man who owns nothing.**
    - Where it is spoken: Corin, III.2: "Those that are good manners at the court are as ridiculous in the country as the behaviour of the country is most mockable at the court." And: "I am a true labourer. I earn that I eat, get that I wear, owe no man hate, envy no man's happiness, glad of other men's good, content with my harm."
    - What the action does with it: Touchstone cannot answer Corin except by calling him damned and shallow. Corin's creed is the one unironic statement of how to live in the play. But he is "shepherd to another man" and does "not shear the fleeces that I graze" (II.4); the gentlefolk buy his master's cottage and flock with gold, and at the end they all go back to court. Content, in this play, is for the people who are left behind.

11. **Melancholy can be a possession a person refuses to give up, and the one who refuses to join is granted the clearest sight.**
    - Where it is spoken: Jaques, IV.1: "it is a melancholy of mine own, compounded of many simples, extracted from many objects." Rosalind: "I had rather have a fool to make me merry than experience to make me sad." Jaques, V.4: "I am for other than for dancing measures."
    - What the action does with it: Jaques's blessings on each couple as he leaves are the most accurate forecasts in the play (Orlando "to a love that your true faith doth merit," Touchstone to "wrangling"). He goes to learn from the convertite Duke: "Out of these convertites / There is much matter to be heard and learned." The play does not punish or convert him; it lets him walk off. Comedy here includes someone who declines it, and he is not a fool.

12. **To watch another person's happiness is to feel one's own lack, and pretending stops working.**
    - Where it is spoken: Orlando, V.2: "how bitter a thing it is to look into happiness through another man's eyes!"; "I can live no longer by thinking."
    - What the action does with it: This line ends the game. The wooing-by-proxy that Rosalind has run for three acts is sufficient until Oliver and Celia show what the real thing looks like; then thinking is not enough and Rosalind drops the disguise. The play knows the exact point at which play has to give way.

## What it teaches

The play keeps putting a sentiment next to a body. Jaques says life ends sans everything and Adam is carried in alive; the Duke says adversity is sweet and takes his dukedom back; Rosalind says no one dies of love and faints at a handkerchief. None of these contradictions is argued; they are staged. What you learn, if you are watching the stage rather than listening to the speeches, is that fine talk about the human condition is a way of bearing it, not a description of it, and that the people who talk best are the ones who need the talk most. That is not a cynical lesson. The Duke's style does make exile bearable. Rosalind's wit does make love survivable. The play respects the uses of a well-made sentence and refuses to mistake one for a fact.

The second lesson is in the word "if." Rosalind can only tell Orlando the truth under a hypothesis; Touchstone says seven justices cannot settle a quarrel that one "if" can. The play's method is the hypothetical, and its title says so. You are allowed to try a feeling on under cover before you commit to it in your own name. That is a generous thing to be told, and it is also a warning: the mock marriage in IV.1 is binding in every way but the one that counts, and Orlando learns that thinking is not living.

What the play does not resolve is what happens after. Every conversion is sudden and unexplained; every marriage is a day old; the one man who has actually lived a long life of service drops out of the story. Jaques's exit is the text's own acknowledgment that the dance does not include everybody, and that the person outside it may be seeing something the dancers are not. Corin's creed of contentment is the only philosophy in Arden that is not undercut, and it belongs to someone the comedy leaves in the field. You are left holding a wedding and a forecast of December. As you like it.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between a fine sentiment and the body or fact standing next to it
- brothers who hate each other for no reason they can name
- adversity made bearable by style, and the limits of that
- love as a madness that its sharpest critics have caught
- disguise and hypothesis as the only safe way to tell the truth
- time felt differently by different people; the forest without a clock
- the fool's licence; who is allowed to say what
- the one who declines the comedy and is not wrong
