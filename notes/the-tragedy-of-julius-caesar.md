# The Tragedy of Julius Caesar

## What happens
Rome is celebrating Caesar's triumph over Pompey's sons, and two tribunes are already pulling the decorations off his statues. Cassius, who cannot bear to be "in awe of such a thing as I myself," works on Brutus, Caesar's friend, until Brutus persuades himself that Caesar must die not for what he is but for what crowning "might" make him. The conspirators kill Caesar in the Senate on the Ides of March, bathe their arms in his blood, and declare liberty. Brutus, overruling Cassius at every turn, spares Antony, lets him speak at the funeral, and leaves the Forum before he does; Antony turns the crowd with the corpse, the mantle, and the will, and the mob tears a poet apart for having the wrong name. The conspirators flee; Antony, Octavius, and Lepidus sit down to prick names for death. In camp at Sardis, Brutus and Cassius quarrel bitterly, reconcile, and learn that Portia has killed herself; Caesar's ghost visits Brutus. At Philippi, Brutus again overrules Cassius on strategy. Cassius, misreading the battlefield, has his servant kill him on his birthday; Titinius dies on the same sword; Brutus, beaten, runs on his own, saying he kills himself with better will than he killed Caesar. Antony pronounces him "the noblest Roman of them all," and Octavius takes his servants into service.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **People insist they are masters of their own fates, and the play shows them undone by what they cannot see.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cassius, 1.2: "Men at some time are masters of their fates: / The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings."
   - *What the action does with it:* The man who says this dies by his own hand on a misreading. Cassius sends Titinius to check whether distant troops are friend or enemy, Pindarus reports him "ta'en" when he has in fact been crowned with a victory garland by friends, and Cassius has himself killed: "Mistrust of my success hath done this deed" (5.3). Messala names the mechanism: "O hateful Error, Melancholy's child! / Why dost thou show to the apt thoughts of men / The things that are not?" The play does not say the stars rule; it says that the self, which Cassius trusts, is exactly where the fault lies, and that "my sight was ever thick" is true of everyone in it.

2. **A man can talk himself into killing a friend by reasoning from what the friend might become rather than what he is.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Brutus, 2.1: "I know no personal cause to spurn at him, / But for the general"; "since the quarrel / Will bear no colour for the thing he is, / Fashion it thus"; "think him as a serpent's egg / Which hatch'd, would, as his kind grow mischievous; / And kill him in the shell."
   - *What the action does with it:* Brutus admits in the same speech that "I have not known when his affections sway'd / More than his reason." He kills on a hypothesis. The action then produces the thing the hypothesis feared, only without Caesar: proscriptions, "an hundred Senators" dead (4.3), Antony cutting legacies from the will he had just used to inflame the crowd (4.1), and Octavius closing the play. The crowd's first response to Brutus's justification is "Let him be Caesar" (3.2). The serpent's egg hatches anyway.

3. **Those who want to kill a spirit and not a body find that the body is what the living respond to, and the spirit outlives the killing.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Brutus, 2.1: "Let us be sacrificers, but not butchers... We all stand up against the spirit of Caesar, / And in the spirit of men there is no blood."
   - *What the action does with it:* The killing is a butchery; they bathe "up to the elbows" (3.1). Antony then uses nothing but the body: the mantle, each rent in it, "sweet Caesar's wounds, poor poor dumb mouths" (3.2). The spirit, meanwhile, returns as a ghost at Sardis and Philippi, and Brutus concedes at Cassius's corpse, "O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! / Thy spirit walks abroad, and turns our swords / In our own proper entrails" (5.3). The play reverses Brutus's hope exactly: the blood was easy to shed and the spirit impossible to kill.

4. **Honour can be worked on like a metal, and the honourable man is the easiest to seduce because you need only tell him what he already believes about himself.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cassius, 1.2: "Thy honourable metal may be wrought / From that it is dispos'd: therefore 'tis meet / That noble minds keep ever with their likes; / For who so firm that cannot be seduc'd?"
   - *What the action does with it:* Cassius forges letters "as if they came from several citizens" and Brutus reads them as Rome's petition (2.1). Antony then does the same thing in reverse: "Brutus is an honourable man" repeated until the word means traitor (3.2). Yet the play does not make Brutus a fool. Each of his ruinous decisions (no oath, spare Antony, let him speak, march to Philippi) is made on honourable grounds and overrides Cassius, who is right each time; Cassius finally says so: "This tongue had not offended so today, / If Cassius might have rul'd" (5.1). Honour is real in Brutus, and it is the lever by which he is moved and the cause of his defeat.

5. **Crowds respond to names, bodies, and money, not to reasons, and they can be made to want the opposite thing within an hour.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Casca, 1.2: "the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre"; Marullus, 1.1: "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!"
   - *What the action does with it:* The citizens who cheered Pompey cheer Caesar (1.1); after Brutus's prose argument they cry "Let him be Caesar"; after Antony's verse, the mantle, and "seventy-five drachmas," they cry "Revenge... let not a traitor live" (3.2). Then they tear Cinna the poet apart: "It is no matter, his name's Cinna; pluck but his name out of his heart" (3.3). Cassius had argued that "Brutus" and "Caesar" are only names that weigh the same; the mob proves that a name is enough to kill for.

6. **The person who proclaims himself unchangeable is the most exposed to change, and the proclamation itself can be the moment of ruin.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Caesar, 3.1: "I am constant as the northern star, / Of whose true-fix'd and resting quality / There is no fellow in the firmament"; and 1.2: "for always I am Caesar."
   - *What the action does with it:* Casca stabs him on the next line. Before that, the play has shown Caesar with a deaf ear, "the falling-sickness," a swoon in the market-place, a wife who talks him out of going, and a flatterer who talks him back in: Decius knows that "when I tell him he hates flatterers, / He says he does, being then most flattered" (2.1). Caesar speaks of himself in the third person as if he were already a monument. The action makes a double point: his constancy was a performance, and yet after death it becomes true; dead Caesar is the one fixed thing in the second half of the play.

7. **Men philosophize about death bravely and then find that what they said is not what they do.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Caesar, 2.2: "Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once." Brutus, 3.1: "That we shall die, we know; 'tis but the time / And drawing days out, that men stand upon." Brutus, 5.1, on Cato's suicide: "I do find it cowardly and vile."
   - *What the action does with it:* Casca turns Brutus's maxim at once into a joke that excuses murder: "he that cuts off twenty years of life / Cuts off so many years of fearing death." Brutus condemns suicide in the morning and runs on his sword by nightfall, with a new argument ready: "It is more worthy to leap in ourselves / Than tarry till they push us" (5.5). The play does not mock the maxims; it shows that a maxim is adopted to fit the hour.

8. **People read signs according to their wishes, and the same sign can be made to mean anything.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cicero, 1.3: "men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves."
   - *What the action does with it:* This is the play's most thoroughly confirmed line. Casca reads the storm as divine anger; Cassius reads it as heaven's endorsement (1.3). Calphurnia's dream of the bleeding statue is turned by Decius into "a vision fair and fortunate" (2.2). Brutus reads Antony as a reveller who "can do no more than Caesar's arm / When Caesar's head is off" (2.1). Cassius, having "held Epicurus strong," decides to "partly credit things that do presage" on the day he dies (5.1). Then the catastrophe at Philippi is literally a misconstruction of what Titinius's horsemen were doing. Cicero, who said it, is proscribed and killed offstage.

9. **Friendship between proud men survives only by one of them yielding, and the quarrel is often about something other than what is said.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Brutus, 4.2: "When love begins to sicken and decay / It useth an enforced ceremony." Cassius, 4.3: "A friend should bear his friend's infirmities; / But Brutus makes mine greater than they are."
   - *What the action does with it:* The quarrel in the tent is nominally about bribes and gold; it turns out Brutus has been holding Portia's death in silence through the whole of it ("No man bears sorrow better. Portia is dead"). Cassius offers his breast and dagger ("Strike as thou didst at Caesar") and Brutus yields with a joke about Cassius's mother. The play lets the reconciliation be real and yet also lets us see that it is Cassius who always gives way, and that his giving way on strategy minutes later ("Then, with your will, go on") is what kills them both.

10. **A man can keep a Roman face so well that even his grief must be performed twice.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Brutus, 2.1: "Let not our looks put on our purposes, / But bear it as our Roman actors do, / With untired spirits and formal constancy." Brutus, 2.1: "the state of man, / Like to a little kingdom, suffers then / The nature of an insurrection."
   - *What the action does with it:* Brutus tells Cassius flatly that Portia "swallow'd fire," then minutes later lets Messala break the same news as if fresh and answers, "Why, farewell, Portia. We must die, Messala," so that Messala can admire it: "Even so great men great losses should endure" (4.3). Cassius sees through it: "I have as much of this in art as you, / But yet my nature could not bear it so." The play shows us the mask and the face in the same scene and does not tell us which is Brutus.

11. **Plain reasons lose to a story told over a body; the speaker who disclaims eloquence is the one who has it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Antony, 3.2: "I am no orator, as Brutus is; / But, as you know me all, a plain blunt man... I only speak right on."
   - *What the action does with it:* Brutus argues in balanced prose ("as he was ambitious, I slew him") and asks for a reply; Antony withholds the will, descends to the corpse, names each wound, and only then reads the bequests. The citizens move from "Caesar was a tyrant" to "We will be revenged" inside one speech. Antony's private comment afterwards is the play's own gloss: "Now let it work. Mischief, thou art afoot, / Take thou what course thou wilt!" The man who says he has no "power of speech / To stir men's blood" is the only one in Rome who does.

12. **The winners of a civil war are not the better men; they are the ones who treat other men as property.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Antony on Lepidus, 4.1: "Do not talk of him / But as a property"; "This is a slight unmeritable man, / Meet to be sent on errands."
   - *What the action does with it:* The first thing the triumvirs do is trade relatives for death ("Your brother too must die; consent you, Lepidus?") and cut "some charge in legacies" from the will Antony had read aloud to the people. Octavius crosses Antony in the field with "I do not cross you; but I will do so" (5.1). Antony's eulogy for Brutus is generous and true, but it is Octavius who gets the last word, takes Brutus's servants, and calls it "this happy day." The play grants Brutus the nobility and the losers the honour, and gives the world to the man who speaks least.

## What it teaches

The hardest thing this play asks you to hold is that Brutus is both genuinely the "noblest Roman" and the cause of every disaster in it. He is not corrupt, not ambitious, not deceived about his own motives in any crude way. He reasons in the orchard with real care and arrives at murder; he spares Antony out of a refusal to be a butcher and so hands Rome to a butcher; he lets Antony speak because truth should be able to bear a hearing, and he insists on Philippi because he has a theory about tides. Every time the practical man, Cassius, is right, and every time the honourable man overrules him. If you want a lesson, it is that virtue is not a guide to consequences, and that the people most confident in their own honesty ("I am arm'd so strong in honesty, / That they pass by me as the idle wind") are the people least able to hear a warning. But the play refuses to turn this into cynicism, because it also shows that no one was ever false to Brutus, and that his servants will hold the sword for him and his enemies will bury him with honour. Being right about yourself and wrong about the world is a fully human condition, and the play does not resolve it.

The second lesson is about seeing. Almost no one in this play perceives what is in front of them. Caesar does not hear the soothsayer twice, does not read Artemidorus, is deaf in one ear. Brutus cannot see his own face "but by reflection," and the reflection Cassius offers is forged. The crowd sees a name and kills a poet. Cassius sees fire in his tents and friends taken, and he is wrong. Cicero's one real line, that men construe things after their fashion, is the nearest thing to a thesis in the play, and the dramatist proves it by having both parties read the same lightning as a sign in their favour. The practical counsel this leaves you with is uncomfortable: your interpretation of events is a product of your wishes and your melancholy, and the moment of greatest conviction ("I am constant as the northern star") may be the moment just before the knife.

What is unresolved is whether Caesar needed killing at all. The play gives no evidence that he would have been a tyrant and plenty that he was vain, frail, and flattered. It also gives no evidence that Rome was better off; the "liberty" cried in the Senate turns into proscription lists within two scenes. The conspirators' deed produces the Caesar they feared, in the plural. And yet the play does not say that nothing should have been done, because the same crowd that cheers Pompey and then Caesar would clearly have cheered a crown. It leaves you where Brutus leaves Volumnius: with a world that "goes" as it goes, and the question of what a decent man does inside it, answered only by the fact that Brutus's friends stayed true and his enemies called him a man.

- "Th' abuse of greatness is, when it disjoins / Remorse from power." (Brutus, 2.1)
- "Between the acting of a dreadful thing / And the first motion, all the interim is / Like a phantasma, or a hideous dream." (Brutus, 2.1)
- "O conspiracy... Hide it in smiles and affability." (Brutus, 2.1)
- "when I tell him he hates flatterers, / He says he does, being then most flattered." (Decius, 2.1)

- "Your wisdom is consum'd in confidence." (Calphurnia, 2.2)
- "That every like is not the same, O Caesar, / The heart of Brutus yearns to think upon." (Brutus, 2.2)
- "The evil that men do lives after them, / The good is oft interred with their bones." (Antony, 3.2)
- "It is no matter, his name's Cinna; pluck but his name out of his heart." (Fourth Citizen, 3.3)

## Preoccupations

- the gap between what people say about themselves and what they do
- how honourable motives produce disastrous outcomes
- misreading: people construe signs, faces, and events according to their wishes
- the crowd as a thing that responds to names, bodies, and money rather than reasons
- the spirit of the dead outliving and overpowering the living
- friendship between proud men, and who yields
- public mask versus private self; stoic performance of grief
- how a killing done for liberty produces a harder tyranny
