# The Merchant of Venice

## What happens

Antonio, a Venetian merchant sad for no reason he can name, borrows three thousand ducats from the Jewish moneylender Shylock so that his friend Bassanio can go woo the heiress Portia at Belmont. Shylock, long spat on by Antonio, proposes "in a merry sport" that the forfeit be a pound of Antonio's flesh; Antonio, confident his ships will come home, agrees. In Belmont Portia is bound by her dead father's will to marry whichever suitor picks the right one of three caskets; Morocco chooses gold and Arragon silver, and both lose; Bassanio chooses lead and wins her. Meanwhile Shylock's daughter Jessica elopes with the Christian Lorenzo, taking his money and jewels, and Antonio's ships are reported wrecked. Shylock, embittered by the loss of his daughter and the mockery of the Christians, refuses repayment and insists on the bond. Portia, disguised as a young doctor of laws, first pleads for mercy, then grants Shylock his literal bond and no more: flesh but not blood, exactly a pound, under pain of death; then invokes an alien statute against his life, and the Duke and Antonio spare him on condition he convert and leave his estate to Lorenzo and Jessica. The disguised wives extract their husbands' rings as fees, return home ahead of them, accuse them of faithlessness, and then reveal the trick. Antonio's ships turn out to be safe. Shylock does not reappear.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Knowing what is good and doing it are different faculties, and the second is the rarer.**
   - Where it is spoken: Portia, I.2: "If to do were as easy as to know what were good to do, chapels had been churches, and poor men's cottages princes' palaces... I can easier teach twenty what were good to be done than to be one of the twenty to follow mine own teaching."
   - What the action does with it: The play confirms the remark against its speaker. The same Portia who later preaches "The quality of mercy is not strain'd" (IV.1) proceeds to deliver "justice more than thou desir'st," strips Shylock of his goods, and stands by while his conversion is made a condition of pardon. The Duke asks "How shalt thou hope for mercy, rend'ring none?" and then renders a mercy that takes everything the man lives by. Nerissa's "They would be better if well followed" (I.2) hangs over the court scene.

2. **Cruelty is taught, and the wronged learn to copy their wrongers exactly.**
   - Where it is spoken: Shylock, III.1: "The villainy you teach me I will execute, and it shall go hard but I will better the instruction." Antonio has already set the terms in I.3: "I am as like to call thee so again, / To spet on thee again, to spurn thee too."
   - What the action does with it: Confirmed in both directions. Shylock's insistence on his bond ("since I am a dog, beware my fangs," III.3) is modelled on the contempt he has received. Then the court out-Shylocks Shylock: Portia's "Thou shalt have justice more than thou desir'st" and Gratiano's "Now, infidel, I have you on the hip" (IV.1) echo Shylock's own "If I can catch him once upon the hip" (I.3). Each side reproduces the other's hardness and calls it justice.

3. **People choose by outward show and are deceived by it.**
   - Where it is spoken: The casket scrolls, II.7: "All that glisters is not gold... Gilded tombs do worms infold"; Bassanio, III.2: "So may the outward shows be least themselves. / The world is still deceiv'd with ornament."
   - What the action does with it: The caskets confirm it neatly: Morocco chooses what "many men desire," Arragon what he "deserves," and both get a death's-head or a fool's head. But the play complicates the lesson. Bassanio, who wins by rejecting ornament, is a man who has "disabled mine estate / By something showing a more swelling port / Than my faint means would grant continuance" (I.1) and woos on borrowed money. And the plot's rescue depends on an outward show: Portia's lawyer's gown. Antonio says of Shylock, "O, what a goodly outside falsehood hath!" (I.3); the line could be turned on half the cast.

4. **Love is measured by what one is willing to hazard, and such hazards compete.**
   - Where it is spoken: The lead casket, II.7: "Who chooseth me must give and hazard all he hath." Antonio, I.1: "My purse, my person, my extremest means / Lie all unlock'd to your occasions." Bassanio in court, IV.1: "life itself, my wife, and all the world, / Are not with me esteem'd above thy life."
   - What the action does with it: Antonio does hazard his flesh, and the play honours it. But Portia, standing by in disguise, hears her husband rank her below his friend ("Your wife would give you little thanks for that / If she were by to hear you make the offer"), and the ring plot of Act V is her way of making him feel the cost of that. The resolution is not that friendship yields to marriage but that Antonio is made "surety" a second time (V.1: "I dare be bound again, / My soul upon the forfeit"). The two loves are left yoked, not reconciled.

5. **Strict law destroys, but what saves Antonio is not mercy; it is a sharper reading of the same law.**
   - Where it is spoken: Bassanio, IV.1: "To do a great right, do a little wrong." Portia: "It must not be, there is no power in Venice / Can alter a decree established." Shylock: "I stand here for law."
   - What the action does with it: Portia refuses to bend the law and instead reads it more literally than Shylock did: "This bond doth give thee here no jot of blood." Shylock's own instrument is turned on him, and then a statute he did not know of is produced against his life. The mercy speech fails to move anyone; literalism wins. The play shows that a legal system can be made to produce any outcome by whoever reads it most cleverly, and leaves it to the reader whether that is justice.

6. **Some sadness has no reason a person can find, and no plot cures it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Antonio, I.1: "In sooth I know not why I am so sad... And such a want-wit sadness makes of me, / That I have much ado to know myself." And: "I hold the world but as the world, Gratiano, / A stage, where every man must play a part, / And mine a sad one."
   - What the action does with it: Never explained. Salarino guesses merchandise, then love; Antonio denies both. Solanio's report, II.8, that "he only loves the world for him" (Bassanio) is the nearest the play comes. In court Antonio calls himself "a tainted wether of the flock, / Meetest for death" (IV.1). At the end, among three married couples, his reply to the news that his ships are safe is "I am dumb." He is given back his living and remains alone.

7. **Loss of money and loss of a child can be felt in the same breath, and onlookers will mock the mixture.**
   - Where it is spoken: Solanio reporting Shylock, II.8: "My daughter! O my ducats! O my daughter! / Fled with a Christian! O my Christian ducats!" Shylock himself, III.1: "It was my turquoise, I had it of Leah when I was a bachelor. I would not have given it for a wilderness of monkeys."
   - What the action does with it: The play gives us Shylock's grief only through hostile reporters, and then gives him a line about his dead wife's ring that no reporter could have invented. He also says "I would my daughter were dead at my foot, and the jewels in her ear." Both are true of him. The Christians laugh; the text does not quite let the reader.

8. **An oath sworn absolutely will be broken for a good reason, and the person who swore will still have broken it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Bassanio, III.2: "when this ring / Parts from this finger, then parts life from hence." Nerissa, V.1: "Though not for me, yet for your vehement oaths, / You should have been respective and have kept it."
   - What the action does with it: Bassanio gives up the ring at Antonio's urging ("Let his deservings and my love withal / Be valued 'gainst your wife's commandment," IV.1), out of gratitude, shame, and courtesy. Portia's verdict is "Even so void is your false heart of truth." Then she forgives him, but only because she herself engineered the breach and holds the ring. Her joke on his swearing "by thine own fair eyes, / Wherein I see myself" ("Swear by your double self, / And there's an oath of credit," V.1) is the play's judgment: oaths are only as good as the self that swears them, and that self is double.

9. **Nothing is good in itself; value depends on season, setting, and comparison.**
   - Where it is spoken: Portia, V.1: "Nothing is good, I see, without respect"; "The crow doth sing as sweetly as the lark / When neither is attended"; "How many things by season season'd are / To their right praise and true perfection!" Nerissa, I.2: "they are as sick that surfeit with too much as they that starve with nothing."
   - What the action does with it: The whole fifth act moves from the daylight court to moonlit Belmont and re-prices everything: a "paltry ring" becomes a marriage, music heard in silence sounds sweeter, a good deed "shines in a naughty world." But the same relativity worked in the court: a pound of flesh was worth nothing "but to bait fish withal," and then worth a man's life. The play does not decide whether this is wisdom or merely the privilege of those who end up on top.

10. **Children do not inherit their parents' manners, and fathers rarely know their children.**
    - Where it is spoken: Launcelet, II.2: "it is a wise father that knows his own child." Jessica, II.3: "though I am a daughter to his blood, / I am not to his manners." II.5: "I have a father, you a daughter, lost."
    - What the action does with it: Jessica's flight is played as comedy and rewarded with Shylock's estate. But in V.1 she and Lorenzo trade examples of betrayed lovers, and her contribution is "Did young Lorenzo swear he loved her well, / Stealing her soul with many vows of faith, / And ne'er a true one." She also says, "I am never merry when I hear sweet music." The play lets her escape and does not promise that she is happy.

11. **A claim to shared humanity can be granted in speech and denied in law.**
    - Where it is spoken: Shylock, III.1: "Hath not a Jew eyes?... If you prick us, do we not bleed?"
    - What the action does with it: In court the distinction the speech collapses is restored by statute: the bond gives "no jot of blood," and "One drop of Christian blood" would forfeit everything; then "If it be proved against an alien / That by direct or indirect attempts / He seek the life of any citizen..." (IV.1). The play lets the speech stand unanswered and then shows the state answering it anyway, with a law that treats the alien as a different kind of person.

12. **Pleasure is keener in the chase than in the having.**
    - Where it is spoken: Gratiano, II.6: "All things that are, / Are with more spirit chased than enjoy'd."
    - What the action does with it: Bassanio wins Portia and in the same scene receives the letter that sends him away from her unbedded. Portia prays "O love, be moderate; allay thy ecstasy" (III.2) before the news comes. The play's joys are repeatedly cut short in the instant of achievement, as if to confirm Gratiano's cynicism, though it ends with the couples going in to bed.

## What it teaches

The play puts the finest speech about mercy in the English language into the mouth of someone who does not then show it, and it does this without comment. That is the central difficulty. Portia's plea is sincere, and it is true, and it moves nobody in the room; what moves the room is a trick about blood. If you want to believe that mercy and justice were reconciled in Venice, the text will not help you. The Christians get their friend back by being cleverer at the law than the man they call a dog, and then they call the pardon they grant him "the difference of our spirit." You are left holding Shylock's question, "What judgment shall I dread, doing no wrong?", and the play's answer, which is that he is an alien and the rule is different for him.

What the play does know, and shows with a steadiness that does not depend on whose side you take, is that hardness is contagious. Antonio spits; Shylock whets his knife; Gratiano crows "a halter gratis." Everyone's cruelty is a quotation. The one person who tries to step outside the chain, Antonio offering to lend "to thine enemy" so the penalty can be exacted with "better face," is the one who ends up on the block. There is no suggestion that this is a world where the humane choice is rewarded on its own; Antonio's ships come back by "strange accident," not by desert.

Against this the play sets Belmont, where value is a matter of lighting and comparison and a ring can be lost and found in one night. It is tempting to read the fifth act as healing. It is better to read it as a demonstration of how quickly the winners move on. Jessica is not merry when she hears music; Antonio is dumb; Shylock has left the stage sick. The couples go in. The lesson, if there is one, is that comedy is a point of view, and that the people whose story has ended well are not well placed to judge whether it ended justly.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between what people preach and what they do
- cruelty learned by imitation; revenge as a copy of the original wrong
- the deceptiveness of outward show, and the usefulness of disguise
- law as a weapon that cuts whoever holds it least cleverly
- friendship and marriage competing for the same loyalty
- the outsider denied the rights he is told he shares
- sadness without a cause
- value as a matter of comparison, timing, and point of view
