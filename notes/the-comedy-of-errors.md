# The Comedy of Errors

## What happens

An old Syracusan merchant, Egeon, is condemned to die at sundown in Ephesus for the crime of being a Syracusan, unless he can raise a thousand marks; he tells the Duke how a shipwreck split his family in two decades ago, leaving him one twin son and one twin servant, and how he has spent five years searching for the others. Unknown to him, both sets of twins are in Ephesus that same day: one Antipholus and Dromio have lived there for years, the others have just landed. Through one afternoon, wives, goldsmiths, courtesans, officers and servants deliver messages, money, chains and dinner invitations to the wrong man, each twin concluding that the town is bewitched or that everyone else is mad. The Ephesian husband is locked out of his own house, arrested for a debt he never incurred, declared possessed and bound in a cellar; the Syracusan stranger is fed, wooed, given gold, and flees into an abbey. At five o'clock the Duke, the condemned father, the two pairs of twins and the abbess (who turns out to be Egeon's lost wife) converge, the errors are laid side by side, the death sentence is quietly dropped, and the family goes in to a feast.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A person is not self-contained; who you are depends on being recognised by others.**
   - *Where spoken:* Antipholus of Syracuse, I.2: "I to the world am like a drop of water / That in the ocean seeks another drop, / Who, failing there to find his fellow forth, / Unseen, inquisitive, confounds himself. / So I, to find a mother and a brother, / In quest of them, unhappy, lose myself." Adriana uses the same figure of marriage, II.2: "as easy mayst thou fall / A drop of water in the breaking gulf, / And take unmingled thence that drop again ... As take from me thyself, and not me too."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play literalises the metaphor. The Syracusan does "lose himself": a whole town knows him by a name and history that are not his, and he has no way to refute it ("Known unto these, and to myself disguis'd!" II.2). He recovers himself only when the man he is being mistaken for stands beside him. The Duke's question at the end, "which is the natural man, / And which the spirit? Who deciphers them?" (V.1), is answered not by either twin's self-assertion but by the mother's story. Identity, in this play, is settled by witnesses, not by the self.

2. **Madness is the name a community gives to behaviour that does not fit its story about you.**
   - *Where spoken:* Dromio of Ephesus, II.1: "sure he's stark mad." Courtesan, IV.3: "Now, out of doubt Antipholus is mad." Pinch, IV.4: "both man and master is possess'd, / I know it by their pale and deadly looks." Antipholus of Ephesus, IV.4: "Peace, doting wizard, peace; I am not mad." The Duke, V.1: "I think you all have drunk of Circe's cup."
   - *What the action does with it:* Nobody in the play is mad, yet the sanest and most consistent man in it, Antipholus of Ephesus, is bound and thrown into "a dark and dankish vault" by his own wife on the word of a quack, because every honest witness contradicts him. The Duke, hearing all the testimony, cannot decide and is reduced to "I think you are all mated, or stark mad." The play shows that being right is no protection when all the evidence, honestly given, is against you; sanity is a verdict, not a fact you can carry on your own.

3. **The one who goes along with a confusing world fares better than the one who insists on the truth.**
   - *Where spoken:* Antipholus of Syracuse, II.2: "Until I know this sure uncertainty / I'll entertain the offer'd fallacy," and "I'll say as they say, and persever so, / And in this mist at all adventures go."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Syracusan twin, who accepts a free dinner, a free gold chain and a wife he does not have, is fed, enriched, and gets the sister; the Ephesian twin, who at every turn says exactly what happened to him, is locked out, arrested, bound and gnaws through his ropes. The play's comic logic rewards accommodation and punishes accuracy, and does not apologise for it. The truth, when it comes, comes from outside both men.

4. **Counsel of patience comes most easily from those who are not in pain.**
   - *Where spoken:* Adriana, II.1: "A wretched soul bruis'd with adversity, / We bid be quiet when we hear it cry; / But were we burd'ned with like weight of pain, / As much, or more, we should ourselves complain." And: "They can be meek that have no other cause."
   - *What the action does with it:* Luciana has just preached that "A man is master of his liberty" and "Ere I learn love, I'll practise to obey." Within the hour she is being wooed by the man she takes to be her sister's husband and is far from serene about it; she runs to report it (IV.2). Her later defence of Adriana against the Abbess ("She never reprehended him but mildly," V.1) is the play's quiet concession that Adriana's charge was fair: theory is cheap until it is tested.

5. **Law is rigid toward strangers and dissolves once the stranger is known.**
   - *Where spoken:* The Duke, I.1: "I am not partial to infringe our laws ... we may pity, though not pardon thee," and "passed sentence may not be recall'd / But to our honour's great disparagement."
   - *What the action does with it:* At the end Antipholus of Ephesus offers the ducats to ransom his father and the Duke waves it off: "It shall not need, thy father hath his life" (V.1). Nothing in the statute has changed; what has changed is that Egeon is now the father of a man the Duke has "been patron to" for twenty years. The unbendable law bends the moment the condemned acquires a social identity. The play lets this pass as happy and does not remark on it.

6. **Reputation is a kind of credit, and like money credit it can be called in and collapse in an afternoon.**
   - *Where spoken:* Balthasar, III.1: "slander lives upon succession, / For ever hous'd where it gets possession." Angelo, IV.1: "Consider how it stands upon my credit," "This touches me in reputation." Angelo, V.1, of Antipholus of Ephesus: "Of very reverend reputation, sir, / Of credit infinite, highly belov'd" — spoken in the same breath as "most dishonestly he doth deny it."
   - *What the action does with it:* A chain of obligations (the Merchant presses Angelo, Angelo presses Antipholus) turns a single misdelivered necklace into an arrest, a drawn sword and a charge of perjury. The town's most respected man loses his standing between dinner and five o'clock, on the honest testimony of honest people. The play shows how belief in a person is a network, and how one bad link pulls on all the others.

7. **Servants absorb the blows for their masters' errors.**
   - *Where spoken:* Dromio of Syracuse, II.2: "Was there ever any man thus beaten out of season, / When in the why and the wherefore is neither rhyme nor reason?" Dromio of Ephesus, IV.4: "I have served him from the hour of my nativity to this instant, and have nothing at his hands for my service but blows." And II.1 to Adriana: "Am I so round with you, as you with me, / That like a football you do spurn me thus?"
   - *What the action does with it:* Every beating in the play falls on a Dromio, and every one is for a mistake made by a master or a mistress. Antipholus of Syracuse strikes his own servant for a conversation held with the other servant. The play ends with the two Dromios going out "hand in hand, not one before another," an equality the brothers above them never articulate. The comedy counts the cost and leaves it with the people who paid it.

8. **A home is a fiction kept up by recognition; a door can unmake a husband.**
   - *Where spoken:* Antipholus of Ephesus, III.1: "What art thou that keep'st me out from the house I owe?" and "Since mine own doors refuse to entertain me, / I'll knock elsewhere, to see if they'll disdain me." Earlier in the scene: "welcome more common, for that's nothing but words."
   - *What the action does with it:* The master of the house is turned from his own door by his own wife because another man wearing his face is inside. His response is to dine with a courtesan and give her the chain meant for his wife "be it for nothing but to spite my wife." The lock-out is the hinge of the plot: ownership and marriage are shown to be things the household confers, not things a man possesses in himself.

9. **Jealousy wounds the jealous first, and a true suspicion is still poison.**
   - *Where spoken:* Luciana, II.1: "Self-harming jealousy! fie, beat it hence." Adriana, II.1: "What ruins are in me that can be found / By him not ruin'd?" The Abbess, V.1: "The venom clamours of a jealous woman / Poisons more deadly than a mad dog's tooth."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Abbess's diagnosis is delivered with great authority and is factually wrong: the "madness" she explains was caused by mistaken identity, not by nagging. Yet Adriana's suspicion is not groundless either; her husband does go to the courtesan and does plan to buy a rope's end for her. Adriana's reply, "She did betray me to my own reproof," accepts a lesson the audience knows was built on a false premise. The play complicates both the wife's grievance and the moralist's cure, and ends by restoring the marriage without resolving the quarrel.

10. **Time disfigures and parents cannot always be known by their children, but the damage can be partly reversed by telling the whole story.**
   - *Where spoken:* Egeon, V.1: "careful hours with time's deformed hand, / Have written strange defeatures in my face," and "Not know my voice! O time's extremity, / Hast thou so crack'd and splitted my poor tongue / In seven short years that here my only son / Knows not my feeble key of untun'd cares?" The Abbess: "Thirty-three years have I but gone in travail / Of you, my sons."
   - *What the action does with it:* Egeon's bitter lament is addressed to the wrong son, who really never knew him. Time is blamed for a failure of recognition that time did not cause. What finally restores the family is not any single person's memory but the assembling of everyone's partial account; the Duke says "here begins his morning story right." Lost years are not returned, but the narrative is, and the play treats that as enough.

## What it teaches

The play keeps asking a question it never lets a character answer alone: who am I, if everyone around me agrees I am someone else? Its answer, acted out rather than stated, is uncomfortable. You are, for all practical purposes, who the people around you take you to be. Antipholus of Ephesus has twenty years of reputation, a wife, a house, a servant, and a scar from saving the Duke's life, and none of it survives one afternoon in which honest witnesses, each telling the truth as they saw it, assemble a picture of him as a lunatic debtor. Nobody lies to bring him down. That is the frightening part, and it is what the farce is built on. The play does not say the world is malicious; it says the world is made of partial views, and that you can be destroyed by their sum.

Against that, the play offers two consolations, and both are modest. One is accommodation: the Syracusan brother, who decides to "entertain the offer'd fallacy," comes through the day fed, paid and in love, while his brother who insists on the facts comes through it bound and bitten. The lesson is not that truth doesn't matter but that in a confused world the person who waits and goes along often suffers less than the one who fights for an account nobody will accept. The other consolation is narrative: everything is set right in the last scene not by any one person being proved correct but by all the stories being told in the same place at the same time. Recognition is a collective act.

What the play leaves unresolved is the marriage. Adriana's jealousy is called poison by the one figure with moral authority on stage, and in the same scene it is shown to have been well-founded. Her husband went to another woman, planned to beat her, and is never asked to account for it; the sentence of the Abbess falls on the wife. The feast at the end covers this over. A thoughtful reader will notice that the play restores the household exactly as it was, faults included, and that the only people who leave the stage as equals are the two servants who were beaten all day for other men's mistakes.

## Preoccupations

- identity as something given by other people's recognition, not held by the self
- the gap between what a person knows happened and what everyone else believes
- madness as a social verdict rather than a state of mind
- marriage as a quarrel over liberty and jealousy that is restored but not resolved
- law and mercy, and how the law softens once the stranger becomes a known person
- servants paying for their masters' mistakes
- time as the thing that separates families and disfigures faces
- the home and the door: belonging as something that can be withdrawn
