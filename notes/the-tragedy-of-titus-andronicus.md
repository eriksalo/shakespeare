# The Tragedy of Titus Andronicus

## What happens

A Roman general comes home from ten years of war against the Goths with his captives and the coffins of his sons. His surviving sons demand a prisoner to sacrifice at the tomb; he gives them the captive queen's eldest boy and ignores her plea for mercy. Offered the empire by the people, he hands it instead to the late emperor's unpleasant elder son, Saturninus, who promptly marries the captive queen, Tamora. From that point the play is one long answer to the sacrifice. Tamora's two sons, coached by her lover Aaron, kill the emperor's brother, rape Titus's daughter Lavinia, and cut out her tongue and cut off her hands so she cannot name them; Aaron frames two of Titus's sons for the murder, and tricks Titus into cutting off his own hand as a ransom, sending back the hand with his sons' heads. Lavinia, using a book and a staff held in her mouth, writes the rapists' names in sand. Titus feigns madness, Tamora comes to him dressed as the figure of Revenge, and he kills her sons, bakes them in a pie, serves it to her, kills Lavinia, kills Tamora, is killed by the emperor, who is killed by Titus's last son Lucius. Lucius, returning with a Gothic army, is made emperor, buries Aaron alive, and throws Tamora's body to the birds.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Mercy is what makes a person like a god, and the people who say so are the first to refuse it when they get the power.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Tamora, I.1: "Wilt thou draw near the nature of the gods? / Draw near them then in being merciful. / Sweet mercy is nobility's true badge." Lavinia to Tamora, II.3: "O, be to me, though thy hard heart say no, / Nothing so kind, but something pitiful"; "For my father's sake, / That gave thee life when well he might have slain thee, / Be not obdurate." Tamora: "Even for his sake am I pitiless."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play is built on one refused plea answering another. Titus refuses Tamora's; Tamora refuses Lavinia's, in nearly the same words, and tells her sons "The worse to her, the better loved of me." Everything that follows is the cost of the first refusal. The claim about mercy is confirmed entirely by its absence: no one in the play is merciful, and the result is a table of corpses and a pie. The one who preached it is the one who most enjoys withholding it.

2. **"Roman" and "barbarous" name the same act depending on who is doing it; piety and cruelty are one deed.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lucius, I.1: "See, lord and father, how we have performed / Our Roman rites. Alarbus' limbs are lopped." Tamora: "O cruel, irreligious piety!" Chiron: "Was never Scythia half so barbarous!" Marcus to Titus, I.1: "Thou art a Roman; be not barbarous." Titus, III.1: "Rome is but a wilderness of tigers."
   - *What the action does with it:* Rome commits the first atrocity in the name of religion; the Goths answer it; Titus answers that with a revenge he explicitly measures against myth ("worse than Procne I will be revenged," V.2). By the end a Gothic army restores Rome and a Roman emperor buries a man alive. The play does not let "civilized" and "barbarian" keep their places for a single scene.

3. **Justice has left the earth; the suppliant finds that stones are better listeners than magistrates.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Titus, III.1: "I tell my sorrows to the stones... A stone is soft as wax, tribunes more hard than stones; / A stone is silent, and offendeth not, / And tribunes with their tongues doom men to death." Titus, IV.3: "Terras Astraea reliquit"; "sith there's no justice in earth nor hell, / We will solicit heaven." Publius, IV.3: "If you will have Revenge from hell, you shall. / Marry, for Justice, she is so employed." Marcus, IV.1: "O, why should nature build so foul a den, / Unless the gods delight in tragedies?"
   - *What the action does with it:* The tribunes literally pass by while he lies in the dust. The arrows to the gods come down in the emperor's courtyard. The messenger to Jupiter is a clown with pigeons who is hanged for carrying a letter. What arrives, when something finally does, is Revenge, played by Tamora in costume. The play confirms Titus's diagnosis completely: there is no justice in it from start to finish, only revenge, and the last sentences passed by the new emperor are revenge too.

4. **Grief that cannot be spoken burns inward, and grief beyond reason cannot be bound by reason.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Marcus, II.4: "Sorrow concealed, like an oven stopped, / Doth burn the heart to cinders where it is." Titus, III.1: "If there were reason for these miseries, / Then into limits could I bind my woes... I am the sea." Titus, III.1, after the heads arrive: "Ha, ha, ha!... Why, I have not another tear to shed." Young Lucius, IV.1: "Extremity of griefs would make men mad."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play's central image is a woman with no tongue and no hands. It then shows her finding a way to say what happened: she turns the pages of Ovid to the tale of Philomel and writes the rapists' names with a staff in her mouth (IV.1). Titus had promised to "wrest an alphabet" from her signs (III.2), and does. Grief does find speech, and as soon as it does it turns into a plan. The laugh at the end of III.1 is the hinge: the point where tears stop and revenge begins.

5. **Pity and vengeance are the same impulse, and a single word can turn one into the other.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Titus, III.2, when Marcus kills a fly: "How if that fly had a father and mother? / How would he hang his slender gilded wings / And buzz lamenting doings in the air!" Marcus: "'twas a black ill-favoured fly, / Like to the empress' Moor." Titus: "Give me thy knife, I will insult on him... There's for thyself, and that's for Tamora."
   - *What the action does with it:* This is the only moment in the play where anyone pities a creature for its own sake, and it reverses in one line. The same man who cannot bear the death of a fly stabs the dead fly for resembling an enemy. The play is showing you the machinery: compassion for one's own is identical in substance to hatred of the other, and needs only a label to switch.

6. **Being able to read is not the same as understanding what you read; the mutilated can write and the educated can miss the point.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Chiron, IV.2, reading the Horace wrapped round Titus's gift of weapons: "O, 'tis a verse in Horace; I know it well. / I read it in the grammar long ago." Aaron, aside: "Now, what a thing it is to be an ass!... The old man hath found their guilt, / And sends them weapons wrapped about with lines, / That wound, beyond their feeling, to the quick." Titus, IV.1: "I will go get a leaf of brass, / And with a gad of steel will write these words."
   - *What the action does with it:* The crime is discovered through a book (Ovid) and a line of writing in sand; the criminals are told they are discovered by a line of Latin and cannot see it. Aaron, who sees everything, carves letters into dead men's skins ("Let not your sorrow die, though I am dead," V.1). Literacy in this play belongs to everyone and insight to almost no one.

7. **The only parent who protects a child at cost to himself is the play's self-declared devil; the pious father kills two of his own.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lavinia to Chiron, II.3: "When did the tiger's young ones teach the dam? / O, do not learn her wrath; she taught it thee; / The milk thou suck'st from her did turn to marble." Aaron, IV.2: "My mistress is my mistress; this my self... This maugre all the world will I keep safe." Aaron, V.1: "Touch not the boy, he is of royal blood"; he trades his whole confession for an oath that the child will live.
   - *What the action does with it:* Titus stabs Mutius in I.1 for barring his way and kills Lavinia in V.3 on a precedent supplied by his enemy. Tamora sends her own newborn to be killed. Aaron kills the nurse, arranges a substitute baby, and carries his son toward the Goths to raise him "to be a warrior and command a camp." The play gives its one unconditional act of parental love to the man who says "If one good deed in all my life I did, / I do repent it from my very soul" (V.3), and does not resolve the contradiction.

8. **A man can hold evil as a creed and be truthful about it, while everyone around him lies in the name of virtue.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Aaron, III.1: "Let fools do good, and fair men call for grace, / Aaron will have his soul black like his face." Aaron, IV.2: "is black so base a hue?... Coal-black is better than another hue / In that it scorns to bear another hue." Aaron, V.1, on why he can extract an oath from Lucius: "An idiot holds his bauble for a god, / And keeps the oath which by that god he swears."
   - *What the action does with it:* Aaron's confession in V.1 is the only full and accurate account of events anyone gives in the play. Saturninus calls Titus "father of my life" and disowns him within forty lines (I.1); Tamora promises reconciliation while planning "to massacre them all" (I.1); Titus feigns madness to lure her. Aaron's cynicism about oaths is a correct prediction (Lucius keeps the oath and the child lives) and also an observation that belief binds people even when it is being used against them. The play's honest man is its monster, and its account of color is put in the mouth of the only character who says what he is.

9. **Everyone dissembles, and the one who dissembles best is the one who seems to have gone mad.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Tamora, I.1, aside: "Dissemble all your griefs and discontents... I'll find a day to massacre them all." Tamora, V.2: "I am Revenge, sent from th' infernal kingdom." Titus, V.2, aside: "I knew them all, though they suppose me mad, / And will o'erreach them in their own devices." Titus, IV.3: "there's no justice in earth nor hell."
   - *What the action does with it:* The feigner of madness beats the feigner of allegory at her own game. The play treats disguise as a universal condition rather than a villain's trick: Tamora calls herself a Roman, Saturninus calls Titus his father, Lucius calls the Goths his "faithful friends," Titus calls himself a cook. Marcus's "He takes false shadows for true substances" (III.2) is wrong; Titus sees the substances very well.

10. **Revenge repays the crime with interest, and the cycle stops only when there is no one left to continue it.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Titus, V.2: "worse than Philomel you used my daughter, / And worse than Procne I will be revenged." Lucius, V.3: "There's meed for meed, death for a deadly deed." Marcus, V.3: "let me teach you how to knit again / This scattered corn into one mutual sheaf, / These broken limbs again into one body."
    - *What the action does with it:* The deaths in V.3 take six lines. Marcus's speech about knitting Rome together is given over a table with a cannibal pie on it, and the new emperor's first two acts are to bury a man breast-deep and starve him and to throw a woman's corpse to the birds. Marcus asks the crowd "Have we done aught amiss?" and offers to leap from the wall if they say yes; the crowd says "all hail." The play ends the cycle not by resolving it but by running out of people, and it leaves the last judgment to the reader rather than the Romans.

11. **The ingratitude of a state toward its servant is real, and the servant helped choose his own tormentor.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Titus, I.1: "Rome, I have been thy soldier forty years... And buried one and twenty valiant sons." Titus, III.1: "For all my blood in Rome's great quarrel shed... Be pitiful to my condemned sons." Titus, IV.3: "I made thee miserable / What time I threw the people's suffrages / On him that thus doth tyrannize o'er me." First Goth, V.1: "Ingrateful Rome requites with foul contempt."
    - *What the action does with it:* Rome's ingratitude is shown in full, but so is its origin: the people chose Titus, Titus overrode them for Saturninus, and Saturninus is the one who sends back the hand. The grievance is just, and the grieved man wrote the first line of it himself.

12. **A story can be used as a precedent for anything, including killing your own daughter.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Titus, V.3: "Was it well done of rash Virginius / To slay his daughter with his own right hand, / Because she was enforced, stained, and deflowered?" Saturninus: "Because the girl should not survive her shame, / And by her presence still renew his sorrows." Titus: "A pattern, precedent, and lively warrant / For me, most wretched, to perform the like."
    - *What the action does with it:* Titus takes a ruling from his enemy and a precedent from a tale and kills Lavinia with it, the daughter whose signs he had spent an act learning to read. She is given no say and the play gives her none. Ovid supplied the crime's model (Philomel), Virginius supplies the daughter's death, Procne the revenge: the characters are living inside books and treating them as law. The play does not comment. It moves straight to the pie.

## What it teaches

This is a play in which every atrocity is committed under a principle. Alarbus is hewn as a religious rite; Mutius is killed for honor; the clown is hanged by law; Lavinia is killed on a precedent; Tamora comes to the door as Revenge in person. The one character who acts without a principle is Aaron, and he is the only one who tells the truth about what has happened. The hard lesson is not that principles are bad but that the words for them ("piety," "mercy," "Roman," "justice") are available to anyone and bind no one; Tamora can preach mercy in Act I and refuse it in Act II with the same breath. If you want to know what a person is, the play suggests, look at what they do when the plea is in front of them, not at what they said when they were the one pleading.

The second thing the play knows is what grief does when it has no outlet. Lavinia with no tongue and no hands is the play's whole argument in one body: the first question is not whether the wrong will be avenged but whether it can even be said. The answer is yes, slowly, through a book and a staff, and the moment it is said the play changes from lament to plot. Titus's laugh at the end of III.1 is the sound of that change. The play does not pretend the plot that follows is justice; it calls it Revenge, lets Tamora wear the costume, and has the man who serves the pie say he is outdoing Procne. What it does insist is that silence was worse.

What it leaves unresolved is the ending. Lucius is hailed emperor by "the common voice," the same voice Titus threw away in the first scene; his first acts are two more cruelties; a boy stands weeping over a grandfather who killed two of his own children; and Aaron, buried to the chest, has the last unrepentant word. Marcus asks the Romans whether the Andronici have done anything wrong, and the Romans say no. A reader is not obliged to agree. The play has spent five acts showing that the people who say "we are the just ones" are always the next to do harm, and it ends by letting the survivors say exactly that. Whether you take the final "all hail" as a healing or as the start of the next cycle is the question the play hands you and does not answer.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between what people say and what they do (mercy preached, mercy refused)
- revenge as a cycle that repays wrong with interest and cannot stop itself
- grief that cannot speak, and what it does to find a voice
- whether justice exists anywhere, on earth or above it
- "Roman" versus "barbarian" as labels that swap sides
- parents who kill their children, and the one parent who will not
- books, writing, and stories used as both evidence and license
- ingratitude of the state toward the soldier who served it
- the body cut apart: hands, tongues, heads, as the play's literal subject
