# A Midsummer Night's Dream

## What happens

Theseus, Duke of Athens, is four days from marrying Hippolyta, whom he conquered in war. Egeus demands that his daughter Hermia marry Demetrius or die under Athenian law; she loves Lysander, and the two flee into the wood at night, telling Helena, who loves Demetrius and is scorned by him, and who betrays the plan to him in the hope of a kind look. In the wood the fairy king and queen, Oberon and Titania, are at war over a changeling boy, and their quarrel has thrown the seasons out of joint. Oberon has Puck fetch a flower whose juice makes the sleeper love the first creature seen on waking; he uses it on Titania, who wakes to dote on Bottom the weaver, whom Puck has given an ass's head in the middle of a rehearsal for a play to be performed at the Duke's wedding. Puck anoints the wrong Athenian, so both young men abandon Hermia and pursue Helena, who takes it as mockery; the two women turn on each other and the two men go off to fight. Puck leads the men apart in a fog, undoes the charm on Lysander, and the four wake in the morning paired off; Theseus overrides Egeus and the couples marry together. Oberon, having got the boy, releases Titania. The workmen perform Pyramus and Thisbe before the court, who heckle it; the fairies bless the house at midnight, and Puck asks pardon if the shadows have offended.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Love is not a judgement of the object; it is something that happens to the eye.**
   - *Where spoken:* Helena, I.1: "Things base and vile, holding no quantity, / Love can transpose to form and dignity. / Love looks not with the eyes, but with the mind; / And therefore is wing'd Cupid painted blind." Theseus, V.1: "the lover, all as frantic, / Sees Helen's beauty in a brow of Egypt."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play takes Helena's figure and makes it mechanical: a juice on the eyelids, and the next creature seen is loved. Titania wakes and loves an ass; Lysander wakes and loves Helena; neither has changed their mind, only their sight. The play confirms that love does not track merit and corrects Helena on where it lives: not in the mind but in the eye, where it can be altered from outside without the lover knowing.

2. **Lovers describe an arbitrary change of desire as the arrival of reason.**
   - *Where spoken:* Lysander, II.2, newly enchanted: "The will of man is by his reason sway'd, / And reason says you are the worthier maid. / Things growing are not ripe until their season; / So I, being young, till now ripe not to reason." Demetrius, IV.1: "my love to Hermia, / Melted as the snow, seems to me now / As the remembrance of an idle gaud ... But, as in health, come to my natural taste."
   - *What the action does with it:* Both speeches are made under the flower. Demetrius's "natural taste" is a charm that is never lifted; the marriages at the end rest on an enchantment still in force. The play gives its most confident language about reason to the characters who have the least of it, and lets the audience know.

3. **The law that cannot be bent is bent as soon as the arrangement of desires makes it convenient.**
   - *Where spoken:* Theseus, I.1: "the law of Athens yields you up / (Which by no means we may extenuate) / To death, or to a vow of single life." And: "To you your father should be as a god ... To whom you are but as a form in wax / By him imprinted, and within his power / To leave the figure, or disfigure it."
   - *What the action does with it:* In IV.1, with Demetrius now wanting Helena, Theseus says "Egeus, I will overbear your will" and marries everyone. Nothing in the law has changed; Egeus still begs "the law, the law upon his head." The play shows law and paternal right as things that hold only while they align with what the powerful lover happens to want.

4. **The crosses of love are expected to come from outside; the one that actually comes is the lover changing.**
   - *Where spoken:* Lysander, I.1: "The course of true love never did run smooth," listing blood, years, friends, war, death, sickness; "So quick bright things come to confusion." Hermia: "let us teach our trial patience, / Because it is a customary cross."
   - *What the action does with it:* None of Lysander's listed obstacles strikes in the wood. What strikes is Lysander himself, who within an hour calls Hermia "you Ethiope," "thou cat, thou burr," "you dwarf." Hermia's question, "Am not I Hermia? Are not you Lysander? / I am as fair now as I was erewhile" (III.2), is the play's real cross: the beloved is the same and the lover is not. He never apologises; the matter is simply forgotten at dawn.

5. **A long friendship between women is torn in a night by the men's shifting, and is not shown mended.**
   - *Where spoken:* Helena, III.2: "So we grew together, / Like to a double cherry, seeming parted, / But yet a union in partition ... And will you rent our ancient love asunder, / To join with men in scorning your poor friend? / It is not friendly, 'tis not maidenly."
   - *What the action does with it:* The quarrel sinks to "painted maypole," "you minimus," "my nails can reach unto thine eyes." Helena's last words to Hermia are "I will not trust you, I." In IV.1 the women wake, say a few lines about dreaming, and go to the temple; they do not speak to each other again in the play. The men's quarrel is dissolved by Puck; the women's is left where it fell.

6. **Being unloved teaches a person to abase herself and then to disbelieve love when it comes.**
   - *Where spoken:* Helena, II.1: "I am your spaniel; and, Demetrius, / The more you beat me, I will fawn on you. / Use me but as your spaniel, spurn me, strike me, / Neglect me, lose me; only give me leave, / Unworthy as I am, to follow you." And: "We cannot fight for love as men may do. / We should be woo'd, and were not made to woo."
   - *What the action does with it:* When both men do woo her, she can only hear mockery: "you all are bent / To set against me for your merriment." She wins Demetrius, but by a charm and not by any change in herself, and her last word on him is "Mine own, and not mine own" (IV.1). The play gives her what she wanted and lets her feel that it is not hers.

7. **Quarrel at the top disorders everything below, and peace is made by one party being tricked into yielding.**
   - *Where spoken:* Titania, II.1: "the spring, the summer, / The childing autumn, angry winter, change / Their wonted liveries; and the mazed world, / By their increase, now knows not which is which. / And this same progeny of evils comes / From our debate, from our dissension; / We are their parents and original."
   - *What the action does with it:* The remedy is not reconciliation but humiliation: Oberon makes Titania dote on a monster, mocks her, and takes the boy while she is enchanted ("I then did ask of her her changeling child; / Which straight she gave me," IV.1). She wakes, loathes what she loved, and asks "Tell me how it came this night"; the play does not show him telling her. The seasons are restored on these terms. Order in the play's world is bought by a husband's trick.

8. **Each level of the world watches the level below it as a comedy and does not see itself.**
   - *Where spoken:* Puck, III.2: "Lord, what fools these mortals be!" and "those things do best please me / That befall prepost'rously." Oberon: "Shall we their fond pageant see?" Theseus, V.1: "The best in this kind are but shadows; and the worst are no worse, if imagination amend them." Hippolyta: "It must be your imagination then, and not theirs."
   - *What the action does with it:* The fairies watch the lovers as sport; the lovers watch the workmen's Pyramus and Thisbe as sport and heckle a story of two lovers dying by a misunderstanding in a wood at night, having just spent a night in a wood misunderstanding. Puck's epilogue, "If we shadows have offended," addresses the audience as one more tier. Nobody in the play laughs at themselves; the play arranges it so that the audience can.

9. **Reason's confident account of the night is wrong, and the audience knows it.**
   - *Where spoken:* Theseus, V.1: "I never may believe / These antique fables, nor these fairy toys. / Lovers and madmen have such seething brains ... How easy is a bush supposed a bear?" Hippolyta: "But all the story of the night told over, / And all their minds transfigur'd so together, / More witnesseth than fancy's images, / And grows to something of great constancy."
   - *What the action does with it:* The fairies are real in the play, have been seen, and are about to bless Theseus's own bed. His speech on imagination is eloquent and mistaken; Hippolyta's objection, that a shared account counts for more than private fancy, is the one the play bears out. The most powerful man on stage is the one who knows least about what happened.

10. **What a person has actually experienced outruns what they can say about it.**
   - *Where spoken:* Bottom, IV.1: "I have had a most rare vision. I have had a dream, past the wit of man to say what dream it was ... The eye of man hath not heard, the ear of man hath not seen, man's hand is not able to taste, his tongue to conceive, nor his heart to report, what my dream was." Demetrius, IV.1: "These things seem small and undistinguishable, / Like far-off mountains turnèd into clouds."
   - *What the action does with it:* The one mortal who met the fairies waking is the one with least language for it, and he does not try to explain; he commissions a ballad. The lovers agree to "recount our dreams" and are never shown doing so. The play treats the gap between the night and the morning's account of it as permanent.

11. **The plainest truth about love is spoken by the man with the ass's head.**
   - *Where spoken:* Bottom, III.1, to Titania who has just declared love: "reason and love keep little company together nowadays. The more the pity that some honest neighbours will not make them friends."
   - *What the action does with it:* Titania replies, "Thou art as wise as thou art beautiful," which is true of neither and is the play's joke. But Bottom is also the only character who is courteous to everyone he meets, who asks the fairies' names and inquires after their parents, and who is unafraid. His wish for "a peck of provender" while a queen offers jewels is the clearest head in the wood.

12. **Generosity toward the humble is professed by the powerful and then not practised.**
   - *Where spoken:* Theseus, V.1: "never anything can be amiss / When simpleness and duty tender it," and "Love, therefore, and tongue-tied simplicity / In least speak most to my capacity." Hippolyta: "I love not to see wretchedness o'ercharged, / And duty in his service perishing."
   - *What the action does with it:* Theseus then jeers at every line of the workmen's play, and the lovers with him. Hippolyta calls it "the silliest stuff that ever I heard" and then "Beshrew my heart, but I pity the man." The workmen, for their part, are not wounded; Bottom corrects the Duke from the stage ("No, in truth, sir, he should not"). The play lets the gap between professed and practised courtesy stand, and gives the dignity to the mocked.

13. **Marriages in this play begin in a man's victory.**
   - *Where spoken:* Theseus, I.1: "Hippolyta, I woo'd thee with my sword, / And won thy love doing thee injuries; / But I will wed thee in another key."
   - *What the action does with it:* Oberon wins Titania back by a trick; Demetrius is given Helena by a drug; Lysander keeps Hermia because the drug is removed. No woman in the play chooses her ending. Hippolyta, whose consent is never shown, is the coolest voice at the feast. The play's blessing on the bride-beds ("the issue there create / Ever shall be fortunate") is spoken by the fairy who obtained his own peace by enchanting his wife.

## What it teaches

The play is honest about love in a way that the lovers never are. It shows desire as something done to the eye, from outside, while the person it is done to explains the change as growth, ripeness, reason, recovered health. If you have ever heard someone justify a change of heart as finally seeing clearly, this play has already written the speech, and given it to a man with flower juice on his eyelids. What is hard is that the play does not punish this. Demetrius's charm is never removed. The marriages are made, the Duke blesses them, the fairies bless the beds. The play suggests that a good ending can rest on a fiction nobody examines, and that this is how most good endings are made.

It is also a play about watching. Every group in it is someone else's comedy: the fairies laugh at the lovers, the lovers laugh at the workmen, and Puck turns at the end and reminds the audience that they too have been watching shadows. The lesson in that is not that laughter is cruel, though the play shows it can be; it is that none of the watchers sees that they are also watched. The four lovers, who spent a night in a wood misreading each other, heckle Pyramus and Thisbe for misreading a mantle. Theseus, who explains the night away as imagination, will have fairies in his bedroom within the hour. The one character who has nothing to be ashamed of is Bottom, who met a queen and asked for hay, and who, waking from the strangest thing that happened to anyone, has the sense not to explain it.

What is left unresolved is the women. Helena gets her man by a drug and knows it is "mine own, and not mine own." Hermia is called a dwarf and an Ethiope by the man she will marry in the morning and he never takes it back. The two friends who grew "like to a double cherry" part with "I will not trust you" and do not speak again. Titania gives up the child she had promised to a dying friend to raise, and is never told how she lost him. The play ends in blessing, and the blessings are real, and a careful reader will notice who paid for them.

## Preoccupations

- desire as something that happens to the eye, not a judgement of the beloved
- the lover's habit of calling a change of heart "reason"
- law and a father's right bending to whatever the powerful lover wants
- friendship between women broken by men and not repaired
- rulers' quarrels disordering the world, and peace bought by a trick on the wife
- each tier of the world watching the one below as a comedy
- imagination and reason, and reason being the one that is wrong
- experience that outruns anyone's ability to tell it
- marriage founded on a man's victory
