# The Tempest

## What happens
A ship carrying the King of Naples, his brother Sebastian, his son Ferdinand, the usurping Duke of Milan Antonio, and the old counsellor Gonzalo is wrecked in a storm that turns out to be the work of Prospero, the true Duke of Milan, who was put to sea in a rotten boat twelve years earlier with his infant daughter Miranda and has ruled a small island since with the help of books, the spirit Ariel, and the enslaved islander Caliban. Over about four hours Prospero separates the castaways and works on each group: Ferdinand is led to Miranda, tested with log-carrying, and betrothed to her; the king's party is marched about, shown a vanishing banquet, and accused by Ariel as a harpy; Antonio tempts Sebastian to murder the sleeping king and Ariel wakes them; Caliban recruits the drunken butler Stephano and the jester Trinculo to kill Prospero and is hunted by spirit-dogs. Prospero presents a wedding masque, breaks it off when he remembers the plot, abjures his magic, forgives everyone, reveals Ferdinand and Miranda at chess, reclaims his dukedom, frees Ariel, and in an epilogue asks the audience to release him with their applause.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Rank has no purchase on the elements, and the person who says so is the one doing the actual work.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Boatswain: "What cares these roarers for the name of king? ... You are a counsellor: if you can command these elements to silence, and work the peace of the present, we will not hand a rope more" (1.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* The storm is Prospero's art, so the "roarers" are not indifferent at all — they are under the command of a man who was a duke. The play half-retracts the boatswain's point in the next scene, then confirms it in another key: Alonso, Antonio, and Sebastian are helpless on the island exactly because their titles mean nothing there ("This cell's my court: here have I few attendants, / And subjects none abroad", 5.1). Power on the island is knowledge, not rank.

2. **Unbounded trust creates the betrayal it did not expect, and the betrayed man knows his own share in it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Prospero: "my trust, / Like a good parent, did beget of him / A falsehood in its contrary as great / As my trust was; which had indeed no limit, / A confidence sans bound" (1.2); and "I, thus neglecting worldly ends ... in my false brother / Awak'd an evil nature" (1.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* Prospero tells the story with his own negligence in it, which is unusual for a wronged man. But the play does not let the self-blame excuse Antonio: Miranda's "Good wombs have borne bad sons" (1.2) puts it back on nature, and Antonio's conduct on the island — tempting Sebastian to kill a sleeping king within hours of being saved from drowning — shows a man whose appetite needs no neglectful brother to feed it.

3. **A lie told long enough becomes the liar's belief, and conscience is a thing some people simply do not have.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Prospero on Antonio: "like one / Who having into truth, by telling of it, / Made such a sinner of his memory, / To credit his own lie, he did believe / He was indeed the Duke" (1.2). Antonio on conscience: "I feel not / This deity in my bosom: twenty consciences / That stand 'twixt me and Milan, candied be they / And melt ere they molest!" (2.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* Antonio is the test case and he never fails the description. Forgiven by Prospero at the end ("I do forgive / Thy rankest fault, all of them"), he does not reply; his only remaining line is a joke about whether Caliban is "marketable" (5.1). Gonzalo's "all of us ourselves, / When no man was his own" is spoken in Antonio's hearing, and the play gives no sign he is included. Forgiveness is given and not received, and the play lets that stand without comment.

4. **Evil spreads by precedent: one usurpation teaches the next.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Sebastian: "Thy case, dear friend, / Shall be my precedent: as thou got'st Milan, / I'll come by Naples" (2.1). Antonio: "what's past is prologue, what to come / In yours and my discharge" (2.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* The play stages three plots to seize the island or a crown in parallel — Antonio's against Prospero (past), Antonio and Sebastian's against Alonso, Caliban, Stephano and Trinculo's against Prospero — and lets the low plot parody the high one. Stephano's "Monster, I will kill this man. His daughter and I will be king and queen" (3.2) is Antonio in a jester's coat. The play suggests ambition is a single shape repeated at every level, and that what differs is not the desire but the competence.

5. **The wronged can become the wronger, and the master's "thing of darkness" is his own.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Caliban: "This island's mine, by Sycorax my mother, / Which thou tak'st from me. When thou cam'st first, / Thou strok'st me and made much of me ... and then I lov'd thee" (1.2). Prospero: "this thing of darkness I / Acknowledge mine" (5.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* Prospero, dispossessed of Milan, dispossesses Caliban and justifies it by the attempted rape of Miranda — which Caliban does not deny ("Would 't had been done!"). The play gives both sides their case and settles neither. What it does show is a reversal of Prospero's own prediction: he calls Caliban one "on whose nature / Nurture can never stick" (4.1), and Caliban is the one character who ends the play saying "I'll be wise hereafter, / And seek for grace" (5.1). Antonio, raised in a court, learns nothing; the "born devil" is the one who changes.

6. **Language is a gift that enables cursing, and also longing.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Caliban: "You taught me language, and my profit on 't / Is, I know how to curse" (1.2). Later: "the isle is full of noises, / Sounds, and sweet airs, that give delight, and hurt not ... that, when I wak'd, / I cried to dream again" (3.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* Caliban's own words refute his claim. The most beautiful speech in the play is his, delivered to two drunks who hear in it only "a brave kingdom to me, where I shall have my music for nothing." What language gave him was not only curses but the capacity to want what he cannot have. The play puts it in the mouth of the one it has called a monster.

7. **What people call freedom is usually a change of master.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Ariel: "My liberty" (1.2). Caliban, kneeling to Stephano: "Freedom, high-day! high-day, freedom!" (2.2). Ferdinand, enslaved: "space enough / Have I in such a prison" (1.2); and marrying, "with a heart as willing / As bondage e'er of freedom" (3.1). Miranda: "I'll be your servant, / Whether you will or no" (3.1). Prospero, Epilogue: "release me from my bands."
   - *What the action does with it:* Everyone in the play is in service to someone, and every freedom claimed is a new bondage chosen: Caliban's freedom song is sung while licking a butler's shoe; Ferdinand's prison is his delight; Miranda's independence from her father's precepts is a proposal of marriage; the all-powerful Prospero ends asking the audience to set him free. Only Ariel is let go outright — "to the elements / Be free" (5.1) — and Ariel is not human. The play observes that for humans freedom is a direction, not a state.

8. **Labour is made light or heavy by whom it is for; and easy winning cheapens the prize.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Ferdinand: "some kinds of baseness / Are nobly undergone; and most poor matters / Point to rich ends ... The mistress which I serve quickens what's dead, / And makes my labours pleasures" (3.1). Prospero: "lest too light winning / Make the prize light" (1.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* The log-carrying test works exactly as designed; Prospero: "thou / Hast strangely stood the test" (4.1). The play endorses the idea without irony, but it also shows that the test was rigged by a father who knew the outcome ("It goes on, I see, / As my soul prompts it"). The lovers' freely chosen love is also the plan of the magician watching them. The play does not decide whether that makes it less real.

9. **A perfect commonwealth is imagined by a man who would be its king.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Gonzalo: "no sovereignty" — Sebastian: "Yet he would be King on't." Antonio: "The latter end of his commonwealth forgets the beginning" (2.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* The mockers are villains and they are right. The island has been or would be ruled by Sycorax, by Prospero (through enslavement), by Stephano (through drink), and by Caliban's intended "peopling" of it; Gonzalo's innocent commonwealth is the only version that is never attempted. The play lets the dream be spoken by its best man and refuted by its worst, and gives no third option.

10. **Guilt is a slow poison; some it purges, some it does not touch.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Gonzalo: "their great guilt, / Like poison given to work a great time after, / Now 'gins to bite the spirits" (3.3). Ariel as harpy: "The powers, delaying, not forgetting ... nothing but heart-sorrow, / And a clear life ensuing" (3.3).
   - *What the action does with it:* Alonso hears the thunder "pronounce / The name of Prosper" and repents at once; Sebastian and Antonio draw swords ("But one fiend at a time, / I'll fight their legions o'er"). The same vision, delivered to three men of sin, works on one. The play's theory of conscience is not that it is universal but that it is a capacity, present in Alonso, absent in his brother, and that no amount of staging can supply it where it is missing.

11. **Pity is taught to the human by the non-human, and virtue is the rarer action.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Ariel: "your charm so strongly works 'em, / That if you now beheld them, your affections / Would become tender." Prospero: "Dost thou think so, spirit?" Ariel: "Mine would, sir, were I human." Prospero: "the rarer action is / In virtue than in vengeance" (5.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* The turn from revenge to forgiveness is prompted not by Prospero's reflection but by a spirit of air observing what a human would feel. Prospero's "shall not myself, / One of their kind ... be kindlier mov'd than thou art?" is a man shamed into his own nature. The play places the decisive moral insight outside the human and lets the human catch up.

12. **Life is a pageant that dissolves, and the thought comes when reality interrupts the show.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Our revels now are ended ... We are such stuff / As dreams are made on, and our little life / Is rounded with a sleep" (4.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* The speech is triggered by Prospero remembering, mid-masque, that three men are coming to kill him ("I had forgot that foul conspiracy"). The vision of universal dissolution is a man's response to a lapse in his own vigilance. He is "vex'd," his "old brain is troubled," and the next thing he does is set dogs on the conspirators. The play puts its most serene statement about mortality in the mouth of a man at his least serene, and the audience can see the gap.

13. **Wonder at mankind is a function of not having met much of it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Miranda: "O, wonder! / How many goodly creatures are there here! / How beauteous mankind is! O brave new world / That has such people in 't!" Prospero: "'Tis new to thee" (5.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* The "goodly creatures" she admires include the man who put her in a boat to die and the man who, that morning, tried to murder his sleeping brother. Her father's four words carry the play's whole knowledge of men. Earlier, Gonzalo said the island's spirits were "more gentle, kind, than of / Our human generation," and Prospero, aside: "some of you there present / Are worse than devils" (3.3). The play lets Miranda's wonder stand as true of her and false of them.

14. **The power to compel is finally laid down, and the one who laid it down is left asking for mercy.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "this rough magic / I here abjure ... I'll break my staff ... And deeper than did ever plummet sound / I'll drown my book" (5.1). Epilogue: "Now my charms are all o'erthrown, / And what strength I have's mine own, / Which is most faint ... As you from crimes would pardon'd be, / Let your indulgence set me free."
   - *What the action does with it:* Alonso, in grief, wished to seek his son "deeper than e'er plummet sounded"; Prospero drowns his book at the same depth. The play aligns the renunciation of power with the acknowledgment of loss. Then, in the Epilogue, the man who commanded the sea stands without any means but speech and asks for what he gave Antonio. "Every third thought shall be my grave" (5.1) is the retirement he names. The play's last word on power is that giving it up is the only thing a man can do with it that does not need to be forgiven.

## What it teaches

The play is honest about the fact that a happy ending can be engineered. Everything that happens on the island happens because one man planned it, and he watches it happen and says "It works." That gives the reader a problem the play does not resolve: the love of Ferdinand and Miranda is real to them and a stage-trick to Prospero, and the text never says which description is the right one. If you have ever arranged other people's lives for their good, you know the discomfort; the play lets Prospero feel it only once, in the aside "So glad of this as they, I cannot be, / Who are surpris'd withal" (3.1). The man who controls everything is the one person who cannot be surprised by any of it.

What the play teaches about forgiveness is colder than its reputation. Prospero forgives Antonio and Antonio does not answer. The forgiveness is a decision taken by the strong man for his own sake — "the rarer action is / In virtue than in vengeance" is about what kind of action is rare, not about whether the forgiven party deserves it — and it coexists with a threat held in reserve: "at this time / I will tell no tales." The play suggests that forgiving someone who does not want to be forgiven is possible and necessary, and that it does not change them. It changes you.

The hardest thing in the play is Caliban. The text gives him the island's title, the memory of being loved, the attempted rape, the plot to murder, the loveliest speech, and the only line of repentance from anyone who did wrong. It then gives Prospero the line "this thing of darkness I / Acknowledge mine," which can be read as ownership of a slave or ownership of a part of himself, and the play does not pick. What you are left with is the knowledge that the one Prospero called incapable of nurture is the one who learned, and that the learning may or may not be sincere, and that neither Prospero nor the reader can tell. That is roughly the condition of anyone who has tried to teach anyone anything.

## Preoccupations

- power: who has it, how it is taken, and what it costs to lay it down
- freedom as a change of masters rather than an escape from them
- forgiveness given to those who neither ask for it nor receive it
- whether nurture can change nature, and who turns out teachable
- the engineered happy ending: love and reconciliation as another man's plan
- the gap between innocent wonder at mankind and experienced knowledge of it
- betrayal within families — brother against brother
- mortality and the dissolving of all show
- the island as a test of what men do when there is no law over them
