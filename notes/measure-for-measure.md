# Measure for Measure

## What happens

The Duke of Vienna, having let the city's laws against fornication sleep for fourteen years, hands his power to the austere deputy Angelo and pretends to leave, staying on in disguise as a friar to watch "if power change purpose, what our seemers be." Angelo immediately revives the dead statute and sentences Claudio to death for getting his betrothed Juliet with child. Claudio's sister Isabella, about to enter a convent, pleads for his life; Angelo, stirred by her virtue as no "strumpet" ever stirred him, offers to spare Claudio if she will sleep with him, and tells her no one will believe her if she tells. She refuses; her brother, after a brave speech, begs her to yield; she curses him. The disguised Duke, who has overheard everything, arranges a bed-trick: Mariana, whom Angelo jilted when her dowry was lost at sea, will take Isabella's place in the dark. It works, and Angelo orders Claudio's execution anyway. The Duke persuades the Provost to send a dead pirate's head in Claudio's stead, tells Isabella her brother is dead, and stages his own return, at which Isabella publicly accuses Angelo and is sent to prison as a slanderer. The friar is unmasked as the Duke; Angelo confesses and asks for death; the Duke marries him to Mariana, sentences him to die "measure for measure," relents when Mariana and Isabella kneel for him, produces Claudio alive, pardons the murderer Barnardine, sentences the slanderer Lucio to marry the whore he got with child, and proposes marriage to Isabella. She does not answer.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Power is the test of what a person is, and the strict are not exempt.**
   - Where it is spoken: The Duke, I.3: "Lord Angelo is precise; / Stands at a guard with envy; scarce confesses / That his blood flows or that his appetite / Is more to bread than stone. Hence shall we see, / If power change purpose, what our seemers be." Angelo, II.1: "'Tis one thing to be tempted, Escalus, / Another thing to fall"; "When I that censure him do so offend, / Let mine own judgement pattern out my death."
   - What the action does with it: Angelo falls within two scenes of the test. His own sentence is pronounced on him in V.1: "An Angelo for Claudio, death for death... measure still for measure." And then it is not carried out. The test works perfectly; the judgment it was meant to produce is set aside by the pleas of two women. The play shows that the strictest man is the most brittle and that the rule he proposed for himself is one nobody in the end will apply.

2. **Authority makes a person a performing ape before heaven; and the man who says so is himself playing tricks.**
   - Where it is spoken: Isabella, II.2: "O, it is excellent / To have a giant's strength; but it is tyrannous / To use it like a giant"; "man, proud man, / Dressed in a little brief authority, / Most ignorant of what he's most assured, / His glassy essence, like an angry ape / Plays such fantastic tricks before high heaven / As makes the angels weep."
   - What the action does with it: Angelo confirms every word. But the Duke, who has the real authority, spends the play in disguise arranging other people's beds, lying to Isabella that her brother is dead "To make her heavenly comforts of despair / When it is least expected" (IV.3), and staging a trial in which she is jailed so that his reveal will land better. The deputy's tricks are a sin; the Duke's are a plot. The play does not say which is more like an angry ape.

3. **Judge as you would be judged; the question is answered against the judge the moment it is asked.**
   - Where it is spoken: Isabella, II.2: "How would you be / If He, which is the top of judgement, should / But judge you as you are?"; "Go to your bosom, / Knock there, and ask your heart what it doth know / That's like my brother's fault." Escalus, II.1: "Whether you had not sometime in your life / Erred in this point which now you censure him."
   - What the action does with it: Angelo's bosom answers at once: "She speaks, and 'tis such sense / That my sense breeds with it"; "Dost thou desire her foully for those things / That make her good?" The self-examination Isabella prescribes produces not mercy but lust. The play shows that knowing one's own guilt does not make a person kinder; it can make him worse, because now he has something to hide.

4. **A law that sleeps is a scarecrow; a law that wakes bites the wrong man; and you cannot hang the whole city.**
   - Where it is spoken: The Duke, I.3: "liberty plucks justice by the nose, / The baby beats the nurse." Angelo, II.1: "We must not make a scarecrow of the law." Pompey, II.1: "Does your worship mean to geld and splay all the youth of the city?... If you head and hang all that offend that way but for ten year together, you'll be glad to give out a commission for more heads."
   - What the action does with it: The revived law's first victim is a man who got his own betrothed with child "upon a true contract." Pompey's prophecy is confirmed by IV.3, where the prison is full of Mistress Overdone's old customers. The Provost says of Claudio, "He hath but as offended in a dream; / All sects, all ages, smack of this vice" (II.2). The Duke's reform ends in four marriages and no executions. Neither severity nor licence is vindicated; the play's last word on the statute is to marry everyone it caught.

5. **Virtue can tempt where vice cannot; the saint is caught with saints for bait.**
   - Where it is spoken: Angelo, II.2: "The tempter or the tempted, who sins most, ha?"; "Can it be / That modesty may more betray our sense / Than woman's lightness?"; "O cunning enemy, that, to catch a saint, / With saints dost bait thy hook!" IV.4: "Alack, when once our grace we have forgot, / Nothing goes right; we would, and we would not."
   - What the action does with it: Angelo is undone by the first good woman he admires. Lucio's joke that his "blood is very snow-broth" turns out to describe someone who has never been tested, not someone who cannot fall. The play's account of temptation is that it comes through what you honour, not what you despise.

6. **Reasoning yourself out of the fear of death works until there is any hope of living.**
   - Where it is spoken: The Duke, III.1: "Be absolute for death. Either death or life / Shall thereby be the sweeter... Thou art not thyself; / For thou exists on many a thousand grains / That issue out of dust." Claudio, III.1: "Ay, but to die, and go we know not where; / To lie in cold obstruction, and to rot... The weariest and most loathed worldly life / That age, ache, penury, and imprisonment / Can lay on nature is a paradise / To what we fear of death."
   - What the action does with it: Claudio accepts the Duke's consolation ("Let it come on") and abandons it the instant his sister names a way out. Barnardine, "insensible of mortality and desperately mortal," simply declines to be executed ("I will not consent to die this day") and is pardoned. Claudio is saved by a pirate who died of fever that morning: "an accident that heaven provides." The Duke's speech is the best argument in the play and the play shows nobody can live by it; chance, not philosophy, decides who dies.

7. **A person can hold chastity above a brother's life, and the play lets her be right and shows what it costs.**
   - Where it is spoken: Isabella, II.4: "Then, Isabel, live chaste, and, brother, die. / More than our brother is our chastity." III.1, to Claudio: "O, you beast! / O faithless coward! O dishonest wretch!... Die, perish!... I'll pray a thousand prayers for thy death, / No word to save thee."
   - What the action does with it: Her resolve is never actually tested; the bed-trick lets her keep both. In V.1 she kneels for Angelo's life with an argument she had no use for when it was her brother's: "Thoughts are no subjects; / Intents but merely thoughts." The play honours her refusal and does not hide that the woman who refused is also the one who told her brother to die quickly. It gives her the last mercy and leaves her silent at the end.

8. **The powerful are believed and the wronged are not; without a hidden witness the slander would stand.**
   - Where it is spoken: Angelo, II.4: "Who will believe thee, Isabel? / My unsoiled name, th' austereness of my life, / My vouch against you, and my place i' th' state / Will so your accusation overweigh / That you shall stifle in your own report"; "my false o'erweighs your true." Isabella: "To whom should I complain? Did I tell this, / Who would believe me?"
   - What the action does with it: In V.1 it happens exactly as Angelo said. The Duke, playing the credulous prince, says "This is most likely!" and "To prison with her!" Friar Peter, Escalus, everyone sides with Angelo. The truth comes out only because the Duke overheard the original proposition in disguise. The play confirms Angelo's cynicism entirely and rescues Isabella only by a device that no real complainant has.

9. **Calumny strikes the whitest virtue, and a prince can forgive an attempted rape sooner than a joke at his own expense.**
   - Where it is spoken: The Duke, III.2: "No might nor greatness in mortality / Can censure 'scape. Back-wounding calumny / The whitest virtue strikes." V.1: "I find an apt remission in myself. / And yet here's one in place I cannot pardon. / [To Lucio] You, sirrah, that knew me for a fool, a coward..."
   - What the action does with it: Angelo (attempted coercion, ordered execution after payment) is pardoned; Barnardine (murder) is pardoned; Lucio (talk) is sentenced to whipping, hanging, and marriage to a whore, commuted to the marriage. The one wound the Duke actually feels is to his reputation. The play lets him say so and does not comment.

10. **Mercy that is not itself is the nurse of second woe; and the ending is all mercy.**
    - Where it is spoken: Escalus, II.1: "Mercy is not itself that oft looks so; / Pardon is still the nurse of second woe." Angelo, II.2: "I show it most of all when I show justice; / For then I pity those I do not know." Isabella, II.4: "Lawful mercy / Is nothing kin to foul redemption." The Duke, IV.2: "When vice makes mercy, mercy's so extended / That for the fault's love is th' offender friended." Mariana, V.1: "best men are moulded out of faults, / And, for the most, become much more the better / For being a little bad."
    - What the action does with it: Everybody is pardoned. Angelo's last word before his pardon is that he "crave[s] death more willingly than mercy"; after it, the Duke notes "a quick'ning in his eye." Whether the general amnesty is the mercy Isabella described in II.2 or the "second woe" Escalus warned of is left to the reader; the play ends before anyone has to live with it.

11. **Seeming is everywhere, and the cure for Angelo's seeming is a bigger seeming.**
    - Where it is spoken: Isabella, II.4: "Seeming, seeming! / I will proclaim thee, Angelo." The Duke, III.2: "That we were all, as some would seem to be, / Free from our faults, as faults from seeming, free!"; "O, what may man within him hide, / Though angel on the outward side!" Isabella, V.1: "one, the wicked'st caitiff on the ground, / May seem as shy, as grave, as just, as absolute, / As Angelo."
    - What the action does with it: The Duke exposes a hypocrite by means of a false habit, a substituted woman, a substituted head, a false report of death, and a staged trial. Lucio's "Cucullus non facit monachum" is aimed at the friar and is literally true of him. The play does not pretend otherwise; it has the Duke say, "So disguise shall, by th' disguised, / Pay with falsehood false exacting" (III.2). Deceit is the only instrument the play has against deceit.

12. **Marriage is handed out as a sentence, and the one person asked does not reply.**
    - Where it is spoken: Lucio, V.1: "Marrying a punk, my lord, is pressing to death, whipping, and hanging." The Duke: "Slandering a prince deserves it." To Isabella: "Give me your hand and say you will be mine."
    - What the action does with it: Angelo is married to the woman he jilted, Lucio to the woman he abandoned, Claudio restored to the woman he wronged, and the Duke proposes to the novice. Three of the four are punishments or reparations. Isabella says nothing. The play's final act of government is a set of weddings, and the text does not record whether its heroine consents.

## What it teaches

The play puts the two hardest questions about justice to the same test and watches them fail in opposite directions. Isabella's plea is true: no one can bear to be judged as they are. Angelo's reply is also true: a law that excuses everyone who has felt the same temptation is no law. The play's answer is a duke who tries both, fourteen years of laxity and then a deputy of ice, and ends by marrying everybody. If that feels like an evasion, it is meant to. There is no principle in the fifth act, only a sovereign deciding case by case, and the one principle he does announce ("measure still for measure") he abandons within forty lines. What you are left with is the suspicion that justice is always someone's discretion dressed in a maxim.

The second thing the play knows is how temptation actually arrives. Angelo is not undone by vice; he has never noticed it. He is undone by the first virtue he admires. "Dost thou desire her foully for those things / That make her good?" is the most honest line in the play about desire, and the most frightening, because it means that purity is no protection and may be the opposite. The precise man had simply never been near anything he wanted. When he is, the whole structure goes in a scene, and he knows it is going and cannot stop: "we would, and we would not."

What is unresolved is the Duke, and through him the possibility that the only way to be good in Vienna is to lie. He lies to the Provost, to Isabella, to Angelo, to the city; he plays with Isabella's grief for the sake of a better surprise; he feels the slander on his name more than the deputy's crime. He also saves Claudio, rights Mariana, and spares everyone. The play does not make him a villain or a saint; it makes him the government. If his last question to Isabella is a proposal, her silence is the text's last observation about power: that it can arrange everything, including the answer it will not wait to hear.

## Preoccupations

- power as the test of what a person is, and the strict failing it first
- the law asleep, the law awake, and the wrong man caught either way
- judging others by a standard the judge cannot meet
- temptation arriving through virtue, not vice
- who is believed: the powerful against the wronged
- the fear of death and the uselessness of philosophy against it
- seeming, disguise, and the lie used to expose a lie
- mercy as discretion rather than principle; marriage as sentence
