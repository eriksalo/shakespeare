# The Tragedy of Macbeth

## What happens

A victorious general is told by three witches that he will be king; the first part of their greeting comes true within minutes, and he and his wife take the rest as a thing to be made true by killing the king who is sleeping as their guest. He does it, is crowned, and finds that being king is nothing "but to be safely thus": he has his friend Banquo murdered because the witches promised Banquo's children the throne, then has Macduff's wife and children slaughtered because Macduff fled. His wife, who had scorned his scruples, walks in her sleep trying to wash blood off her hands and dies offstage, probably by her own hand. He goes back to the witches, is given promises that sound like guarantees (no man of woman born can harm him; he will not fall until a wood moves), and holds his castle on the strength of them until the wood moves (soldiers carrying branches) and the man of no woman born (delivered by Caesarean) cuts off his head. The shape is a steep climb followed by a long, fully conscious walk down into numbness.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **We fear a thought more than a fact; the imagined deed shakes us harder than the done one, until the done one starts to shake us harder than anything.**
   - Where it is spoken: Macbeth, I.3: "Present fears / Are less than horrible imaginings. / My thought, whose murder yet is but fantastical, / Shakes so my single state of man / That function is smother'd in surmise, / And nothing is but what is not."
   - What the action does with it: The play first confirms it, then reverses it. Before the murder Macbeth sees a dagger that is not there (II.1); after it he cannot look at his own hands (II.2). By III.4 the dead man is sitting in his chair at dinner. The imagination does not calm down once the deed is done; it gets worse, because now it has material. The final reversal is V.5, where he has "almost forgot the taste of fears" and a scream of women cannot start him. The play shows that the only cure for horrible imaginings is to kill the faculty that produces them, and that this is a kind of death before death.

2. **The act you take to secure yourself is the act that teaches others how to destroy you.**
   - Where it is spoken: Macbeth, I.7: "we but teach / Bloody instructions, which being taught, return / To plague th' inventor. This even-handed justice / Commends th' ingredience of our poison'd chalice / To our own lips."
   - What the action does with it: Macbeth states the law and then breaks it anyway, and the play executes the law on him exactly. The fear of Banquo makes him murder Banquo, which produces the ghost and the ruined banquet that lose him the thanes; the slaughter of Macduff's family is what turns Macduff from a fugitive into the one man on the field who must kill him personally ("If thou be'st slain and with no stroke of mine, / My wife and children's ghosts will haunt me still," V.7). Every security measure produces the danger it was meant to prevent. The play confirms the observation absolutely, and shows that knowing it in advance did not help.

3. **Getting what you wanted without being at peace in it is worse than never having had it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Lady Macbeth, III.2: "Naught's had, all's spent, / Where our desire is got without content: / 'Tis safer to be that which we destroy, / Than by destruction dwell in doubtful joy." Macbeth, III.1: "To be thus is nothing, / But to be safely thus."
   - What the action does with it: This is said by the two people who have just become king and queen, and the rest of the play is the proof. Macbeth envies Duncan his grave: "After life's fitful fever he sleeps well; / Treason has done his worst: nor steel, nor poison... nothing / Can touch him further" (III.2). The murdered man is better off than the murderer. By V.3 the king lists what he has instead of "honour, love, obedience, troops of friends": "Curses, not loud but deep, mouth-honour, breath." The play lets nothing good come of the crown at all.

4. **Once you have gone far enough into wrong, turning back costs as much as going on, so you go on.**
   - Where it is spoken: Macbeth, III.4: "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o'er." And III.2: "Things bad begun make strong themselves by ill."
   - What the action does with it: The play shows this as a true description of how the murders multiply, not as a justification: Banquo, then Fleance attempted, then Macduff's household, then "Hang those that talk of fear" (V.3). But it also quietly shows that the premise is false. Returning would not have cost the same: Lady Macbeth does not wade on, and the play gives her a different death, in bed, haunted, with a doctor saying "More needs she the divine than the physician" (V.1). Both routes end in death; only one ends in a man who can no longer feel. The play shows that the "might as well go on" reasoning is itself one of the things the blood does to the mind.

5. **A prophecy that tells the truth can still be the thing that destroys you, because you will supply the meaning.**
   - Where it is spoken: Banquo, I.3: "oftentimes to win us to our harm, / The instruments of darkness tell us truths; / Win us with honest trifles, to betray's / In deepest consequence." Macbeth at the end, V.8: "these juggling fiends... / That palter with us in a double sense; / That keep the word of promise to our ear, / And break it to our hope."
   - What the action does with it: Every word the witches say is literally true: Cawdor, king, Banquo's issue, Birnam wood, the man not of woman born. The witches never tell Macbeth to kill anyone. He hears "king hereafter" and his mind goes at once to murder ("that suggestion / Whose horrid image doth unfix my hair," I.3); he even sees for a moment that he need not act ("If chance will have me king, why, chance may crown me / Without my stir") and then acts. Banquo hears the same prophecy, suspects it, and does nothing. The play confirms Banquo's warning and makes clear that the "instruments of darkness" needed Macbeth's cooperation. Hecate says it plainly: "security / Is mortals' chiefest enemy" (III.5).

6. **A man can be persuaded to do what he has already decided not to do by having his manhood questioned.**
   - Where it is spoken: Macbeth, I.7: "I dare do all that may become a man; / Who dares do more is none." Lady Macbeth: "When you durst do it, then you were a man; / And, to be more than what you were, you would / Be so much more the man."
   - What the action does with it: Macbeth has reasoned his way, in the best speech of the play, to "We will proceed no further in this business." His wife's reply contains no counter-argument, only shame, and it works in about forty lines. The play then repeats the pattern downward: Macbeth recruits the murderers by telling them that if they do not kill Banquo they are not men but dogs ("in the catalogue ye go for men," III.1), and the ghost scene turns on "Are you a man?" (III.4). Malcolm's "Dispute it like a man" to the grieving Macduff gets the only sane answer in the play: "I shall do so; / But I must also feel it as a man" (IV.3). The play shows the manhood taunt as the lever that opens every door to blood, and gives one man who refuses to let it cancel his grief.

7. **Blood does not wash off, and the person who said it would is the one who finds this out.**
   - Where it is spoken: Macbeth, II.2: "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" Lady Macbeth, same scene: "A little water clears us of this deed: / How easy is it then!"
   - What the action does with it: The play takes three acts to hand Lady Macbeth's line back to her: "What, will these hands ne'er be clean?... all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand" (V.1). The woman who called the sleeping and the dead "but as pictures" ends by seeing them in her sleep. The precision of the reversal is the point. Macbeth's version is wrong too, but in the other direction: the blood does not stain the seas; it just stains him until he stops noticing.

8. **Sleep is the thing murder destroys first, and it is the thing the murderer most needs.**
   - Where it is spoken: Macbeth, II.2: "Sleep that knits up the ravell'd sleave of care, / The death of each day's life, sore labour's bath, / Balm of hurt minds... Macbeth shall sleep no more!"
   - What the action does with it: Duncan is killed asleep; Banquo asks heaven to restrain "the cursed thoughts that nature / Gives way to in repose" (II.1) and is killed walking in the dark; Macbeth and his wife "sleep / In the affliction of these terrible dreams / That shake us nightly" (III.2); Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking is sleep that does the work of waking. The Lord in III.6 lists what Scotland has lost: "sleep to our nights." The play makes sleep the measure of innocence and shows every guilty person deprived of it, and it ends with Malcolm hoping "that chambers will be safe" (V.4).

9. **A tyranny makes everyone a suspect to everyone, and makes doing good dangerous and doing harm respectable.**
   - Where it is spoken: Lady Macduff, IV.2: "I am in this earthly world, where to do harm / Is often laudable; to do good sometime / Accounted dangerous folly." Ross, IV.2: "cruel are the times, when we are traitors, / And do not know ourselves." Donalbain, II.3: "There's daggers in men's smiles: the near in blood, / The nearer bloody."
   - What the action does with it: Lady Macduff says it minutes before being killed for her husband's choice. Malcolm, meeting Macduff in England, has to pretend to be a monster to test whether Macduff is a spy (IV.3), because "Though all things foul would wear the brows of grace, / Yet grace must still look so." Macbeth keeps "a servant fee'd" in every house (III.4). Macduff's son, asked what a traitor is, works out that "the liars and swearers are fools: for there are liars and swearers enow to beat the honest men and hang up them" (IV.2), and is killed. The play confirms the observation and makes a child its clearest witness.

10. **Life can look, from inside enough loss, like a story told by an idiot meaning nothing; the play does not say that it is.**
    - Where it is spoken: Macbeth, V.5: "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, / Creeps in this petty pace from day to day... Life's but a walking shadow; a poor player... it is a tale / Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing."
    - What the action does with it: The speech is spoken on hearing his wife is dead, and his first reaction is that she "should have died hereafter"; there would have been time for grief later. There is no later. The play sets the speech against Macduff's grief in IV.3, which is full of sound and fury and signifies everything ("All my pretty ones? / Did you say all?"), and against Siward's acceptance of his son's death ("They say he parted well and paid his score"). The nihilism is real and earned, but it is the view from one man's ruin, and the play surrounds it with people for whom things still mean. Whether that is consolation or only contrast is left to you.

11. **Grief that cannot speak breaks the heart; grief that speaks can be turned into something else, and that something else is not always better.**
    - Where it is spoken: Malcolm, IV.3: "Give sorrow words. The grief that does not speak / Whispers the o'er-fraught heart, and bids it break." Then: "Let grief / Convert to anger; blunt not the heart, enrage it."
    - What the action does with it: Macduff does both: he feels it, insists on feeling it, and then turns it into a whetstone. The play rewards this; he kills Macbeth. But it also shows Lady Macbeth, who gives sorrow no words to anyone awake and whose heart does break: "The heart is sorely charged" (V.1). And it shows that the conversion of grief to anger is the same mechanism Macbeth used ("Be this the whetstone of your sword" is Malcolm's line, but "Let grief / Convert to anger" is a policy). The play endorses the outcome and keeps the method in view.

12. **There is no way to read a person's mind in their face, and the people who most need to know this learn it last.**
    - Where it is spoken: Duncan, I.4: "There's no art / To find the mind's construction in the face: / He was a gentleman on whom I built / An absolute trust." Lady Macbeth, I.5: "look like the innocent flower, / But be the serpent under't."
    - What the action does with it: Duncan says this about the first Cawdor and in the next breath greets the second Cawdor, who will kill him that night, as "worthiest cousin." The play's irony is exact and unsparing. Macbeth is a bad actor ("Your face, my thane, is as a book where men / May read strange matters"), and still nobody reads him until it is too late; Banquo suspects and keeps "my bosom franchis'd" and is killed anyway; Malcolm, having learned the lesson, trusts no one and nearly loses Macduff. The play confirms the observation and shows that the only defenses against it are either suspicion of everyone or luck.

## What it teaches

The play is unusual in that its hero knows, before he does anything, nearly everything the play will teach. The speech in I.7 is a complete moral argument against the murder: it will teach others to murder; the king is his guest and his kinsman and a good man; pity will plead against him; he has no motive but "vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself." He is right about all of it, and he does it. What the play leaves you with is not that wrongdoing is punished, which is the easy reading, but that knowing a thing is wrong and knowing what it will cost gives you almost no protection against doing it if someone you love tells you that refusing makes you less than you are. The gap between knowing and doing in this play runs the other way from the usual: the failure is not that he cannot act, but that he cannot not act once the thought has taken hold. "Nothing is but what is not."

The second thing the play teaches is the structure of a slide. Macbeth never decides to become a butcher. He decides one thing, and that one thing produces a fear, and the fear produces the next decision, and by the time he is ordering the deaths of children he is not deciding at all: "The very firstlings of my heart shall be / The firstlings of my hand" (IV.1). The play is honest that this is a kind of relief; it is the end of the horrible imaginings. It is also the end of him. The man who says "Tomorrow, and tomorrow" has no imagination left to be tormented and so has nothing left to live with. The difficulty the play leaves you is that it is hard to say at which step he could still have stopped, and whether anyone, at that step, would have.

What is unresolved is the witches. They tell only truths, and every truth they tell is a trap, but the trap only closes because Macbeth builds it. Banquo heard the same words. You can read the play as showing that fate is real and chooses its instruments, or as showing that "fate" is the name we give to our own desires when they are spoken aloud by someone else. The play does not choose, and Macbeth himself does not know; he blames the fiends with his last breath and fights anyway. What he gets for his trouble is a head on a pole and a one-line epitaph from the man who inherits: "this dead butcher, and his fiend-like queen." The play has spent five acts making you unable to think of them that simply, and then gives the last word to someone who can. Whether that is justice or only the way the living talk about the dead is the last thing the play asks.

## Preoccupations

- how a wrong act breeds the next one until there is no choosing left
- the imagination as a torturer, and numbness as its only cure
- the fear that comes with possession, worse than wanting
- manhood used as a weapon to shame people into violence
- sleep, and the loss of sleep, as the measure of guilt
- prophecy and the trap of true words wrongly understood
- the impossibility of reading a face
- what a tyranny does to trust between ordinary people
