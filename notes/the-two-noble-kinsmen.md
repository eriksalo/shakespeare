# The Two Noble Kinsmen

## What happens
Theseus' wedding to Hippolyta is interrupted by three widowed queens whose husbands' bodies Creon of Thebes will not let them bury; Theseus postpones the wedding, destroys Thebes, and brings back two wounded Theban cousins, Palamon and Arcite, as prisoners for life. In prison the cousins vow that their friendship makes the cell a sanctuary; then Emilia, Hippolyta's sister, walks in the garden below, both see her, and within thirty lines they are enemies ("I saw her first"). Arcite is released and banished, stays in disguise, wins Theseus' games, and becomes Emilia's servant. The Jailer's Daughter, in love with Palamon, frees him and follows him into the woods, where he does not come to meet her and she goes mad. The cousins arm each other in the forest and fight; Theseus finds them and condemns both; the women beg; he orders instead a formal combat in a month, the loser to be beheaded. Emilia cannot choose between them. The Daughter is "cured" by a doctor's plan in which her Wooer pretends to be Palamon and is told to sleep with her. Each cousin prays to a god and receives a sign — Arcite from Mars, Palamon from Venus, Emilia from Diana. Arcite wins the combat; Palamon lays his head on the block; a messenger cries hold; Arcite's horse has thrown him, and he dies giving Emilia to Palamon. Theseus orders a funeral and a wedding and says the gods are above our question.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Grief and time consume everything, including the people who think they are the consumers.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Theseus, recalling the First Queen on her wedding day: "O grief and time, / Fearful consumers, you will all devour!" (1.1). Third Queen: "This world's a city full of straying streets, / And death's the market-place where each one meets" (1.5).
   - *What the action does with it:* The play opens on a wedding interrupted by a funeral and closes on a funeral turned into a wedding. The three queens who kneel in Act 1 are the image of what Emilia will be in Act 5, the woman the strong men leave behind. Theseus' own last word is "bear us like the time" (5.4), a surrender to the process he named at the start.

2. **To be ruled by the senses is to lose the "human title," and no one in the play keeps it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Theseus, having put off his wedding night to fight for the queens: "As we are men, / Thus should we do; being sensually subdued, / We lose our human title" (1.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* Theseus is the one man who overrules desire by duty, and the play then shows him governed by something just as irrational — a rash oath. "I have sworn" (3.6) nearly kills both cousins, and only Emilia's argument that "if such vows / Stand for express will, all the world must perish" moves him. The cousins are subdued by a single glance; the Daughter by one kiss; Emilia's fancy by whichever picture she looks at last. The claim is true as a standard and no one in the play meets it, including the man who states it.

3. **A friendship that calls itself everything can be dissolved by one look.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Arcite in prison: "We are one another's wife, ever begetting / New births of love; we are father, friends, acquaintance; / We are, in one another, families; / I am your heir, and you are mine" (2.2). Palamon: "I do not think it possible our friendship / Should ever leave us." Arcite: "Till our deaths it cannot."
   - *What the action does with it:* Emilia enters the garden below in the same scene. Palamon: "Never till now I was in prison, Arcite." Then: "I saw her first." "That's nothing." The vow is broken within thirty lines of being made. And yet the friendship does not die: they arm each other with care ("Is't not too heavy?" "I'll buckle 't close", 3.6), remember old battles, share wine and bawdy jokes, and Palamon's last words to Arcite are "One that yet loves thee dying" (5.4). The play observes that love and friendship cannot hold the same object, and that the friendship survives underneath the fight, unable to prevent it.

4. **A woman may believe she will never love a man, and the belief may be true and be overridden anyway.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Emilia on Flavina: "the true love 'tween maid and maid may be / More than in sex individual" (1.3). Hippolyta: "I must no more believe thee in this point— / Though in 't I know thou dost believe thyself— / Than I will trust a sickly appetite." Emilia: "I am not / Against your faith, yet I continue mine."
   - *What the action does with it:* Emilia never chooses a man. She prays to Diana to remain a virgin or else to be given "he of the two pretenders that best loves me" (5.1): "I am guiltless of election." Asked to choose by Theseus, she says "they are both too excellent" (3.6); asked by herself, she flips between portraits and calls herself "a fool; my reason is lost in me" (4.2). The outcome is decided by Mars, Venus, and a horse. Her original claim is never refuted; it is simply not consulted. The play lets the reader notice that the marriage at the end is one she consented to under the words "Yes, I must, sir, / Else both miscarry" (3.6).

5. **The mind can declare itself self-sufficient a moment before it is proved otherwise.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Arcite: "Let's think this prison holy sanctuary, / To keep us from corruption of worse men ... What worthy blessing / Can be but our imaginations / May make it ours?" (2.2). Palamon: "What a misery / It is to live abroad and everywhere! / 'Tis like a beast, methinks."
   - *What the action does with it:* They say liberty is "the poison of pure spirits" and that a wife "might part us lawfully," and then a woman they have not met parts them while they are still locked in. The scene is built as a demonstration: the ideal of inner freedom is stated in full and then disproved by a window.

6. **Love is a tyranny that overthrows reason, and the play calls it that without romance.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Theseus' list of what drives men past nature: "Love's provocations, zeal, a mistress' task, / Desire of liberty, a fever, madness" (1.4). Hippolyta: "'Tis pity love should be so tyrannous" (4.2). The Daughter: "I love him beyond love and beyond reason, / Or wit, or safety" (2.6). The Doctor: "That intemperate surfeit of her eye hath distempered the other senses" (4.3). Emilia: "What a mere child is Fancy, / That, having two fair gauds of equal sweetness, / Cannot distinguish, but must cry for both" (4.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* No one in the play loves wisely or is rewarded for loving. The Daughter goes mad; the cousins try to kill each other; Emilia is handed to a winner and then to the loser. Palamon's prayer to Venus describes her power as a yoke "heavier / Than lead itself" and tells of a crippled eighty-year-old with a fourteen-year-old bride, "'twas thy power / To put life into dust" (5.1). The play does not pretend the force it describes is kind.

7. **The one who actually acts for love is the one of lowest rank, and she is paid with a lie.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Daughter: "I am base, / My father the mean keeper of his prison, / And he a prince. To marry him is hopeless; / To be his whore is witless" (2.4). After freeing him: "he has not thanked me / For what I have done; no, not so much as kissed me" (2.6).
   - *What the action does with it:* She is the only character who risks her father's life, her own, and the law for the person she loves, and she is never named: the dramatis personae calls her "Daughter." Palamon misses the meeting place; she goes mad; she is cured by a Wooer in Palamon's clothes whom the Doctor tells to "Lie with her, if she ask you" (5.2); she marries him. Palamon, on the block, gives her a purse and calls her "A right good creature, more to me deserving / Then I can 'quite or speak of" (5.4). The low plot shadows the high one and takes the real cost.

8. **A false belief is cured by a false cure, and the father's objection goes unanswered.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Doctor: "It is a falsehood she is in, which is with falsehoods to be combated" (4.3). "Ne'er cast your child away for honesty" (5.2). The Jailer: "Methinks you are i' th' wrong still."
   - *What the action does with it:* The cure works — "Sir, she's well restored, / And to be married shortly" (5.4). The play permits the success and lets the Jailer's doubt stand as the last honest word on the method. The Daughter's final lines ("But you shall not hurt me ... If you do, love, I'll cry") are spoken to a man she thinks is someone else.

9. **Refusing to choose is a choice, and pity without judgement does harm.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Theseus to Emilia: "You are a right woman, sister: you have pity, / But want the understanding where to use it" (3.6). Emilia: "For me, a hair shall never fall of these men." Hippolyta: "Infinite pity / That four such eyes should be so fixed on one / That two must needs be blind for 't" (5.3).
   - *What the action does with it:* Emilia's refusal to decide is what produces the combat and the beheading; her plea "banish 'em" is rejected by both cousins, who would rather die than forget her. Her "Is this winning?" (5.3) is the play's honest response to its own machinery. The play gives her the moral high ground and shows it accomplishing nothing.

10. **Prayers are answered exactly, and exact answers are how the gods destroy people.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Arcite to Mars: "by thee / Be styled the lord o' th' day" (5.1). Palamon to Venus: "Give me the victory of this question, which / Is true love's merit." Emilia to Diana: "He of the two pretenders that best loves me / And has the truest title in 't, let him / Take off my wheaten garland." Theseus afterward: "The powerful Venus well hath graced her altar, / And given you your love. Our master Mars, / Hath vouched his oracle, and to Arcite gave / The grace of the contention" (5.4).
   - *What the action does with it:* Every petition is granted to the letter and the result is a corpse. Arcite gets the day and not the lady; Palamon gets the lady and not the day; Emilia gets the one with "the truest title" by Arcite's own confession. Palamon: "That we should things desire, which do cost us / The loss of our desire!" Theseus: "The conquered triumphs; / The victor has the loss" (5.4). The play's gods are precise, and precision is not mercy.

11. **Honour is the thing these men will not trade even for their lives, and the play neither mocks nor endorses it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Palamon: "I'll be cut a-pieces / Before I take this oath! Forget I love her?" (3.6). Arcite: "'Tis worse to me than begging / To take my life so basely." Pirithous: "These are men!" Palamon on the block: "we come towards the gods / Young and unwappered, not halting under crimes" (5.4). Arcite dying: "I was false, / Yet never treacherous. Forgive me, cousin."
   - *What the action does with it:* The honour code is what makes the combat inevitable and the death pointless; it is also what makes the arming scene tender and the deathbed gift possible. The play lets the Thebans in Act 1 complain that soldiers are "paid with ice to cool 'em" (1.2) and then shows what the soldier's code costs when there is no war to spend it in. No one in the play argues against honour; no one is saved by it.

12. **We are children before whatever arranges our lives, and the only wisdom offered is to stop asking.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Theseus' last speech: "O you heavenly charmers, / What things you make of us! For what we lack / We laugh, for what we have are sorry, still / Are children in some kind. Let us be thankful / For that which is, and with you leave dispute / That are above our question" (5.4).
   - *What the action does with it:* This is the play's final word and it is explicitly a refusal to interpret. Theseus, who was "as dearly sorry" for Palamon an hour ago as he is now glad, admits his judgements track events rather than govern them. Emilia had already said it: "Our reasons are not prophets / When oft our fancies are" (5.3). The play ends by declining to make sense of itself and calling that acceptance.

## What it teaches

This is a play in which almost nobody gets what they want in the form they wanted it, and the few who do (Palamon gets Emilia; the Wooer gets the Daughter) get it through someone else's death or madness. If you come to it for a lesson about love, what you find is a demonstration that the people most capable of love — the two cousins, the Daughter — are made by it into enemies, prisoners, and fools, and that the one person asked to choose is the one who cannot. The Daughter's "To marry him is hopeless; / To be his whore is witless" is as clear a statement as the play contains, and the play's answer to it is to let her go mad and be deceived into recovery.

What is hard here is the gods. Each prayer is answered literally. A reader looking for injustice will not find it: Arcite asked for victory and got it; Palamon asked for Emilia and got her; Emilia asked for whichever loved her best and Arcite, dying, confesses that was Palamon. The machinery is fair. It is also what kills Arcite. The play's deepest observation may be the one Palamon makes over the body: that to desire things is to risk paying for them with the thing desired, and that there is no version of wanting that escapes this. Theseus' closing advice — be thankful for what is, stop disputing with what is above question — is either wisdom or exhaustion, and the text lets you hear it as both.

What the play does not do is pretend the friendship was false. The arming scene, where each cousin buckles the other's armour and asks if it pinches, is the tenderest thing in it, and it precedes an attempt at mutual killing. If there is something to carry away, it is that the loves in a life do not sort themselves into compatible kinds; they compete, and the competition does not cancel any of them. Arcite's last words are "I was false, / Yet never treacherous." The play asks you to believe both halves.

## Preoccupations

- friendship and love competing for the same object and destroying each other
- desire as a tyranny that overthrows reason in everyone it touches
- prayers answered exactly, and exactness as a form of cruelty
- the woman who is the prize and never the chooser
- rank: the lowest person acts most and is paid least
- honour as a code that makes death inevitable and tenderness possible
- grief and time as the consumers of every celebration
- the refusal to interpret: being children before the gods
