# Cymbeline

## What happens
Imogen, heir to the British king Cymbeline, has married the orphan Posthumus against her father's wish; he is banished, and the Queen, who meant Imogen for her blockish son Cloten, buys poison. In Rome, Posthumus lets the Italian Iachimo wager ten thousand ducats against his diamond that Imogen can be seduced. Iachimo fails with Imogen, hides in a trunk in her bedroom, inventories the room, steals Posthumus's bracelet, notes a mole on her breast, and returns with "simular proof." Posthumus believes him, curses all women, and orders his servant Pisanio to kill her at Milford Haven. Pisanio disobeys, gives her a boy's clothes and the Queen's "cordial" (which the honest doctor has secretly made harmless), and reports her dead. Imogen, as "Fidele," stumbles on a cave in Wales where the exiled lord Belarius has raised Cymbeline's two stolen sons as his own; they love her at once. Cloten, wearing Posthumus's clothes to rape her, is beheaded by one of the boys. Imogen takes the drug, is mourned with a dirge, wakes beside the headless body and takes it for her husband, and enters the service of the Roman general Lucius. Rome invades because Britain, at the Queen's urging, has stopped paying tribute; Posthumus, come to die, fights disguised as a peasant beside Belarius and the boys in a lane and turns the battle, then surrenders as a Roman to be hanged. In prison his dead family and Jupiter appear to him. In the last scene every secret comes out in turn: the Queen's confession, Iachimo's, Pisanio's, Guiderius's killing of Cloten, Belarius's theft of the princes. Posthumus forgives Iachimo, Cymbeline pardons all, and the victor voluntarily pays Rome the tribute he refused.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A man who says his wife is not for sale will stake her the moment his confidence is challenged.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Posthumus, 1.5: "the other is not a thing for sale, and only the gift of the gods." Iachimo: "I make my wager rather against your confidence than her reputation"; "If you buy ladies' flesh at a million a dram, you cannot preserve it from tainting."
   - *What the action does with it:* Within twenty lines of calling her priceless Posthumus has put his ring on the table and drawn up "articles." Iachimo names the weakness exactly: the wager is against the husband's confidence, not the wife. When Iachimo returns, Posthumus demands "some corporal sign about her" and is satisfied by a bracelet and a mole; he knows her by inventory, as Iachimo did her chamber. The man who said she was a gift of the gods accepts the thief's proof that she was goods.

2. **Clothes are taken for the man, and even the truest eye is fooled by them.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cloten, 4.2: "Know'st me not by my clothes?" Guiderius: "No, nor thy tailor, rascal, / Who is thy grandfather; he made those clothes, / Which, as it seems, make thee." Posthumus, 5.1: "To shame the guise o' th' world, I will begin / The fashion less without and more within."
   - *What the action does with it:* Cloten puts on Posthumus's suit because Imogen said she valued "his mean'st garment" above Cloten's person (2.3). Headless in that suit, he is mistaken by Imogen herself for her husband: "I know the shape of's leg; this is his hand" (4.2). The play's most loving eye reads a fool's body as a hero's by the clothes, and Imogen's grief is real while its object is false. Posthumus, dressed as a peasant, does the one great deed of the war; Iachimo, beaten by him, wonders how "this carl, / A very drudge of nature's" could do it (5.2). The play says the outside lies and then shows that nobody, not even Imogen, can read past it.

3. **Blood shows through any upbringing; rank shows nothing at all.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Belarius, 3.3: "How hard it is to hide the sparks of nature!... nature prompts them / In simple and low things to prince it much / Beyond the trick of others." 4.2: "Cowards father cowards and base things sire base. / Nature hath meal and bran, contempt and grace."
   - *What the action does with it:* The cave-bred boys love Fidele before they know she is their sister ("I know not why / I love this youth"), hold a lane against an army, and Guiderius is proved by "a sanguine star" on his neck. Cymbeline cries "O rare instinct!" But the counter-case is Cloten, a queen's son, "an empty purse; / There was no money in't," whom Imogen chose against as "a puttock." Cymbeline calls Posthumus "thou basest thing" and is wrong. The play endorses blood and discredits place: nature is legible, titles are not, and the King is the worst reader of both.

4. **Slander cuts deeper than a sword and reaches where no sword can.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Pisanio, 3.4: "The paper / Hath cut her throat already. No, 'tis slander, / Whose edge is sharper than the sword, whose tongue / Outvenoms all the worms of Nile."
   - *What the action does with it:* No blade touches Imogen; a letter does. Iachimo's lie moves her from princess to corpse-in-a-cave by way of Milford. And the slander spreads: Imogen concludes that "Men's vows are women's traitors! All good seeming, / By thy revolt, O husband, shall be thought / Put on for villainy" (3.4), and Posthumus concludes that every vice is "the woman's part" (2.5). One false report poisons each of them against a whole sex. Iachimo, landing in Britain, feels the air "revengingly enfeebles me" (5.2): the slanderer is the only one physically weakened by the slander.

5. **A wronged man will blame his own nature on the woman in him; the cure is to forgive before he knows she was innocent.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Posthumus, 2.5: "Could I find out / The woman's part in me! For there's no motion / That tends to vice in man but I affirm / It is the woman's part." Posthumus, 5.1: "You married ones, / If each of you should take this course, how many / Must murder wives much better than themselves / For wrying but a little!"
   - *What the action does with it:* The turn in Posthumus comes in 5.1, still believing her guilty: he forgives her "wrying" and condemns himself. Only in 5.5 does he learn she was chaste. The play puts repentance before vindication so that it is not bought by the truth. Then it undercuts him: his next act is to strike the page who interrupts his grief, who is Imogen ("Thou scornful page, / There lies thy part"), and Pisanio says, "You ne'er kill'd Imogen till now." The reformed man is still dangerous.

6. **True service may require disobedience and lies, and the servant will be cursed for it by the person he saves.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Pisanio, 3.2: "If it be so to do good service, never / Let me be counted serviceable"; 4.3: "Wherein I am false I am honest; not true, to be true." Posthumus, 5.1: "Every good servant does not all commands; / No bond but to do just ones." Cornelius, 1.6: "I the truer / So to be false with her."
   - *What the action does with it:* Every rescue in the play is a deception. Pisanio disobeys a murder order, forges a letter for Cloten, and passes on the Queen's box in good faith; Cornelius fakes the poison. Imogen, waking beside the corpse, curses "damn'd Pisanio" as a murderer, and in the last scene says "Thou gav'st me poison. Dangerous fellow, hence!" The play confirms that fidelity is not obedience and shows that the faithful servant is misjudged until the very end, by the one he kept alive.

7. **The court is a place where climbing is falling; the cave teaches hardiness; and the young cannot bear to stay in the cave.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Belarius, 3.3: "the art o' th' court, / As hard to leave as keep, whose top to climb / Is certain falling, or so slipp'ry that / The fear's as bad as falling." Imogen, 4.2: "Our courtiers say all's savage but at court. / Experience, O, thou disprov'st report!" Guiderius, 3.3: "unto us it is / A cell of ignorance... A prison for a debtor."
   - *What the action does with it:* The cave produces the three men who save Britain: "Two boys, an old man (twice a boy), a lane, / Preserv'd the Britons" (5.3). Imogen, starving, learns that "Plenty and peace breeds cowards; hardness ever / Of hardiness is mother" (3.6). Yet the boys are "asham'd / To look upon the holy sun" as unknowns (4.4) and insist on going to war, and the play ends with everyone back at court. Belarius is right about the court and the boys are right that his cave is a cage; the play grants both and sends them all home.

8. **The gods cross those they love, and the man who is finally favoured admits he does not know why.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Jupiter, 5.4: "Whom best I love I cross; to make my gift, / The more delay'd, delighted." Lucius, 4.2: "Some falls are means the happier to arise." Posthumus, 5.1: "You snatch some hence for little faults; that's love."
   - *What the action does with it:* The providence is asserted by a god on an eagle and then delivered by a chain of confessions, a doctor's substitution, and a pun (the Soothsayer derives "mulier" from "mollis aer"). Posthumus, waking from the vision, says, "Many dream not to find, neither deserve, / And yet are steep'd in favours; so am I, / That have this golden chance, and know not why" (5.4). The play lets the gods claim design and lets its hero say the favour is unearned. Cymbeline's "the gods do mean to strike me / To death with mortal joy" keeps the violence inside the blessing.

9. **Death makes all dust alike, and the living insist on distinctions anyway.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The dirge, 4.2: "Golden lads and girls all must, / As chimney-sweepers, come to dust." Guiderius: "Thersites' body is as good as Ajax', / When neither are alive." Belarius: "Though mean and mighty rotting / Together have one dust, yet reverence... doth make distinction / Of place 'tween high and low."
   - *What the action does with it:* The dirge is sung over a living woman; the fool is buried beside her "as a prince"; she wakes and cannot tell the fool's body from her husband's. The Gaoler adds the commoner's version: "O, the charity of a penny cord! It sums up thousands in a trice" (5.4). The play stages the leveller as literally as it can, a headless noble body indistinguishable from a base one, and then has the survivors reassert rank in the last scene by mole and mantle. Both are true: dust is dust, and the living need the distinction.

10. **Everyone misjudges; the play honours the posture of trust even when it is wrong.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cymbeline, 5.5: "Mine eyes / Were not in fault, for she was beautiful; / Mine ears, that heard her flattery; nor my heart / That thought her like her seeming. It had been vicious / To have mistrusted her." Imogen, 4.2: "Our very eyes / Are sometimes, like our judgements, blind."
   - *What the action does with it:* Cymbeline is wrong about Posthumus, the Queen, Belarius, and his own sons, and keeps his crown. Posthumus is wrong about Imogen; Imogen is wrong about Pisanio and about a corpse; Iachimo is wrong about what she will do with a proposition. The Queen "never lov'd you; only / Affected greatness got by you, not you." The play refuses to make trust a fault: Cymbeline says mistrust would have been "vicious," and the ending rewards the trusters and kills the schemers. It does not say trust is wise, only that it is not vicious.

11. **Pardon is a choice the strong make, and the irredeemable are removed before it is offered.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Posthumus, 5.5: "The pow'r that I have on you is to spare you; / The malice towards you to forgive you. Live, / And deal with others better." Cymbeline: "Pardon's the word to all."
   - *What the action does with it:* Iachimo kneels and is spared; Belarius, who stole the princes, is called "brother"; Guiderius, who killed a prince, is unbound. But the Queen has died "madly" and Cloten is headless before the pardons begin; the play forgives only those who can be forgiven and disposes of the others offstage or by a boy's sword. Then the victor pays the tribute he fought to refuse: "Although the victor, we submit to Cæsar." Peace is made by the winner giving up the thing the war was about, which the Queen and Cloten were the loudest voices for.

12. **Constancy is not passivity: the faithful woman is the one who moves, disguises, lies, and chooses.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Imogen, 1.2: "I chose an eagle, / And did avoid a puttock." 3.2: "Accessible is none but Milford way." 3.4: "I see into thy end, and am almost / A man already." 3.4: "Hath Britain all the sun that shines?... In a great pool a swan's nest."
   - *What the action does with it:* Imogen does the travelling in this play. She defies her father, rides to Milford, offers her breast to Pisanio's sword, takes a page's clothes, keeps house in a cave, lies to Lucius about the corpse's name ("Richard du Champ"), and in the last scene declines to beg Lucius's life because "There's other work in hand." Lucius, hurt, says, "Briefly die their joys / That place them on the truth of girls and boys." Her constancy to Posthumus costs her master her gratitude. The play makes her faithful and not simple: she tells useful lies and chooses whom to save.

## What it teaches

The first thing this play leaves with you is how little the people in it know about one another, and how much they act on anyway. Posthumus knows Imogen by a bracelet and a mole; Imogen knows her husband by the shape of a leg on a headless corpse; Cymbeline knows his wife by her flattery and his sons by a mole on a neck. Nobody in Britain or Rome sees past the outside, and the play keeps saying so ("Our very eyes / Are sometimes, like our judgements, blind"). What it does not do is punish them for trusting. Cymbeline's line about the Queen, "It had been vicious / To have mistrusted her," is the play's strange ethics in one sentence: you will be wrong about people, you will be wrong in ways that nearly kill the ones you love, and the alternative, a life of suspicion, is worse. Iachimo, who trusts nothing and tests everything, is the one who ends on his knees.

The second thing is the shape of Posthumus's repentance. The play arranges for him to forgive Imogen while he still believes her guilty, and only afterwards to learn she was innocent. If his forgiveness had come after the truth, it would have cost him nothing. As it is, he forgives "wrying but a little" in a woman he thinks betrayed him, and then, in prison, offers his life for hers to gods he does not expect to answer. That he also strikes her to the ground in the final scene is not an accident of plotting; it is the play's refusal to let the reformed man be safe. Forgiveness in this play is a repeated act, not a state, and the man who has just said "Pardon's the word to all" is a man who, five minutes earlier, knocked down a page.

What is unresolved is whether any of the good fortune was earned. Jupiter says "Whom best I love I cross," which is the comfortable version. Posthumus, waking, says he has "this golden chance, and know not why," and that many who deserve nothing "are steep'd in favours." The ending is engineered by a doctor who disobeyed, a servant who disobeyed, a nurse who stole children, and a drug that happened not to be fatal; the oracle is explained by a pun. You can read the last scene as providence or as luck dressed in a god's robe, and the play gives you Jupiter's thunder and Posthumus's shrug side by side. What it insists on, through the dirge, is that all of it ends in the same dust; what it chooses, through the pardons and the paid tribute, is that while the dust is still standing up, mercy and submission are the moves worth making. It does not claim they are rewarded. It claims they are "the word."

## Preoccupations

- the gap between seeming and being; clothes and tokens taken for the person
- misjudging the people closest to you, and whether trust is a fault
- slander and false report as weapons sharper than swords
- nature showing through nurture; blood versus rank
- true service as disobedience; faithful lying
- court as corruption, wilderness as school, and the pull back to court
- death as leveller, and the living reasserting distinctions
- forgiveness chosen by the strong; providence versus luck
