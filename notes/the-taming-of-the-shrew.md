# The Taming of the Shrew

## What happens

A lord finds a drunken tinker, Christopher Sly, asleep outside an alehouse and, for sport, has him carried to bed, dressed as a nobleman and told by everyone that he has been a lord all along who dreamed he was a beggar; a play is then performed for him. In that play, a rich Paduan father will not let anyone marry his gentle younger daughter Bianca until someone takes the elder, Katherina, whose sharp tongue has made her famous as a shrew. Petruchio, come from Verona "to wive it wealthily," agrees to marry her for the dowry, woos her by contradicting everything she says, turns up late and in rags to the wedding, carries her off before the feast, and then at his country house denies her food, sleep and clothes while insisting it is all done out of love, until she agrees to call the sun the moon if he says so. Meanwhile Bianca's suitors disguise themselves as tutors, a servant impersonates his master, a stranger is hired to impersonate the master's father, and the real father, arriving unannounced, is nearly jailed as an impostor. At the closing feast the three new husbands wager on whose wife will come when sent for; only Katherina comes, and she delivers a long speech on the duty wives owe their husbands. In this text the Sly frame is not closed; he is last heard nodding at the play's start.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **People can be talked into being someone else if everyone around them agrees to the story.**
   - *Where spoken:* The Lord, Induction 1: "Would not the beggar then forget himself?" The Huntsman: "He is no less than what we say he is." Sly, Induction 2: "Am I a lord? and have I such a lady? / Or do I dream? ... Upon my life, I am a lord indeed; / And not a tinker, nor Christophero Sly."
   - *What the action does with it:* Sly holds out for a dozen lines ("Am not I Christopher Sly, old Sly's son of Burton-heath") and then gives in to the weight of unanimous testimony. The main play repeats the technique on Katherina: Petruchio tells her she is "pleasant, gamesome, passing courteous" (II.1) when she is raging, and in IV.5 insists the sun is the moon until she says "What you will have it nam'd, even that it is." The frame and the play are the same experiment run twice, once on a man for a joke and once on a woman for a marriage. Because Sly never wakes in this text, the play leaves unsaid whether such a transformation can be undone.

2. **Marriage is openly traded as merchandise, and the trade is gamed at every point.**
   - *Where spoken:* Petruchio, I.2: "I come to wive it wealthily in Padua; / If wealthily, then happily in Padua." Baptista, II.1: "'Tis deeds must win the prize, and he of both / That can assure my daughter greatest dower / Shall have my Bianca's love," and "now I play a merchant's part, / And venture madly on a desperate mart." Petruchio, III.2: "She is my goods, my chattels; she is my house, / My household stuff, my field, my barn."
   - *What the action does with it:* The auction for Bianca is won by a servant in his master's clothes bidding with ships and houses that do not exist, and the "assurance" Baptista insists on is signed by a hired stranger pretending to be the groom's father. The one honest bidder, old Gremio, is outbid by fiction: "My cake is dough" (V.1). The actual marriage happens by elopement "without asking my good will" (V.1). The play shows the market in daughters as something fathers trust and young people simply route around.

3. **Cruelty can be carried out "under name of perfect love," and the label makes it harder to resist.**
   - *Where spoken:* Petruchio, IV.1: "This is a way to kill a wife with kindness; / And thus I'll curb her mad and headstrong humour." Katherina, IV.3: "Am starv'd for meat, giddy for lack of sleep; / With oaths kept waking, and with brawling fed. / And that which spites me more than all these wants, / He does it under name of perfect love."
   - *What the action does with it:* Petruchio states his method as a falconer's: "My falcon now is sharp and passing empty, / And till she stoop she must not be full-gorg'd." He throws out the meat as burnt, the cap as a "baby's cap," the gown as "masquing stuff," always claiming to protect her. His own servants see it plainly: "He kills her in her own humour" (Peter, IV.1); "By this reckoning he is more shrew than she" (Curtis, IV.1). The method works; the play confirms it without disguising what it is.

4. **A reputation for being difficult is partly made by how a person is treated.**
   - *Where spoken:* Katherina, I.1: "I pray you, sir, is it your will / To make a stale of me amongst these mates?" II.1: "She is your treasure, she must have a husband; / I must dance bare-foot on her wedding-day, / And, for your love to her, lead apes in hell." III.2, when Petruchio is late: "Now must the world point at poor Katherine, / And say 'Lo! there is mad Petruchio's wife.'"
   - *What the action does with it:* Baptista openly prefers Bianca ("Go ply thy needle; meddle not with her"), bargains Katherina away without asking her, and does not blame her for weeping. Meanwhile the "mild" Bianca lies to her tutors, elopes, mocks her husband at the feast ("The more fool you for laying on my duty"), and refuses to come when called; the Widow does the same. By the end the labels "shrew" and "modest" have swapped bodies. Lucentio's closing line is doubt: "'Tis a wonder, by your leave, she will be tam'd so" (V.2).

5. **Being able to speak one's mind is the last thing a person gives up, and the taming does not take it away; it redirects it.**
   - *Where spoken:* Katherina, IV.3: "My tongue will tell the anger of my heart, / Or else my heart, concealing it, will break; / And rather than it shall, I will be free / Even to the uttermost, as I please, in words."
   - *What the action does with it:* Katherina's final speech (V.2) is the longest in the play. She is not silenced; she is given the floor and uses it to say "place your hands below your husband's foot." The play has already shown her saying what she does not believe ("I know it is the moon," IV.5, and then "it is the blessed sun" a line later). The reader is left with a speech that is either converted belief, learned performance, or both, and the text gives no aside to settle it.

6. **Clothes make the man socially, whatever a man says about it.**
   - *Where spoken:* Petruchio, III.2: "To me she's married, not unto my clothes." IV.3: "For 'tis the mind that makes the body rich; / And as the sun breaks through the darkest clouds, / So honour peereth in the meanest habit." Lucentio, I.1: "Nor can we be distinguish'd by our faces / For man or master."
   - *What the action does with it:* Tranio in his master's hat and cloak is taken for Lucentio by Baptista, by the suitors, and by the city; the real Vincentio, seeing "A silken doublet, a velvet hose, a scarlet cloak" on his servant, is called a lunatic and ordered to jail. Petruchio can afford to wear rags because everyone already knows who he is; Tranio needs the clothes because nobody does. The play states the moral and demonstrates its limits in the same act.

7. **People study what they already want to learn; formal education becomes a cover for desire.**
   - *Where spoken:* Lucentio, I.1, arriving to study "that part of philosophy ... that treats of happiness / By virtue specially to be achiev'd." Tranio: "No profit grows where is no pleasure ta'en; / In brief, sir, study what you most affect." Bianca, III.1: "I'll not be tied to hours nor 'pointed times, / But learn my lessons as I please myself."
   - *What the action does with it:* Lucentio abandons philosophy before the scene ends ("I burn, I pine, I perish"). The tutors are suitors, the Latin lesson is a love letter, the music lesson is a proposal in solfège, and the only real school in the play is the "taming-school" where "Petruchio is the master" (IV.2). Tranio's cynical maxim is the one that comes true.

8. **Obedience is paid for in cash.**
   - *Where spoken:* Petruchio, V.2: "Twenty crowns! / I'll venture so much of my hawk or hound, / But twenty times so much upon my wife." Baptista: "The wager thou hast won; and I will add / Unto their losses twenty thousand crowns; / Another dowry to another daughter, / For she is chang'd, as she had never been."
   - *What the action does with it:* The wife who comes when called earns her husband a hundred crowns and a second dowry; the wives who married for love or for "kindness" (Hortensio, IV.2) cost their husbands money. The play ends by converting Katherina's change into a sum. Petruchio names what he thinks he has bought: "peace it bodes, and love, and quiet life, / An awful rule, and right supremacy."

9. **A stranger's truth counts for less than a resident's lie.**
   - *Where spoken:* Vincentio, V.1: "Thus strangers may be haled and abus'd: O monstrous villain!"
   - *What the action does with it:* Baptista, Tranio and the Pedant all stand on the fiction they have agreed to; Gremio, who suspects the truth, will not swear to it ("Nay, I dare not swear it"). The real father is saved only because his son happens to walk in. Truth prevails by accident, not by evidence.

10. **The body can be used to break the will; hunger and sleeplessness do what argument cannot.**
   - *Where spoken:* Grumio, IV.1: "winter tames man, woman, and beast." Katherina, IV.3: "What, did he marry me to famish me? / Beggars that come unto my father's door / Upon entreaty have a present alms ... But I, who never knew how to entreat ... Am starv'd for meat."
   - *What the action does with it:* The wooing scene (II.1) is a draw in wit; Katherina gives as good as she gets. What changes her is not Petruchio's cleverness but two days without food or rest. The play is honest that the victory is physical.

## What it teaches

The play tells you how the trick is done and then does it in front of you. Petruchio's falconry speech is a set of instructions, delivered to the audience, for reducing a person by deprivation and relabelling until she will say anything; he even asks whether anyone knows a better way. The Induction has already shown the same method practised on a drunk for a laugh. What is hard about the play is that it does not pretend the method fails or that Katherina sees through it; she stops contradicting him, she calls the sun the moon, she comes when sent for, she lectures the other wives. The play lets the technique succeed and lets everyone on stage cheer.

What the text refuses to give you is Katherina's interior after IV.5. The one speech in which she tells us what she is feeling, in IV.3, is a complaint ("He does it under name of perfect love"), and after that she speaks only in the terms he has set. Her final speech is eloquent and complete and comes a scene after she has demonstrated that she can say "I know it is the moon" on command. So you cannot tell from the words whether she believes them, and the play does not help you. Lucentio's last line, "'Tis a wonder ... she will be tam'd so," is the play's own hesitation, placed after the applause.

Around this central difficulty the play scatters a cooler lesson about labels. "Curst Katherine" and "modest Bianca" are reputations assigned by a father and a town; by the last scene the modest one is the one who will not come, and the curst one is the one who does. The play suggests that what people call your nature is largely what their treatment of you has produced, and that it can be reversed in either direction by a change of treatment. That is a comfort and a threat in equal measure. The Sly frame, left open, makes the threat general: any of us, told the same story by enough people, might forget who we were.

## Preoccupations

- identity as something imposed from outside by consistent treatment
- marriage as a market and daughters as goods
- the will broken through the body: hunger, sleep, cold
- cruelty renamed as care, and how the renaming disarms resistance
- reputation and labels ("shrew", "modest") as made by others and reversible
- disguise, and clothes as the thing that decides who is believed
- speech as freedom, and the question of whether forced speech can become belief
- the stranger disbelieved and the resident impostor trusted
