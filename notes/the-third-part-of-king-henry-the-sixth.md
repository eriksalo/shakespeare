# The Third Part of King Henry the Sixth

## What happens
York sits in Henry's throne; Henry, admitting aside that "my title's weak," bargains to keep the crown for his lifetime and leave it to York's heirs, disinheriting his own son. Margaret refuses the bargain and raises the north. In the fighting that follows, York's boy Rutland is murdered by Clifford for his father's sake, York himself is mocked with a paper crown and a handkerchief dipped in his son's blood before being stabbed by Clifford and Margaret, and Henry, chased from his own battle at Towton, sits on a molehill wishing he were a shepherd while a son carries in the father he has killed and a father the son. Edward of York takes the crown, then throws away the French alliance Warwick is negotiating for him by marrying a widow he had failed to seduce. Warwick, "setter up and puller down of kings," switches to Margaret out of wounded pride; Clarence switches with him and then switches back; the crown changes hands four times. Henry is captured, freed, recaptured. Warwick dies at Barnet with "nothing left me but my body's length"; at Tewkesbury the three York brothers stab Margaret's son in front of her; Richard of Gloucester rides to the Tower and stabs Henry while he prophesies. Edward ends the play on his throne with a new baby and the words "here, I hope, begins our lasting joy," while Richard, having kissed the infant, says aside "so Judas kissed his master."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **An oath binds only until the swearer finds a reason, and a reason is always available.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Edward, 1.2: "But for a kingdom any oath may be broken. / I would break a thousand oaths to reign one year." Richard, 1.2: "An oath is of no moment, being not took / Before a true and lawful magistrate." Clarence, 5.1: "To keep that oath were more impiety / Than Jephthah's when he sacrificed his daughter." Margaret to Clarence, 5.5: "thou usest to forswear thyself. / 'Twas sin before, but now 'tis charity."
   - *What the action does with it:* Henry swears to York; York swears to Henry; both are broken within the act. Clarence swears to Warwick, gives him "my faith irrevocable," then throws the red rose in his face with "I will henceforth be no more unconstant" — and the prince he helps stab calls him "perjured George" with his last breath. The two Keepers who arrest Henry have a cleaner formula than any lord: "we were subjects but while you were king" (3.1). The play contains no oath that holds and no one who expects one to.

2. **Right is what the stronger side says it is, and everyone knows this including the people claiming right.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Henry, aside, 1.1: "I know not what to say; my title's weak." Prince Edward, 2.2: "If that be right which Warwick says is right, / There is no wrong, but everything is right." Warwick, uncrowning Edward, 4.3: "Then for his mind be Edward England's king; / But Henry now shall wear the English crown." Edward, 4.7: "When we grow stronger, then we'll make our claim."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Mayor of York declares "we owe allegiance unto Henry," is told Edward claims only his dukedom, opens the gates, and is relieved of his keys. Montgomery refuses to fight for a duke, so Edward is proclaimed king on the spot by a soldier reading from a paper. The title Edward "forgets" in one line is "my right" twenty lines later. The play shows legitimacy being manufactured in real time by whoever has the drums.

3. **The crowd goes to the sun, and the one who was kind to it is the first abandoned.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Clifford, dying, 2.6: "The common people swarm like summer flies; / And whither fly the gnats but to the sun?" Henry with a feather, 3.1: "Commanded always by the greater gust, / Such is the lightness of you common men." Henry, 4.8: "My pity hath been balm to heal their wounds... Then why should they love Edward more than me?... when the lion fawns upon the lamb, / The lamb will never cease to follow him."
   - *What the action does with it:* The next sound after Henry's speech is a shout of "A York! A York!" and Edward's soldiers seize him. The play places Henry's catalogue of his own mercies directly before the proof that mercy earned nothing. It does not sneer at the catalogue; everything in it is true. It simply shows that gratitude is not a political force.

4. **Revenge is inherited, compounds, and cannot be settled because every payment is a new debt.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Clifford to Rutland, 1.3: "Thy father slew my father; therefore die." Rutland: "Thou hast one son; for his sake pity me, / Lest in revenge thereof, sith God is just, / He be as miserably slain as I." Warwick, 2.6: "Measure for measure must be answered." Margaret over her son's body, 5.5: "You have no children, butchers; if you had, / The thought of them would have stirred up remorse. / But if you ever chance to have a child, / Look in his youth to have him so cut off."
   - *What the action does with it:* The chain is exact and the play makes you count it: Clifford's father (killed by York in the previous play) buys Rutland; Rutland buys York; York buys Clifford; Clifford's head replaces York's on the gates. The sons of York stab Margaret's son because their father and brother died, and Margaret curses their children. Clifford had said of the Yorks "till I root out their accursed line / And leave not one alive, I live in hell" — revenge as a condition with no exit, and he dies in it.

5. **Pity is called weakness by every active person in the play, and it is the only thing the play finally honours.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Clifford, 2.2: "this too much lenity / And harmful pity must be laid aside." Clifford, 2.6: "what makes robbers bold but too much lenity?" Margaret to Northumberland weeping at York's grief, 1.4: "Think but upon the wrong he did us all, / And that will quickly dry thy melting tears." Edward, 5.5: "Hold, Richard, hold; for we have done too much."
   - *What the action does with it:* Pity appears in flashes and is overruled every time. Northumberland weeps and then stands by while York is stabbed. Edward stops Richard from killing Margaret but has just stabbed her son. Clifford, dying, concedes the logic of his own creed: "The foe is merciless and will not pity, / For at their hands I have deserved no pity." The only character whose pity is never overruled is Henry, and he is murdered by the one person who announces "I that have neither pity, love, nor fear."

6. **The crown promises heaven and delivers fear; the shepherd's hawthorn is sweeter than the canopy.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard, 1.2: "How sweet a thing it is to wear a crown, / Within whose circuit is Elysium." Henry on the molehill, 2.5: "Gives not the hawthorn bush a sweeter shade / To shepherds looking on their silly sheep / Than doth a rich embroidered canopy / To kings that fear their subjects' treachery?" Henry, 3.1: "My crown is called content; / A crown it is that seldom kings enjoy." Warwick, dying, 5.2: "Why, what is pomp, rule, reign, but earth and dust?"
   - *What the action does with it:* The play puts a paper crown on York's head at the molehill (1.4) and then sits Henry on another molehill (2.5) counting the hours of a shepherd's day. Everyone who gets the crown gets no rest: Edward vows "Never to lie and take his natural rest / Till Warwick or himself be quite suppressed," and his closing "lasting joy" is undercut by Richard's aside in the same breath. Henry's shepherd fantasy is the one picture of peace in the play, and he can only have it by not being what he is.

7. **Civil war is a father killing a son and a son killing a father, and neither chose his side.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Son, 2.5: "From London by the King was I pressed forth; / My father, being the Earl of Warwick's man, / Came on the part of York, pressed by his master; / And I, who at his hands received my life, / Have by my hands of life bereaved him." The Father: "O boy, thy father gave thee life too soon, / And hath bereft thee of thy life too late!" Henry: "Whiles lions war and battle for their dens, / Poor harmless lambs abide their enmity."
   - *What the action does with it:* The two men enter looking for gold on the corpses — "Ill blows the wind that profits nobody" — and find their own blood. They were pressed; the quarrel was never theirs. Henry's gloss is accurate and useless: he is the king, and he cannot stop it. The scene is placed in the exact middle of the play, as if to show what all the pedigree-arguments of Act 1 and all the oath-breaking of Act 5 cost at ground level.

8. **A man can read his own body as a fate and then choose to become it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard, 3.2: "Love forswore me in my mother's womb... And am I then a man to be beloved?... since this earth affords no joy to me / But to command, to check, to o'erbear... I'll make my heaven to dream upon the crown." Richard, 5.6: "since the heavens have shaped my body so, / Let hell make crooked my mind to answer it... I am myself alone."
   - *What the action does with it:* Henry's dying prophecy supplies the omens (owl, night-crow, teeth at birth), and Richard, having stabbed him, confirms them: "Indeed, 'tis true that Henry told me of." The play lets him be both fated and self-choosing — he decides to accept the reading of his body that licenses what he already wants. "I can smile, and murder while I smile" is said as a discovery of talent, not a confession. He is the only character who says exactly what he is, and no one in the play is listening.

9. **Tears are treated as the opposite of action, and the play keeps showing that they are not.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard, 2.1: "To weep is to make less the depth of grief: / Tears, then, for babes; blows and revenge for me!" Warwick, 2.3: "Why stand we like soft-hearted women here, / Wailing our losses whiles the foe doth rage?" Margaret, 5.4: "wise men ne'er sit and wail their loss, / But cheerly seek how to redress their harms."
   - *What the action does with it:* York's tears over Rutland's napkin are what move Northumberland — "his passion moves me so / That hardly can I check my eyes from tears" — the only moment anyone on the Lancastrian side softens. Margaret, who mocked York for weeping ("I prithee grieve to make me merry"), ends the play begging to be killed over her son's body: "my heart will burst an if I speak; / And I will speak, that so my heart may burst." The dry-eyed win; the play watches the weeping.

10. **A private appetite in a public man is a public event.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard, 4.1: "hasty marriage seldom proveth well." Warwick, 4.3: "how should you govern any kingdom / That know not how to use ambassadors, / Nor how to be contented with one wife, / Nor how to use your brothers brotherly?" Edward, 4.1: "I am Edward, / Your King and Warwick's, and must have my will."
   - *What the action does with it:* Edward tries to buy a widow's body with her husband's lands, fails ("too mean to be your queen, / And yet too good to be your concubine"), and marries her because she refused. The cost is the French alliance, Warwick, Clarence, and the crown for an act and a half. Lady Grey's refusal is the one act of integrity that succeeds in the play, and it succeeds by accident of a king's whim. The marriage that ends the play with a baby heir is the same marriage that nearly ended the dynasty.

11. **The man who makes kings does it for himself, and ends with a grave's length of ground.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Warwick, 3.3: "I was the chief that raised him to the crown, / And I'll be chief to bring him down again: / Not that I pity Henry's misery, / But seek revenge on Edward's mockery." Warwick dying, 5.2: "My parks, my walks, my manors that I had, / Even now forsake me; and of all my lands / Is nothing left me but my body's length."
   - *What the action does with it:* Warwick's motive is stated plainly and it is personal honour, not Henry's right or Edward's. Both kings call him by his function — "Proud setter up and puller down of kings" — and the play lets him be exactly that until Edward asks the question that ends him: "What is the body when the head is off?" He had the head, lost it by carelessness, and dies asking "who is victor, York or Warwick?" — not Henry or Edward. He never thought in those terms.

12. **A good man without power can only withdraw, prophesy, and forgive; the play lets him do all three and shows what they are worth.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Henry, 4.6: "I here resign my government to thee... While I myself will lead a private life / And in devotion spend my latter days." Henry on Richmond: "This pretty lad will prove our country's bliss." Henry to Richard, 5.6: "'Tis sin to flatter; 'good' was little better: / 'Good Gloucester' and 'good devil' were alike." His last words: "O God, forgive my sins, and pardon thee!"
   - *What the action does with it:* His withdrawal hands government to two men who lose it in a scene. His prophecy about Richmond is the one thing in the play that points past the play. His refusal to flatter Richard is the only clean speech anyone makes to Richard's face. And his forgiveness is answered with a second stab: "Down, down to hell; and say I sent thee thither." The play does not make Henry's goodness effective. It makes it the only thing in the room that is not for sale.

## What it teaches

This play is the most relentless of the three, and what it teaches is about the mechanics of a war in which both sides have stopped believing their own reasons. By Act 2 nobody argues pedigree anymore; they argue who fled at St Albans and who killed whose father. The question "who is the rightful king?" is answered four separate times by whoever has just won, and the characters know it — the young prince says it out loud: if Warwick's word is right, then "everything is right." Once right is detached from anything but force, the only remaining logic is the one Clifford states to a child: "Thy father slew my father; therefore die." The play's great demonstration is that this logic, once entered, has no stopping point, because every act of revenge is also an injury, and every injury is owed. Margaret kills York for her son's sake, and the sons of York kill her son, and she curses their children, and Richard goes to the Tower. The reader who wants to know how feuds perpetuate themselves will find the machinery drawn here with no parts hidden.

Against this, the play sets three things that do not work. Henry's pity does not work; it is overruled, mocked by both his wife and his enemies, and ends with him stabbed mid-prophecy. The oath does not work; no one keeps one. And tears do not work, though they are the only thing that ever moves anyone — Northumberland cannot help weeping at York, and then helps kill him. The play's honesty is that it does not pretend these failures are instructive. It does not say Henry should have been harder; Clifford says that, and Clifford dies in the hell he chose. It does not say pity would have saved the realm; it shows the realm was lost by lenity and would have been lost by cruelty. It leaves you with Henry's molehill — a vision of ordered time, "so many hours must I tend my flock" — as the one picture of a livable life, and makes sure you notice that the man imagining it is a king who cannot have it.

What is hardest is Richard. He is the only person who tells the truth about himself, and the truth is "I am myself alone." The play lets you hear him decide to be what his body has been read as — "Let hell make crooked my mind to answer it" — and then kiss a baby. Everyone else in the play is at least partly deceived about their own motives: Warwick thinks he is about honour, Edward thinks he is about right, Margaret thinks she is about her son. Richard is not deceived. The play seems to say that the person with no illusions about himself is the most dangerous person in the room, and that the kingdom has, by the end, no one left who could recognise him. Henry could, and Henry is dead.

## Preoccupations

- oaths broken as soon as they are inconvenient
- legitimacy as whatever the winner declares
- revenge passed from father to son with no end
- pity scorned as weakness and shown as the only decency
- the crown as a burden that gives no rest
- ordinary people pressed into a quarrel not their own
- a man deciding to become what his body is taken to mean
- tears versus action, and which actually moves people
