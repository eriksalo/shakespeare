# The Sonnets

## What happens
The sequence opens with seventeen poems urging a beautiful young man to marry and breed so that his beauty will outlast him. From 18 the speaker drops that advice and offers his own verse as the young man's immortality instead. The middle hundred poems follow the speaker's love for the young man through absence, the young man's theft of the speaker's mistress (40-42), the speaker's self-abasement and self-excuse (35, 57-58, 88-89), a rival poet (78-86), a farewell (87), reproaches about the young man's "common" conduct (69-70, 93-96), the speaker's own confessed infidelities and public shame (109-112, 117-121), and a sequence of absolute declarations about unchanging love (105, 115-116, 123-125) that sit directly beside admissions of change. Sonnet 126, a twelve-line envoi, tells the "lovely boy" that Nature must finally render him up. From 127 the object is a dark-eyed, dark-haired mistress: the speaker anatomises lust (129), praises her by refusing to flatter (130), discovers that she has his friend too (133-134, 144), and ends in a knot of knowing self-deception: he swears her fair and knows she is not (137, 147-152). Two closing poems about Cupid's fire conclude that "water cools not love."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Beauty is lent, not given, and the only honest repayment is a child.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Nature's bequest gives nothing but doth lend" (4); "nothing 'gainst Time's scythe can make defence / Save breed to brave him" (12); "You had a father, let your son say so" (13).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The argument is pressed seventeen times and then dropped without being answered. Sonnet 17 proposes a compromise ("You should live twice,—in it, and in my rhyme"); 18 keeps only the rhyme. The young man never breeds within the poems, and the speaker never mentions the matter again. The sequence shows a practical remedy that would have served the beloved being quietly replaced by one that serves the speaker.

2. **Verse can defeat time; the sequence asserts this and cannot make it true.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "So long as men can breathe or eyes can see, / So long lives this, and this gives life to thee" (18); "My love shall in my verse ever live young" (19); "Not marble, nor the gilded monuments / Of princes shall outlive this powerful rhyme" (55); "His beauty shall in these black lines be seen" (63).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The lines preserve no face, name, or feature; what they preserve is the speaker's claim. The speaker knows it: "The age to come would say this poet lies" (17); "Theirs for their style I'll read, his for his love" (32); "Why is my verse so barren of new pride?" (76); "Alack what poverty my muse brings forth" (103). Sonnet 65 states the hope in the conditional: "O none, unless this miracle have might." And 126, the envoi to the whole young-man sequence, says Nature "may detain, but not still keep her treasure ... her quietus is to render thee." The sequence's last word on the boy's immortality is that he must be given up.

3. **Time takes back what it gave, and the taking is visible if you look.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Time that gave, doth now his gift confound" (60); "Ruin hath taught me thus to ruminate: / That Time will come and take my love away" (64); "That time of year thou mayst in me behold" (73).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* Confirmed throughout, most honestly in the speaker's own body: "beated and chopt with tanned antiquity" (62), "my days are past the best" (138). On the friend he looks away: "To me fair friend you never can be old" (104) — but the same poem admits "mine eye may be deceived," and the next-to-last young-man sonnet concedes that he "by waning grown" shows "Thy lovers withering" (126). The sequence refuses the consolation it keeps offering.

4. **Love abases the lover, and the abasement is not repaid.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Being your slave what should I do but tend, / Upon the hours, and times of your desire?" (57); "So true a fool is love, that in your will, / (Though you do any thing) he thinks no ill" (57); "Upon thy side, against my self I'll fight, / And prove thee virtuous, though thou art forsworn" (88); "To leave poor me, thou hast the strength of laws, / Since why to love, I can allege no cause" (49).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The speaker volunteers to lie about himself to justify being left (88-89), and the sequence shows no reward: the friend takes his mistress (40-42), "common" grows (69), and the speaker ends up "Like a deceived husband" who chooses not to know (93). The abasement is presented as love's logic and shown to be a trap the speaker builds himself.

5. **Loving someone makes you the accomplice of their wrongs against you.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Thy adverse party is thy advocate ... That I an accessary needs must be, / To that sweet thief which sourly robs from me" (35); "I do forgive thy robbery gentle thief" (40); "But here's the joy, my friend and I are one, / Sweet flattery, then she loves but me alone" (42).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The speaker names his own consolation "sweet flattery" in the act of offering it. Ninety sonnets later the same triangle recurs from the mistress's side and the flattery fails: "Him have I lost, thou hast both him and me, / He pays the whole, and yet am I not free" (134); "I guess one angel in another's hell" (144). The excuse made in 42 is withdrawn in 144, not by argument but by the passage of the sequence.

6. **Outer beauty and inner worth come apart, and those who can hurt and do not are the only owners of themselves.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "They that have power to hurt, and will do none ... They are the lords and owners of their faces, / Others, but stewards of their excellence ... Lilies that fester, smell far worse than weeds" (94); "How like Eve's apple doth thy beauty grow, / If thy sweet virtue answer not thy show" (93); "The soil is this, that thou dost common grow" (69).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The poems around 94 (93, 95, 96) make clear the friend is not the cold self-owner of 94 but the festering lily, and 105 then insists "Fair, kind, and true, is all my argument." The praise is constant; the object is not. The sequence lets the reader see that "Fair, kind, and true" describes the speaker's resolution to keep saying so.

7. **Absence is winter, and the one who complains of it was unfaithful during it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "How like a winter hath my absence been / From thee" (97); "Blessed are you whose worthiness gives scope, / Being had to triumph, being lacked to hope" (52).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* After the absence poems come the confessions: "I have gone here and there, / And made my self a motley to the view, / Gored mine own thoughts, sold cheap what is most dear" (110); "I have hoisted sail to all the winds / Which should transport me farthest from your sight" (117). The speaker then argues that his straying was a test of the friend's constancy (117) and that "ruined love when it is built anew / Grows fairer than at first" (119). The sequence allows him the argument and lets its self-service show.

8. **True love does not alter; and the sonnet that says so is surrounded by sonnets that say it does.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds ... it is an ever-fixed mark / That looks on tempests and is never shaken" (116). Also "I will be true despite thy scythe and thee" (123); "mutual render, only me for thee" (125).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The sonnet immediately before (115) says "Those lines that I before have writ do lie" and that love "still doth grow"; the ones after (117-120) confess wilful error and build a theory of mutual trespass ("Mine ransoms yours, and yours must ransom me", 120). The couplet of 116 stakes everything on a wager ("If this be error and upon me proved, / I never writ, nor no man ever loved"), and the dark-lady sonnets later prove the speaker a perjurer by his own account (152). The sequence does not refute 116; it shows that the person who wrote it could not live it, and that the writing was itself a way of trying.

9. **A man is what he is, and his judgement of that is the faculty love has corrupted.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "No, I am that I am, and they that level / At my abuses, reckon up their own" (121); "my nature is subdued / To what it works in, like the dyer's hand" (111).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* 121 claims the speaker's own sense of good over others' seeing. The mistress sonnets then show that sense broken: "In things right true my heart and eyes have erred" (137); "My thoughts and my discourse as mad men's are" (147); "O me! what eyes hath love put in my head" (148). The defiant self-possession of 121 cannot be squared with the self-diagnosed blindness of 137-150, and the sequence places them in the same voice.

10. **Lust is known to be hell and is pursued anyway; knowing changes nothing.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Th' expense of spirit in a waste of shame / Is lust in action ... Before a joy proposed behind a dream. / All this the world well knows yet none knows well, / To shun the heaven that leads men to this hell" (129).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The twenty-three sonnets after 129 enact its couplet. "Desire is death, which physic did except" (147); "flesh stays no farther reason" (151); and the closing pair: "Love's fire heats water, water cools not love" (154). The analysis in 129 is complete and does nothing, as it says it will.

11. **Love can be kept alive by two people lying to each other and knowing it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "When my love swears that she is made of truth, / I do believe her though I know she lies ... Therefore I lie with her, and she with me, / And in our faults by lies we flattered be" (138); "I have sworn thee fair: more perjured I, / To swear against the truth so foul a lie" (152).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The young-man half ends in the same place: "So shall I live, supposing thou art true, / Like a deceived husband" (93). Both loves conclude in a chosen not-knowing. The sequence does not resolve this; it presents mutual deception as a working arrangement and names the cost in the word "perjured."

12. **Love alters what the eye reports, and the lover calls the alteration different names depending on whom he loves.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Of the friend: "my most true mind thus maketh mine untrue" (113); "'tis flattery in my seeing ... If it be poisoned, 'tis the lesser sin" (114). Of the mistress: "Thou blind fool Love, what dost thou to mine eyes, / That they behold and see not what they see?" (137); "O cunning love, with tears thou keep'st me blind" (148).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The mechanism is identical; the valuation differs. For the friend it is alchemy and a lesser sin; for the mistress a plague. The sequence puts both in the same voice and lets the reader see that the difference is in the object, not the faculty.

13. **Praise is a market, and plain speech is a style, not a safeguard.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "I will not praise that purpose not to sell" (21); "You to your beauteous blessings add a curse, / Being fond on praise, which makes your praises worse" (84); "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun ... And yet by heaven I think my love as rare, / As any she belied with false compare" (130).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The rival-poet sonnets (78-86) show the speaker's plainness as a position taken under competition. 130's honesty about the mistress's looks is followed by "I have sworn thee fair, and thought thee bright, / Who art as black as hell" (147). Refusing false comparison did not prevent false swearing. The sequence separates truthful style from truthful seeing.

14. **The world is unbearable and one stays in it for someone; and what one wants after death keeps changing.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Tired with all these, from these would I be gone, / Save that to die, I leave my love alone" (66); "No longer mourn for me when I am dead ... remember not / The hand that writ it" (71); "Your monument shall be my gentle verse ... Though I (once gone) to all the world must die" (81).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* The speaker wishes for death (66), asks to be forgotten (71-72), insists his spirit stays in the line (74), grants the friend immortality and himself a common grave (81), and finally, in the one religious sonnet, turns to the soul's victory over death (146). These are not reconciled. The sequence shows a man's relation to his own death shifting with his relation to the person he loves.

15. **The soul can be told to feed on death, and the body goes on regardless.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Poor soul the centre of my sinful earth ... So shall thou feed on death, that feeds on men, / And death once dead, there's no more dying then" (146).
   - *What the sequence does with it:* 147 begins "My love is as a fever longing still." The one sonnet that sets body against soul and chooses the soul is wedged between lust sonnets and has no effect on what follows: "My soul doth tell my body that he may, / Triumph in love" (151). The sequence places the religious resolution where it can be seen not to take.

## What it teaches

The sequence is a record of a man saying things about love that are true as statements and that he cannot live, and the record is honest enough to show both. Sonnet 116 is the clearest case: it is right about what love should be, and it is written by someone who, two poems later, is confessing that he "hoisted sail to all the winds." If you read the sonnets looking for a stable teaching, you will not find one; what you find is a mind that keeps making absolute claims — verse will outlive marble, love does not alter, I am that I am — and a sequence that lets each claim stand next to the evidence against it. The lesson is not that the claims are false. It is that making them is one of the things people do inside love, and that the making is not the same as the being.

The hardest thing the sonnets know is how willingly a person becomes complicit in being wronged. The speaker calls himself an "accessary" to the friend's theft (35), forgives the "gentle thief" (40), builds a consolation out of "my friend and I are one" (42), and then, with the mistress, finds the same triangle from the other side and watches the consolation fail (134, 144). He lies to her and she to him and he says so (138) and continues. There is no moment where the sequence steps outside this and judges it; the nearest is 129, which judges lust in general and ends by saying that the judgement will not help. What a reader is left with is the sight of someone who knows exactly what is happening to him and whose knowledge is not a remedy.

What the sonnets do earn, against all this, is the right to say that the writing was worth something. Not because it made anyone immortal — the lines preserve no face — but because it was a way of being faithful when the man was not. "Why write I still all one, ever the same" (76) is a question the sequence answers by continuing. If there is a lesson, it is that constancy may be something you do with words when you cannot do it otherwise, and that this is both a real thing and a confession.

## Preoccupations

- time as the thing that undoes everything, including the beloved and the speaker
- the attempt to defeat time by verse, and the knowledge that it fails
- love as self-abasement that the lover chooses and cannot stop
- becoming the accomplice of the person who wrongs you
- the gap between what the speaker swears and what he knows
- lust known as hell and pursued regardless
- love's corruption of sight, valued differently by its object
- constancy as something done in words when it cannot be done in life
- beauty and inner worth coming apart
