# King Richard the Third

## What happens
Richard of Gloucester tells the audience in the first speech that, since he cannot be a lover, he is "determined to prove a villain," and then does exactly what he says. He gets his brother Clarence imprisoned by a forged prophecy and murdered in a wine-butt; he woos Lady Anne over the corpse of the king he killed, whose son he also killed, and wins her; he lets the dying King Edward's reconciliation of the court stand for a morning before announcing Clarence's death into it. When Edward dies, Richard and Buckingham separate the boy-king from his mother's family, execute the family at Pomfret, send the boy and his brother to the Tower, behead Hastings for saying "if," and stage a show in which Richard, standing between two bishops with a prayer-book, is begged by a silent London to take the crown. Crowned, he has the princes smothered, lets Anne die, refuses Buckingham his promised earldom, and tries to woo Edward's widow for her daughter. Old Queen Margaret, who cursed everyone in Act 1, watches each curse come true. The night before Bosworth the ghosts of eleven of Richard's victims tell him to "despair and die" and tell Richmond to "live and flourish"; Richard wakes to find his conscience has "a thousand several tongues," overrides it ("Conscience is but a word that cowards use"), fights hard, and is killed. Richmond ends the play promising to unite the white rose and the red.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A man can announce exactly what he is, and the people around him will still see what they prefer.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard, 1.3: "I clothe my naked villany / With odd old ends stol'n forth of Holy Writ, / And seem a saint when most I play the devil." Clarence, to his murderers, 1.4: "O no, he loves me, and he holds me dear." Hastings, 3.4, minutes before his arrest: "I think there's never a man in Christendom / Can lesser hide his love or hate than he, / For by his face straight shall you know his heart."
   - *What the action does with it:* Hastings is beheaded in the same scene. Clarence is stabbed by men who have just told him "Your brother Gloucester hates you." The young Prince, warned by Richard against "false friends," replies "but they were none" — and is right about his uncles, and wrong about the man warning him. Those who do see — Margaret, the Duchess ("Ah, that deceit should steal such gentle shape") — are called lunatic or waved away. The play's steady point is that sight is not the problem; people are shown Richard and decline to look.

2. **Words can win what no deed could earn — once.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard after Anne, 1.2: "Was ever woman in this humour wooed? / Was ever woman in this humour won?... Having God, her conscience, and these bars against me, / And I no friends to back my suit at all, / But the plain devil and dissembling looks?" Anne, 4.1: "my woman's heart / Grossly grew captive to his honey words, / And proved the subject of mine own soul's curse."
   - *What the action does with it:* The first wooing succeeds and the play lets us see it as monstrous and as a feat. The second wooing (4.4), of Elizabeth for her daughter, uses the same moves — "Plead what I will be, not what I have been" — and Richard exits crowing "Relenting fool, and shallow, changing woman!" But Stanley reports in the next scene that "the Queen hath heartily consented / He should espouse Elizabeth" — Richmond, not Richard. The trick that worked on a grieving widow does not work on a mother who has learned what Richard's words are worth. The play stages the limit of eloquence by letting the speaker think there is none.

3. **Conscience does not prevent the deed; it arrives afterward and unmakes the doer.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Second Murderer, 1.4: "'Tis a blushing shamefaced spirit that mutinies in a man's bosom... every man that means to live well endeavours to trust to himself and live without it." First Murderer: "Where's thy conscience now?" Second: "O, in the Duke of Gloucester's purse." Richard, 5.3: "O coward conscience, how dost thou afflict me!... My conscience hath a thousand several tongues, / And every tongue brings in a several tale, / And every tale condemns me for a villain."
   - *What the action does with it:* Clarence is killed anyway; the Second Murderer refuses his fee. Tyrrel's hired men "melted with tenderness" and smother the princes anyway. Richard, who had told the murderers "do not hear him plead," wakes at Bosworth to a tent full of pleading dead and answers his own conscience the next morning with "Conscience is but a word that cowards use, / Devised at first to keep the strong in awe." He is dead by evening. The play's observation is precise: conscience is real, universal, and nearly always late.

4. **A curse is a prophecy, and the ones who laugh at it become its proof.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Margaret, 1.3, cursing everyone in the room. Buckingham: "curses never pass / The lips of those that breathe them in the air." Buckingham's own oath of friendship to the Queen's kin, 2.1: "When I have most need to employ a friend... Deep, hollow, treacherous, and full of guile / Be he unto me." Buckingham, 5.1: "That high All-Seer which I dallied with / Hath turned my feigned prayer on my head / And given in earnest what I begged in jest."
   - *What the action does with it:* Every person Margaret curses dies naming her. Grey at Pomfret: "Now Margaret's curse is fall'n upon our heads." Hastings: "O Margaret, Margaret, now thy heavy curse / Is lighted on poor Hastings' wretched head." Anne, who cursed Richard's future wife, becomes her. Elizabeth, who scorned Margaret, asks her to "teach me how to curse." The play's structure is a ledger of curses paid, and it lets Margaret keep the book: "Edward for Edward pays a dying debt."

5. **The grace of princes is the least secure ground a person can stand on.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Hastings, 3.4: "O momentary grace of mortal men, / Which we more hunt for than the grace of God! / Who builds his hope in air of your good looks / Lives like a drunken sailor on a mast." Margaret, 1.3: "They that stand high have many blasts to shake them, / And if they fall they dash themselves to pieces." Brakenbury, 1.4: "Princes have but their titles for their glories, / An outward honour for an inward toil."
   - *What the action does with it:* Hastings's fall is prepared by two scenes of his crowing — "I hold my life as dear as you do yours, / And never in my days... Was it so precious to me as 'tis now" (3.2). Buckingham, "my other self," is dropped the moment he pauses for breath: "I am not in the giving vein today." Richard, the moment he is seated: "But shall we wear these glories for a day?" The play sets each character's highest confidence directly before his fall, as though the confidence were the signal.

6. **Everyone sees the lie, and no one says so.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Scrivener, 3.6: "Who is so gross / That cannot see this palpable device? / Yet who so bold but says he sees it not?" Buckingham on the Guildhall, 3.7: "like dumb statues or breathing stones, / Stared each on other, and looked deadly pale." The Mayor, 3.5: "your Grace's word shall serve / As well as I had seen and heard him speak."
   - *What the action does with it:* Richard is made king by "some ten voices" of Buckingham's own men and a Mayor who agrees to tell the citizens whatever he is told. The citizens in 2.3 already know: "Truly, the hearts of men are full of fear." The play shows a usurpation that needs no deception, only silence — and it shows the silence being given by people who understand exactly what they are watching.

7. **The tyrant's instruments are the first discarded, and they know it as it happens.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard, 4.2: "none are for me / That look into me with considerate eyes. / High-reaching Buckingham grows circumspect." Buckingham: "Made I him King for this? / O, let me think on Hastings, and be gone." Blunt, 5.2: "He hath no friends but what are friends for fear, / Which in his dearest need will fly from him."
   - *What the action does with it:* Buckingham's ghost states the arc in two lines: "The first was I that helped thee to the crown; / The last was I that felt thy tyranny." Stanley hangs back because his son is hostage, then "doth deny to come." Norfolk finds "Dickon thy master is bought and sold" pinned to his tent. Richard, who said "I am myself alone," ends with exactly that: "There is no creature loves me, / And if I die no soul will pity me."

8. **A king's wrath is served by courtiers who later wish they had resisted it, and the king blames them for not doing so.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Edward, 2.1: "Who sued to me for him? Who, in my wrath, / Kneeled at my feet, and bid me be advised?... But for my brother not a man would speak, / Nor I, ungracious, speak unto myself / For him, poor soul." "O God, I fear Thy justice will take hold / On me, and you, and mine and yours for this!"
   - *What the action does with it:* The speech is honest about the mechanism — nobody will cross a king in anger, and the king then discovers he wanted to be crossed — and the play confirms Edward's fear exactly: his sons, his wife's brothers, his friends all die. The scene also shows Richard had arranged for the countermand to arrive "too lag." Everyone in the room is guilty of silence; one of them is guilty of murder; the silence made the murder easy.

9. **"What is done cannot be amended" is the murderer's comfort, and the play tests whether the future can be sworn by.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard, 4.4: "Look what is done cannot be now amended. / Men shall deal unadvisedly sometimes, / Which after-hours gives leisure to repent." Elizabeth: "What canst thou swear by now?" Richard: "The time to come." Elizabeth: "That thou hast wronged in the time o'erpast."
   - *What the action does with it:* Elizabeth strips every oath from him in turn — his George, his Garter, his crown, the world, his father's death, himself, God — and each is shown already profaned. Richard's final oath is on his own success "As I intend to prosper and repent," and it is a self-curse like Buckingham's: "Myself myself confound! / Heaven and fortune bar me happy hours! / Day, yield me not thy light, nor, night, thy rest!" All of which the play delivers at Bosworth: no rest that night, no sun that morning.

10. **The victors of a civil war turn on each other, and a mother outlives the pieces.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Duchess, 2.4: "And being seated, and domestic broils / Clean over-blown, themselves, the conquerors / Make war upon themselves, brother to brother, / Blood to blood, self against self." The Duchess to Richard, 4.4: "Art thou my son?" and "A grievous burden was thy birth to me."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Duchess has three sons at the start; one kills the second and lets the third die of excess; she curses the survivor to his face: "Bloody thou art; bloody will be thy end." Clarence's last appeal is to their father's blessing — "He little thought of this divided friendship." Richmond's summary is the play's: "The brother blindly shed the brother's blood." The house of York, which won the previous three plays, destroys itself from inside in this one.

11. **The self that chose to be alone finds, at the end, that it is a courtroom.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard, 5.3: "What do I fear? Myself? There's none else by. / Richard loves Richard, that is, I am I. / Is there a murderer here? No. Yes, I am. / Then fly. What, from myself?... I am a villain. Yet I lie, I am not. / Fool, of thyself speak well. Fool, do not flatter."
   - *What the action does with it:* The man who could "frame my face to all occasions" has no face for himself. The play then does something stranger: it lets him override the speech, deliver a rousing oration, lose his horse, refuse Catesby's rescue — "I have set my life upon a cast, / And I will stand the hazard of the die" — and die fighting. Catesby: "The King enacts more wonders than a man." The play gives Richard courage at the end without giving him remorse, and refuses to make the courage count for anything.

12. **Deformity is offered as the reason for villainy and then dropped the moment it is contradicted.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Richard, 1.1: "since I cannot prove a lover... I am determined to prove a villain." Richard, 1.2, after Anne: "I do mistake my person all this while! / Upon my life, she finds, although I cannot, / Myself to be a marvellous proper man... Since I am crept in favour with myself, / I will maintain it with some little cost."
   - *What the action does with it:* The excuse lasts one scene. Once a woman has accepted him, the deformity stops being a reason — and the villainy does not stop. The play lets Richard use his body as a story about himself and then shows the story was never the cause. The mother's version is simpler and worse: "Tetchy and wayward was thy infancy... Thy age confirmed, proud, subtle, sly, and bloody, / More mild, but yet more harmful, kind in hatred."

13. **Lamentation does nothing and is the only thing the powerless have, and the play lets it count.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Duchess, 4.4: "Why should calamity be full of words?" Elizabeth: "Windy attorneys to their clients' woes... Let them have scope, though what they do impart / Help nothing else, yet do they ease the heart." Margaret: "I am hungry for revenge, / And now I cloy me with beholding it."
   - *What the action does with it:* The women cannot act; they sit on the ground and count their dead in turn — "I had an Edward, till a Richard killed him." But the Duchess's curse "My prayers on the adverse party fight" is followed by the ghosts, and the ghosts by Richmond's victory. The play gives the women's words a causal role it gives nobody else's. Richard tries to drown them with drums — "Let not the heavens hear these tell-tale women" — and the heavens, in the play's own machinery, hear.

## What it teaches

The play is first a demonstration that intelligence without any scruple is terrifyingly efficient — for about three acts. Richard is the only person who ever says what he intends, and his candour to the audience is the play's most seductive device; you are made complicit by being the one person he does not deceive. But the play is also careful to show what his method depends on, and it is not his cleverness. It is Hastings's vanity, Anne's grief, Clarence's trust, the Mayor's cowardice, the citizens' silence, and the courtiers' habit of not contradicting a king in a temper. Richard supplies the will; everyone else supplies the opportunity. The Scrivener's question — "Who is so gross / That cannot see this palpable device? / Yet who so bold but says he sees it not?" — is the play's indictment, and it is not of Richard.

The second thing the play works through is the economy of curses. Margaret's curses in 1.3 are a prophecy of the whole plot, and each person who dismissed them dies acknowledging them. Buckingham's oath of friendship becomes his death sentence; Anne's curse on Richard's wife becomes her own life. The play treats words as binding in a way the speakers do not intend, and it treats the future as a thing you have already spent. When Elizabeth strips Richard of every oath, ending with "the time to come — that thou hast wronged in the time o'erpast," she is stating the play's logic: there is no clean future to swear by when the past is this bloody. The ghosts at Bosworth are that logic made visible. Richmond's victory is not shown as earned by Richmond, who is barely a character; it is shown as the balance of a ledger closing.

What is hard is the last act of Richard's mind. "Richard loves Richard, that is, I am I" is the one time in the play he does not know what he means. The self that was "myself alone" turns out to contain a jury, and the man who gave everyone else their face has none for himself. The play lets him see this and then lets him refuse it — "Conscience is but a word that cowards use" — and then lets him fight with real courage and die. It does not reward the courage or soften the judgment. You are left with a man who understood everything about other people and nothing about himself, and with the uncomfortable knowledge that for three acts you enjoyed his company more than anyone else's.

## Preoccupations

- a villain who tells the truth about himself and is not believed
- the power of words to win what deeds forbid
- conscience as real but always too late
- curses and oaths that come true on the speaker
- the insecurity of a prince's favour
- silence as the public's share in a crime
- the discarding of those who served the tyrant
- a family destroying itself after winning
- the self as a tribunal that cannot be fled
