# Much Ado About Nothing

## What happens

Don Pedro and his officers return from a war to Leonato's house in Messina. Young Claudio falls for Leonato's daughter Hero and the Prince woos her for him in disguise; the match is made within a day. Benedick and Beatrice, who have a "merry war" of wit going and have each sworn off marriage, are tricked by their friends into believing that each is secretly dying of love for the other, and promptly fall in love. Don Pedro's bastard brother Don John, who wants only to spoil what his brother has made, has his man Borachio woo the waiting-woman Margaret at Hero's window while the Prince and Claudio watch from the orchard and take her for Hero. Claudio denounces Hero at the altar as a "rotten orange"; her father wishes her dead; she faints. The Friar proposes that she be reported dead so that slander turns to remorse. Beatrice, in grief and rage, asks Benedick to kill Claudio, and he challenges his friend. Meanwhile the night watch, under the constable Dogberry, has already overheard Borachio boasting of the plot and arrested him; his confession clears Hero. Claudio does penance at her supposed tomb and agrees to marry Leonato's "niece" sight unseen; she is Hero. Benedick and Beatrice, confronted with love-letters in their own hands, agree to marry. Don John is captured; the play ends with a dance and his punishment put off until tomorrow.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **People are wounded and won by hearsay more surely than by what they see; and what they see can be staged.**
   - Where it is spoken: Hero, III.1: "of this matter / Is little Cupid's crafty arrow made, / That only wounds by hearsay." Don John, III.2: "If you dare not trust that you see, confess not that you know." Claudio, IV.1: "Are our eyes our own?"
   - What the action does with it: The play runs the two methods side by side. Hearsay (the gulling scenes) produces a truth: Benedick and Beatrice really do love each other, as the letters in V.4 prove. Eyewitness (the window scene) produces a lie: Borachio says, "I have deceived even your very eyes" (V.1). The lesson is not "trust your ears" but that both channels are open to manipulation, and the only reliable reader in the play is the Friar, who goes by "noting of the lady," her blushes, and is right.

2. **Everyone can master a grief except the person who has it; counsel is worthless to the sufferer.**
   - Where it is spoken: Benedick, III.2: "everyone can master a grief but he that has it." Leonato, V.1: "men / Can counsel and speak comfort to that grief / Which they themselves not feel"; "there was never yet philosopher / That could endure the toothache patiently."
   - What the action does with it: Confirmed, and turned on the speaker. Leonato, who in IV.1 told his fainting daughter "Do not live, Hero; do not ope thine eyes," discovers in V.1 that he cannot take the patience his brother offers. His speech is the play's most honest moment about pain, and it comes from the man who added to Hero's. Antonio's reply, "Therein do men from children nothing differ," is allowed to stand.

3. **What we have we do not value until it is lost; and absence improves the image of the absent.**
   - Where it is spoken: The Friar, IV.1: "what we have we prize not to the worth / Whiles we enjoy it, but being lack'd and lost, / Why, then we rack the value, then we find / The virtue that possession would not show us."
   - What the action does with it: Partly confirmed, partly undercut. Claudio does say "Sweet Hero! now thy image doth appear / In the rare semblance that I lov'd it first" (V.1), but only after Borachio's confession, not because of her reported death. The Friar's plan does nothing by itself; Dogberry's watch does everything. Claudio then agrees to marry a woman he has never seen, "were she an Ethiope" (V.4). The play suggests that his love was always for an image, and that an image can be replaced.

4. **Men are inconstant by nature, and so are their vows.**
   - Where it is spoken: Balthasar's song, II.3: "Men were deceivers ever; / One foot in sea, and one on shore, / To one thing constant never." Beatrice, I.1: "he wears his faith but as the fashion of his hat; it ever changes with the next block." Benedick, V.4: "man is a giddy thing, and this is my conclusion."
   - What the action does with it: Claudio abandons Hero on a stranger's word, twice (he believes Don John's lie at the masque in II.1 before the window plot even begins). Benedick breaks his sworn bachelorhood within an hour of overhearing a conversation: "When I said I would die a bachelor, I did not think I should live till I were married" (II.3). The play treats giddiness as both the fault that nearly kills Hero and the grace that lets Benedick change. Constancy to an oath against love is not presented as a virtue.

5. **Wit is a defence against feeling, and it fails exactly when feeling becomes serious.**
   - Where it is spoken: Beatrice, II.1: "I thank it, poor fool, it keeps on the windy side of care"; "my mother cried; but then there was a star danced, and under that was I born." Don Pedro, V.1: "What a pretty thing man is when he goes in his doublet and hose and leaves off his wit!"
   - What the action does with it: In IV.1, after the broken wedding, the two wittiest people in the play stop joking. Beatrice: "O that I were a man! I would eat his heart in the market-place"; "manhood is melted into curtsies, valour into compliment, and men are only turned into tongue." Benedick, for once, speaks plainly: "I do love nothing in the world so well as you: is not that strange?" The play's most serious exchange is also its love scene, and it is where the wit goes out of the room. When wit returns in V.2 it is gentler ("Thou and I are too wise to woo peaceably").

6. **Love can be manufactured by a trick, or the trick may only release what was there.**
   - Where it is spoken: Don Pedro, II.1: "Cupid is no longer an archer: his glory shall be ours, for we are the only love-gods." Hero, III.1: "loving goes by haps: / Some Cupid kills with arrows, some with traps."
   - What the action does with it: The trap works. But in V.4, when both deny love "no more than reason," Claudio and Hero produce sonnets each wrote unprompted: "here's our own hands against our hearts." Leonato's "That eye my daughter lent her" claims the credit for the plotters; the letters claim it for the lovers. The play leaves the question of whether they were tricked into love or tricked into admitting it exactly open, and Benedick settles it by not caring: "I take thee for pity."

7. **Rank is taken for evidence; the clever are deceived and the fools find the truth.**
   - Where it is spoken: Leonato, IV.1: "Would the two princes lie? and Claudio lie...?" Borachio, V.1: "what your wisdoms could not discover, these shallow fools have brought to light."
   - What the action does with it: The Prince, the Count, and the governor all condemn Hero on the princes' word; the Friar and Beatrice, with no evidence but their reading of her, do not. Dogberry, who cannot speak a sentence straight and begs to be "writ down an ass," arrests the villains before the wedding; the truth arrives late only because Leonato was too busy to hear him (III.5). Judgment and authority are shown to be unrelated.

8. **"Seeming" is all anyone has to go on, and the same face reads opposite ways.**
   - Where it is spoken: Claudio, IV.1: "O! what authority and show of truth / Can cunning sin cover itself withal... Her blush is guiltiness, not modesty." Hero: "And seem'd I ever otherwise to you?" Claudio: "Out on thee! Seeming! I will write against it."
   - What the action does with it: Hero's blush is read by Claudio as guilt and by the Friar as "a thousand innocent shames / In angel whiteness" (IV.1). Nothing about her changes; the interpreters do. The play's title announces that the whole catastrophe is built on "noting," on what people take themselves to have observed, and shows how little that is worth without charity.

9. **Public accusation is easy and private repair is not; the accuser gets off lightly.**
   - Where it is spoken: Claudio, III.2, before seeing anything: "in the congregation, where I should wed, there will I shame her." Claudio, V.1: "yet sinn'd I not / But in mistaking." Leonato, V.1: "I thank you, princes, for my daughter's death: / Record it with your high and worthy deeds."
   - What the action does with it: Claudio's penance is an epitaph, a song, and a second bride. Leonato's bitterness is real but he settles for a marriage. The play does not punish the mistake it has spent two acts showing to be cruel and avoidable; it absorbs it into the comic ending. Whether the reader can forgive Claudio as readily as Hero does is left to the reader.

10. **Some people are malicious without any reason that would satisfy a reasonable person.**
    - Where it is spoken: Don John, I.3: "I cannot hide what I am: I must be sad when I have cause, and smile at no man's jests"; "it must not be denied but I am a plain-dealing villain"; II.2: "Any bar, any cross, any impediment will be medicinable to me."
    - What the action does with it: Don John is the one honest man about himself in the play and the only one with no interest in being loved. His motive is thin (Claudio "hath all the glory of my overthrow") and his method is cheap; he pays Borachio a thousand ducats and flees. The play does not make him interesting or explain him; it postpones him: "Think not on him till tomorrow" (V.4). Malice is treated as weather.

11. **Love is something one undergoes against one's will, and the will is what has to give.**
    - Where it is spoken: Beatrice, II.3: "Against my will I am sent to bid you come in to dinner." Benedick, V.2: "I do suffer love indeed, for I love thee against my will."
    - What the action does with it: Both lovers have built identities on refusal, and the plot works by making each believe the other has already surrendered. What they fear is not love but being the first to admit it. The play's resolution requires that each be given cover (the other's letter) so that neither has to yield alone. That is a precise picture of pride.

12. **A merry heart is a decision made in the face of knowledge, not an absence of it.**
    - Where it is spoken: Leonato on Beatrice, II.1: "she hath often dreamed of unhappiness and waked herself with laughing." Beatrice, II.1, on marriage: "wooing, wedding, and repenting is as a Scotch jig, a measure, and a cinquepace... till he sink into his grave."
    - What the action does with it: Beatrice sees exactly what marriage costs and jokes about it; then she marries. Benedick's last word is that he will not be flouted "out of my humour" for having changed his mind. The play's closing dance is proposed by the man who once said the married man should be painted on a sign, and he insists it come before the wedding. Cheerfulness here is chosen with open eyes.

## What it teaches

The play is built out of overhearing, and it wants you to notice how much of what you believe you got the same way. The lovers are made by a rumour and the bride is destroyed by one; nobody in Messina has first-hand knowledge of anything that matters except the watch, who stumble on it. What separates the good interpreters from the bad is not information but disposition. The Friar and Beatrice read Hero's face with goodwill and are right; Claudio and the Prince read it with suspicion already planted and are wrong. The difficult implication is that evidence is downstream of charity. If you have decided someone is false, their blush will prove it.

The second thing the play knows is that grief cannot be argued with. Leonato's speech in the fifth act, with its refusal of every proverb and philosopher, is placed just after we have watched him wish his own daughter dead; so the play lets the man who was cruel in his certainty be right in his pain. That is a hard combination to hold and the text holds it without comment. Everyone can master a grief but he that has it.

What is left unresolved is Claudio. The play lets him off, and the ending needs him to be let off, and the reader may find that the comic machinery has forgiven something it had no right to. The marriage he accepts is to a masked woman he is told is Hero's cousin, and he says yes "were she an Ethiope." You can read that as the proof of a chastened heart or as the proof that he never saw Hero at all. The text supports both, and Benedick, who was ready to kill him an act ago, ends by telling him to "live unbruised, and love my cousin." That is the real lesson of the play's title: much of what people make much of is nothing, and much of what they wave away is not.

## Preoccupations

- how belief is formed by hearsay, rumour, and staged appearances
- the gap between what people say they are and what they turn out to be
- the uselessness of advice to someone in pain
- wit as a shield that drops when feeling gets serious
- pride and the fear of being the first to admit love
- public shaming and how cheaply it is forgiven
- authority mistaken for truth; fools finding what the wise miss
- inconstancy as both the danger and the saving grace in people
