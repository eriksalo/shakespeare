# Twelfth Night; or, What You Will

## What happens

Duke Orsino of Illyria is in love with the Countess Olivia, who has sworn seven years of veiled mourning for her dead brother and will not see him. Viola, shipwrecked and believing her twin brother Sebastian drowned, disguises herself as a boy, "Cesario," enters Orsino's service, falls in love with him, and is sent to woo Olivia on his behalf; Olivia falls in love with the messenger. In Olivia's house her drunken uncle Sir Toby fleeces the foolish knight Sir Andrew, who hopes to marry her, and with the waiting-woman Maria, the servant Fabian and the clown Feste, revenges himself on the steward Malvolio, who has reproved their revels, by forging a letter in Olivia's hand that persuades Malvolio she loves him. Malvolio appears smiling, in yellow stockings and cross-garters, is taken for mad, and is locked in a dark room where Feste, as "Sir Topas," torments him. Sir Andrew is set on to duel Cesario; the duel is interrupted by Antonio, the sea-captain who rescued Sebastian and loves him, who mistakes Viola for her brother, is arrested for old offences against Orsino, and is denied by the person he thinks he saved. Sebastian, meanwhile, is mistaken for Cesario by everyone, beats Sir Toby and Sir Andrew, and is married on the spot by Olivia. In the final scene the twins stand side by side; Orsino, learning Cesario is a woman, takes her; Olivia keeps Sebastian; Malvolio is released, is told the trick, and leaves swearing revenge on "the whole pack of you." Feste sings that the rain it raineth every day.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Appetite destroys itself by indulgence; what is loved falls to a low price "even in a minute."**
   - Where it is spoken: Orsino, I.1: "If music be the food of love, play on, / Give me excess of it; that, surfeiting, / The appetite may sicken and so die... nought enters there, / Of what validity and pitch soever, / But falls into abatement and low price / Even in a minute!"
   - What the action does with it: Orsino describes himself exactly and does not know it. He insists in II.4 that no woman's heart is "so big, to hold so much" as his, then at the end transfers his love from Olivia to Viola in the space of a dozen lines: "I shall have share in this most happy wreck." Feste's diagnosis is the true one: "thy mind is a very opal" (II.4). The play lets the speech stand as a confession rather than a boast.

2. **Men say more and swear more; the love that says nothing is the constant one.**
   - Where it is spoken: Viola, II.4: "She never told her love, / But let concealment, like a worm i' th' bud, / Feed on her damask cheek... We men may say more, swear more, but indeed, / Our shows are more than will; for still we prove / Much in our vows, but little in our love."
   - What the action does with it: Confirmed by the whole shape of the play. Orsino's love is loud and moveable. Olivia's vow of seven years' mourning is broken within a scene of making it, and Feste demolishes it in a single catechism ("The more fool you, madonna, to mourn for your brother's soul being in heaven," I.5). Viola, who cannot speak, is the only lover whose object does not change. She is rewarded, but only by an accident of twins.

3. **Disguise is a wickedness that the wearer cannot undo; time, not the person, has to untangle it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Viola, II.2: "Disguise, I see thou art a wickedness / Wherein the pregnant enemy does much... O time, thou must untangle this, not I, / It is too hard a knot for me t'untie!" III.1: "I am not what I am."
   - What the action does with it: She is right that she cannot; Sebastian's arrival does all the untangling and she contributes nothing to it but survival. The play also shows disguise that is worn for the wearer's own benefit. Malvolio's yellow stockings are a costume adopted out of self-love and they destroy him. Feste's curate's gown is put on for a man in a dark room who cannot see it: "Thou mightst have done this without thy beard and gown. He sees thee not" (IV.2). Costume in this play is as much about who the wearer wants to be as about deceiving others.

4. **Love attaches to a surface in an instant, and what it gets is not what it saw.**
   - Where it is spoken: Olivia, I.5: "Even so quickly may one catch the plague? / Methinks I feel this youth's perfections / With an invisible and subtle stealth / To creep in at mine eyes." III.1: "Love sought is good, but given unsought is better."
   - What the action does with it: Olivia marries a man she has never spoken to before that day, because he has Cesario's face. Sebastian's response is not love but bewilderment: "Or I am mad, or else this is a dream... If it be thus to dream, still let me sleep!" (IV.1). The Duke's summary of the twins, "One face, one voice, one habit, and two persons! / A natural perspective, that is, and is not!" (V.1), is also a summary of what love does in Illyria. Sebastian's consolation to Olivia, "You would have been contracted to a maid; / Nor are you therein, by my life, deceived," is a comfort only if one takes the face for the person.

5. **Self-love is a sickness that distorts the taste, and other people will use it against you.**
   - Where it is spoken: Olivia, I.5: "O, you are sick of self-love, Malvolio, and taste with a distempered appetite." Maria, II.3: "it is his grounds of faith that all that look on him love him. And on that vice in him will my revenge find notable cause to work."
   - What the action does with it: The forged letter works because Malvolio has already imagined himself "Count Malvolio" before he finds it (II.5). But the play does not let the punishment match the fault. He is bound in darkness, called possessed, catechised by a false priest, and when released he has only "I'll be revenged on the whole pack of you." Olivia's verdict is "He hath been most notoriously abus'd." Even Sir Toby, mid-prank, says "I would we were well rid of this knavery" (IV.2). And the two lovers who run the play, Orsino ("I myself am best / When least in company," I.4) and Olivia with her inventory of her own beauty, are as sick of self-love as the steward.

6. **Virtue does not abolish cakes and ale, but the revel does not vindicate itself either.**
   - Where it is spoken: Sir Toby, II.3: "Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale?"
   - What the action does with it: The line is unanswerable as a line. But Toby's revels end with "a bloody coxcomb" from a man he took for a coward, a marriage to Maria "in recompense" for a forgery, and his turning on the friend he has bled for two thousand ducats: "An ass-head, and a coxcomb, and a knave, a thin-faced knave, a gull" (V.1). Sir Andrew's "I was adored once too" (II.3) is the saddest line in the play and it belongs to the revellers' victim. The festive side wins the argument and loses the evening.

7. **Madness is a verdict other people pass; the sane man can be kept in the dark and the whole town can act mad at liberty.**
   - Where it is spoken: Feste, IV.1: "Nothing that is so, is so." Sebastian, IV.1: "Are all the people mad?" Olivia, III.4: "I'm as mad as he, / If sad and merry madness equal be." Malvolio, IV.2: "I am not mad, Sir Topas. I say to you this house is dark." Feste: "there is no darkness but ignorance."
   - What the action does with it: Malvolio is the only person in the last two acts who correctly knows who he is and what is happening to him, and he is the one locked up. Everyone else is marrying, fighting, and denying strangers on the strength of a mistaken face. When his letter is read aloud, even Orsino says, "This savours not much of distraction." The play shows that "mad" is what the group calls whoever is not playing its game.

8. **Pleasure is paid for; time brings its revenges round; it rains every day.**
   - Where it is spoken: Feste, II.4: "pleasure will be paid one time or another." V.1: "And thus the whirligig of time brings in his revenges." The closing song: "For the rain it raineth every day."
   - What the action does with it: Malvolio's gibe at Feste in I.5 ("I marvel your ladyship takes delight in such a barren rascal") is quoted back to him word for word in V.1. Toby's drinking ends in a broken head. The last thing the audience hears, after three marriages, is a song about getting older and drunker in the rain, "But that's all one, our play is done." The play refuses to let its own happy ending be the last word.

9. **Ingratitude is the worst of vices, and the play's most generous lover is left unpaid.**
   - Where it is spoken: Viola, III.4: "I hate ingratitude more in a man / Than lying, vainness, babbling, drunkenness." Antonio: "In nature there's no blemish but the mind; / None can be call'd deform'd but the unkind." V.1: "His life I gave him, and did thereto add / My love, without retention or restraint."
   - What the action does with it: Antonio's charge of ingratitude is false (he is talking to the wrong twin) and the play's judgement of ingratitude is confirmed anyway, because at the end nobody thanks him. Sebastian cries "O my dear Antonio!" and then turns to his bride; Orsino, who called him "salt-water thief," says nothing of his release. He stands silent through the reunion. The man who loved "without retention or restraint" is the one person in the final tableau without a partner or a pardon.

10. **Friends flatter and foes tell the truth; so by foes one profits.**
    - Where it is spoken: Feste, V.1: "they praise me, and make an ass of me. Now my foes tell me plainly I am an ass: so that by my foes, sir, I profit in the knowledge of myself, and by my friends I am abused."
    - What the action does with it: Sir Toby is Sir Andrew's friend ("I have been dear to him, lad, some two thousand strong," III.2) and ruins him with flattery about his leg and his galliard. Malvolio's enemies tell him the truth about himself in the cruellest possible way. The play's one honest mirror is a fool asking for money.

11. **Youth is a stuff that will not endure; seize the day, and pay for it.**
    - Where it is spoken: Feste's song, II.3: "What is love? 'Tis not hereafter, / Present mirth hath present laughter. / What's to come is still unsure... Youth's a stuff will not endure." Orsino, II.4: "women are as roses, whose fair flower / Being once display'd, doth fall that very hour." Olivia, III.1: "The clock upbraids me with the waste of time."
    - What the action does with it: The play endorses haste. Olivia abandons seven years of mourning, marries within hours ("Blame not this haste of mine," IV.3), and is rewarded. Orsino's lecture that a woman should take an elder husband is spoken to the younger woman he will marry. Then Feste's last song counts the ages of a man's life and finds rain in every one. Carpe diem and its bill are both presented, and the audience is sent home with the bill.

12. **Real grief is carried quietly; performed grief is dropped at the first distraction.**
    - Where it is spoken: Olivia's vow, reported I.1: "like a cloistress she will veiled walk, / And water once a day her chamber round / With eye-offending brine." Sebastian, II.1: "She is drowned already, sir, with salt water, though I seem to drown her remembrance again with more." Viola, I.2: "My brother he is in Elysium. / Perchance he is not drown'd."
    - What the action does with it: Olivia's brine is a schedule; Viola's and Sebastian's is a fact they do not mention again until the end. The performed mourning lasts one scene past its announcement. The silent mourners are the ones who get their dead back. Sir Toby's "care's an enemy to life" (I.3) is a drunkard's line, but the play half agrees with it about Olivia and not at all about the twins.

## What it teaches

The play is a machine for showing how fast and how thoroughly people can be wrong about one another and still end up married. Nobody in Illyria falls in love with a person; they fall in love with a voice, a leg, a face that turns out to be shared by two people, a letter in a familiar hand. The last scene's happiness depends on the audience agreeing not to ask whether Olivia's husband is the one she wanted or Orsino's wife is the boy he confided in. The text does not hide this; it has Sebastian say it to Olivia's face. If you take the comedy at its word, love is an accident of surfaces that sometimes lands well. That is cheerful or bleak depending on how you feel about accidents.

Against that, the play puts two people who love without a return and without saying so: Viola and Antonio. One is rescued by the plot and one is forgotten by it. The difference between them is not merit but a twin. Viola's speech about the sister who "sat like patience on a monument" is the play's truest account of love, and the play honours it by rewarding her through pure luck, which is a way of saying that patience is admirable and does not by itself get you anything.

What is left unresolved is Malvolio, and the play knows it, because it has Olivia and Orsino both say so. The joke on him was earned by his self-love and it outran its cause, and the man who ends the play promising revenge is not wrong that he was abused. Feste's final song, with its rain in every verse, is the text's own answer to anyone who wants the ending to be clean: this is how it goes, every day, and we will strive to please you every day. The lesson is not that festivity is wrong or that self-love is punished, but that the party has a cost and the bill is always delivered to someone who is not at the party.

## Preoccupations

- love as an accident of surfaces: faces, voices, clothes, handwriting
- the gap between loud vows and silent constancy
- self-love as a sickness that others can exploit
- disguise worn for the wearer as much as for others
- madness as a label the group puts on whoever will not play
- festivity and its bill; pleasure that gets paid for
- the one who loves most going unrewarded
- time and weather as the last word on every happy ending
