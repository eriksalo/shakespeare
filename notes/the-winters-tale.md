# The Winter's Tale

## What happens
Leontes, King of Sicilia, watches his wife Hermione persuade his boyhood friend Polixenes to extend a visit, and between one line and the next decides she is an adulteress and the child she carries is not his. Nobody can move him: Camillo, ordered to poison Polixenes, flees with him instead; Paulina brings him the newborn daughter and is called a witch; Hermione is tried in public; the oracle from Delphos declares her chaste and him "a jealous tyrant," and he says the oracle is false. Then word comes that his son Mamillius has died of grief, Hermione collapses and is reported dead, and Leontes wakes from the dream into sixteen years of daily mourning. The baby, abandoned on the coast of Bohemia by Antigonus (who is eaten by a bear), is raised by a shepherd as Perdita. Time crosses the gap in one speech. Perdita and Polixenes' son Florizel fall in love; Polixenes, disguised at a sheep-shearing, explodes and forbids it; Camillo steers the lovers to Sicilia; the rogue Autolycus picks pockets and, by accident, delivers the shepherd and the proofs of Perdita's birth. The oracle is fulfilled offstage and reported by gentlemen. Then Paulina unveils a "statue" of Hermione that steps down and embraces her husband. Hermione speaks only to her daughter.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Jealousy is self-generated: it builds a world out of nothing and then cannot bear to be told the world is nothing.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Leontes: "Affection! thy intention stabs the centre: / Thou dost make possible things not so held, / Communicat'st with dreams ... With what's unreal thou coactive art, / And fellow'st nothing" (1.2). Then: "Is whispering nothing? ... Why, then the world and all that's in't is nothing, / The covering sky is nothing, Bohemia nothing, / My wife is nothing, nor nothing have these nothings, / If this be nothing" (1.2). Hermione: "My life stands in the level of your dreams." Leontes: "Your actions are my dreams" (3.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* The play gives Leontes no Iago, no handkerchief, no provocation beyond Hermione taking Polixenes' hand at Leontes' own urging. The speech in which he analyses "affection" is itself the disease in progress. Camillo's diagnosis — "you may as well / Forbid the sea for to obey the moon / As or by oath remove or counsel shake / The fabric of his folly, whose foundation / Is pil'd upon his faith" (1.2) — is confirmed to the letter: argument, pleading, the baby's face, and finally the god's own words ("There is no truth at all i' th' oracle") all fail. Only the death of his son reaches him. The work observes that a belief founded on nothing is harder to move than one founded on something, because there is nothing to remove.

2. **It is not the thing but the knowledge of the thing that poisons; and the knowledge can be false.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Leontes: "There may be in the cup / A spider steep'd, and one may drink, depart, / And yet partake no venom, for his knowledge / Is not infected ... I have drunk, and seen the spider" (2.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* The image is true and the application is false; there is no spider. The play turns his own metaphor against him: what cracks his gorge is not a fact but an idea. Yet the damage of the false knowledge is entirely real — a son dead, a daughter exposed, a wife lost for sixteen years. The work insists that imagined wrongs produce actual ruin.

3. **Childhood innocence is a condition that cannot be re-entered, and adult passion is what replaced it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Polixenes on himself and Leontes as boys: "We were as twinn'd lambs that did frisk i' th' sun ... we knew not / The doctrine of ill-doing, nor dream'd / That any did. Had we pursu'd that life, / And our weak spirits ne'er been higher rear'd / With stronger blood, we should have answer'd heaven / Boldly 'Not guilty'" (1.2). Leontes, looking at Mamillius: "methoughts I did recoil / Twenty-three years, and saw myself unbreech'd" (1.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* The nostalgia is spoken a few lines before Leontes' "stronger blood" destroys everything. The literal child, Mamillius, does not survive the adult's sin — he "took it deeply, / Fasten'd and fix'd the shame on't in himself" and dies (2.3, 3.2). The play lets Polixenes' theory stand (the two kings' friendship is restored only through their children), but it will not let innocence be a refuge for grown men. It is something they can only watch in others.

4. **Power that refuses counsel makes its servants choose between obedience and honesty, and obedience is the wrong choice.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Leontes: "Our prerogative / Calls not your counsels" (2.1); "I am a feather for each wind that blows" (2.3). Camillo: "If I could find example / Of thousands that had struck anointed kings / And flourish'd after, I'd not do't" (1.2). Paulina: "It is an heretic that makes the fire, / Not she which burns in 't" (2.3). Antigonus: "I swear to do this, though a present death / Had been more merciful" (2.3).
   - *What the action does with it:* Three servants, three results. Camillo disobeys and lives to broker the ending. Paulina refuses to be ruled ("He shall not rule me") and becomes the king's "physician" for sixteen years. Antigonus obeys the order to expose the child and is eaten by a bear on the beach; the Third Gentleman later notes that "all the instruments which aided to expose the child were even then lost when it was found" (5.2). The play is unsentimental: the loyal man who does the wrong thing out of oath is punished as thoroughly as the king who ordered it.

5. **Innocence cannot prove itself by speech, and the innocent know it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Hermione at trial: "Mine integrity, / Being counted falsehood, shall, as I express it, / Be so receiv'd" (3.2). Paulina: "The silence often of pure innocence / Persuades, when speaking fails" (2.2). Hermione: "if powers divine / Behold our human actions, as they do, / I doubt not, then, but innocence shall make / False accusation blush, and tyranny / Tremble at patience" (3.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* Both speech and silence fail. Hermione's defence is unanswerable and ignored; the baby's resemblance ("the whole matter / And copy of the father", 2.3) is ignored; the oracle is ignored. Her prediction that tyranny will "tremble at patience" is fulfilled, but only after sixteen years and a reported death. The play's position is that innocence will be vindicated and that vindication costs most of a life.

6. **Whether grief should end is a question the play argues both ways and then settles by trickery.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Paulina, in one scene: "Do not repent these things, for they are heavier / Than all thy woes can stir. Therefore betake thee / To nothing but despair" and, a minute later, "What's gone and what's past help, / Should be past grief" (3.2). Cleomenes: "Do as the heavens have done, forget your evil; / With them, forgive yourself" (5.1). Camillo: "Scarce any joy / Did ever so long live; no sorrow / But kill'd itself much sooner" (5.3).
   - *What the action does with it:* Leontes takes the harder counsel and grieves daily for sixteen years; Paulina polices it, blocking every remarriage. The play then reveals she was keeping Hermione alive and waiting for the oracle. So the sixteen-year penance turns out to be the condition of the recovery — but the audience, like Leontes, was never told. The play endorses long repentance while admitting it was managed by a deception, and it does not resolve whether Leontes' grief was "saint-like sorrow" or an afflicted man being kept in affliction by someone who had her own loss to avenge.

7. **The quarrel between art and nature ends with art that is nature.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Polixenes to Perdita on grafting: "nature is made better by no mean / But nature makes that mean ... This is an art / Which does mend nature, change it rather, but / The art itself is nature" (4.4). Leontes at the statue: "If this be magic, let it be an art / Lawful as eating" (5.3).
   - *What the action does with it:* Polixenes argues for marrying "a gentler scion to the wildest stock" and in the same scene forbids his son to marry a shepherdess, so the theory and the man come apart at once. The play dodges the social question — Perdita is royal all along, so no grafting is tested. But it answers the aesthetic one: the "statue" that moves is a living woman, art's masterpiece turning out to be nature kept hidden. The play's final wonder is not a miracle but a concealment.

8. **Time makes and unfolds error alike, and the one who waits is not the one who decides when waiting ends.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Time: "I that please some, try all: both joy and terror / Of good and bad, that makes and unfolds error" (4.1). Leontes: "Whilst I remember / Her and her virtues, I cannot forget / My blemishes in them" (5.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* Time does the healing, but not by itself: Paulina holds the clock. Leontes' "Hermione was not so much wrinkled, nothing / So aged as this seems" (5.3) is the cost stated plainly: the years are not given back. The play does not pretend that what is restored is what was lost.

9. **Death and birth are the same event seen from two sides.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Shepherd: "thou met'st with things dying, I with things new-born" (3.3). Mamillius: "A sad tale's best for winter" (2.1).
   - *What the action does with it:* The structure enacts it: Antigonus and the ship's crew die in the minutes Perdita is found; Mamillius dies as the oracle is read; Hermione "dies" and the play's second half is born. At the end Paulina, the agent of every recovery, is the one left with a husband "that's never to be found again" (5.3). Leontes hands her Camillo in a line, which may be grace or may be the king arranging other people's lives again. The work shows no gain without a loss adjacent to it.

10. **Love claims to be indifferent to rank and fortune; the play agrees and then never puts it to the test.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Florizel: "From my succession wipe me, father; I / Am heir to my affection" (4.4). Perdita: "The selfsame sun that shines upon his court / Hides not his visage from our cottage, but / Looks on alike" (4.4). Camillo: "Prosperity's the very bond of love, / Whose fresh complexion and whose heart together / Affliction alters." Perdita: "affliction may subdue the cheek, / But not take in the mind" (4.4).
   - *What the action does with it:* Florizel is as good as his word and loses nothing by it, because Perdita turns out to be a princess. Perdita's own prediction — "my dignity would last / But till 'twere known" — is the realistic one, and it is cancelled by the revelation rather than disproved. The Shepherd and Clown become "gentlemen born" in "four hours" (5.2), and the Clown's new idea of gentility is that a gentleman may swear false oaths for a friend. The play lets love's claim stand and mocks rank, but it does not show a king's son marrying a shepherd's daughter.

11. **Honesty is a disposition some people lack entirely, and good can still be done through them.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Autolycus: "what a fool Honesty is! and Trust, his sworn brother, a very simple gentleman!" (4.4); "Though I am not naturally honest, I am so sometimes by chance" (4.4); "Here come those I have done good to against my will" (5.2).
   - *What the action does with it:* Autolycus robs the Clown who helps him up, strips the feast of purses while they listen to his songs, and extorts gold from the shepherds by impersonating a courtier; and in doing so he puts the proofs of Perdita's birth on the right ship. The play observes that providence does not require honest instruments, and that an unrepentant rogue can be a necessary part of a happy ending. His last "amendment" is sworn by the Clown, who admits he knows it is false.

12. **Faith is a precondition of seeing, and the thing seen may be no more than an old tale.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Paulina: "It is requir'd / You do awake your faith" (5.3). The Gentlemen: "This news, which is called true, is so like an old tale that the verity of it is in strong suspicion" (5.2); "Like an old tale still" (5.2). Paulina: "That she is living, / Were it but told you, should be hooted at / Like an old tale."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play tells its audience four times that its ending is unbelievable, and asks them to believe it anyway. Leontes: "No settled senses of the world can match / The pleasure of that madness" (5.3). The jealousy of Act 1 was also a madness that made "possible things not so held." The play puts two acts of belief side by side — one destructive, one restorative — and the only visible difference is what each produces.

## What it teaches

The hardest thing in this play is its first act, because nothing causes it. Leontes is not deceived; he deceives himself in real time, in front of you, and the better the arguments against him the more certain he becomes. If you want to know what it is like to watch someone you love construct a lie and live in it, the first three acts are as precise a record as exists. What the play teaches is that you cannot talk a person out of a belief that was never reasoned into — Camillo says so, and is right — and that the only thing that will reach such a person is a loss they cannot reinterpret. Mamillius' death is that loss. The play does not soften it. The boy does not come back.

What the play then offers is not a cure but a long, managed penance, and a reader should notice that the manager is Paulina and that she has her own dead to account for. Her "Do not repent these things ... betake thee / To nothing but despair" is spoken by someone who has just lost a husband to the king's order, and the sixteen years she presides over are as much her sentence on Leontes as heaven's. The ending vindicates her — she was keeping the queen — but the text leaves you free to feel that Leontes was punished by a human being who chose the length of the punishment. The play lets both readings coexist and does not blink.

The last scene asks you to "awake your faith," and it is honest enough to say, through four gentlemen, that what you are being asked to believe is like an old tale. The lesson, if there is one, is that the capacity that destroyed Leontes — belief in what is not there — is the same capacity that lets him embrace his wife, and that the play cannot tell you how to have the second without the first. What it can show is the difference in fruit: one belief made nothings of everything, the other says "O, she's warm." It also shows the price written on the recovered thing: she is older, the boy is dead, Antigonus is dead, and Hermione, who had the most to say in Act 3, says nothing to her husband at all. She speaks to her daughter.

## Preoccupations

- jealousy as a belief built on nothing that no evidence can reach
- the impossibility of returning to innocence; children as what adults destroy
- the duty to disobey a ruler who will not hear counsel
- innocence that cannot prove itself and must wait to be vindicated
- grief and penance: how long is enough, and who decides
- time as what both breaks and mends, at the cost of the years themselves
- death and birth as adjacent events
- faith and belief as the same faculty in ruin and in recovery
- love's claim to be above rank, asserted but not tested
