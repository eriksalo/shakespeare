# The Two Gentlemen of Verona

## What happens

Two friends part in Verona: Valentine goes to the Duke's court in Milan to see the world and mocks Proteus for staying home in love with Julia. Proteus's father abruptly sends him to Milan too. There Valentine, who scorned love, has fallen for the Duke's daughter Silvia and is plotting to elope with her by a rope ladder; Proteus, on first sight of Silvia, decides he loves her, betrays the elopement to the Duke, gets Valentine banished, and then, pretending to woo Silvia on behalf of the foolish rich suitor Thurio, woos her for himself. Julia, meanwhile, disguises herself as a page and arrives in Milan to find Proteus serenading another woman; he hires her, unrecognised, to carry his ring (the ring she gave him) and letters to Silvia. Valentine, waylaid in a forest, is made captain of a band of outlaws. Silvia flees Milan to find him, is captured by the outlaws, and is "rescued" by Proteus, who, refused once more, tries to take her by force. Valentine steps out, denounces him, accepts his five-line repentance, and offers him Silvia; Julia faints, reveals herself, Proteus switches back to her, the Duke arrives, pardons everyone including the outlaws, and gives Silvia to Valentine.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A new desire can erase an old love instantly, and the person it happens to can describe it exactly while it is happening.**
   - *Where spoken:* Proteus, II.4: "Even as one heat another heat expels, / Or as one nail by strength drives out another, / So the remembrance of my former love / Is by a newer object quite forgotten." II.6: "At first I did adore a twinkling star, / But now I worship a celestial sun."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play confirms the claim twice. Proteus abandons Julia on the strength of a look, and in the final scene abandons Silvia in two lines when Julia reappears: "What is in Silvia's face but I may spy / More fresh in Julia's with a constant eye?" (V.4). The symmetry of the two switches shows the first one for what it was. The play rewards him with the woman he first discarded, and does not require him to be any different.

2. **Self-interest can reason its way from a broken promise to a crime, one plausible step at a time.**
   - *Where spoken:* Proteus, II.6: "I to myself am dearer than a friend, / For love is still most precious in itself," and "Unheedful vows may heedfully be broken." V.4: "In love / Who respects friend?"
   - *What the action does with it:* Proteus's soliloquy in II.6 ends with a plan to betray Valentine; III.2 has him agree to slander his friend ("'Tis an ill office for a gentleman" he says, then does it); IV.2 has him wooing under cover of helping Thurio; V.4 has him seizing Silvia: "I'll force thee yield to my desire." Each stage is justified from the last. Silvia names the thing directly: "All men but Proteus" respect friendship in love. The play shows that a person who loves himself first will find the arguments he needs.

3. **Love is a visible illness, obvious to everyone but the sufferer, and the one who mocks it catches it worst.**
   - *Where spoken:* Valentine, I.1: "Love is your master, for he masters you." Speed, II.1: "you are metamorphosed with a mistress, that, when I look on you, I can hardly think you my master," and the follies "shine through you like the water in an urinal, that not an eye that sees you but is a physician to comment on your malady." Valentine, II.4: "I have done penance for contemning Love."
   - *What the action does with it:* Valentine's own lecture to Proteus (I.1) on love as "a folly bought with wit" is turned on him by his servant within an act. The play confirms the claim and adds that the servants diagnose it first.

4. **A lover will say the beloved is his very self, and then treat her as property to be handed to a friend.**
   - *Where spoken:* Valentine, III.1: "To die is to be banished from myself, / And Silvia is myself ... She is my essence, and I leave to be / If I be not by her fair influence / Fostered, illumined, cherished, kept alive." V.4: "All that was mine in Silvia I give thee."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play puts the two speeches two acts apart and does not reconcile them. After Proteus's attempted assault and a five-line apology, Valentine offers Silvia to him as a token of restored friendship. Silvia's last words in the play are "O heaven!" as Proteus seizes her; she does not speak again. The action refutes the speech about essence: when friendship and love compete, the woman is the thing exchanged.

5. **Forgiveness between men can be instant and total, and the cost falls on the women.**
   - *Where spoken:* Valentine, V.4: "Who should be trusted, when one's right hand / Is perjured to the bosom? ... The private wound is deepest." Then: "Then I am paid, / And once again I do receive thee honest." Proteus: "if hearty sorrow / Be a sufficient ransom for offence, / I tender 't here."
   - *What the action does with it:* Valentine moves from "I must never trust thee more" to full reconciliation in the space of Proteus's apology. Julia faints at exactly that moment. The play does not say the forgiveness is wrong; it shows who pays for it. Proteus's "penance" at the end is "but to hear / The story of your loves discovered."

6. **Women change their clothes for love; men change their minds.**
   - *Where spoken:* Julia, V.4: "It is the lesser blot, modesty finds, / Women to change their shapes than men their minds." Proteus, V.4: "O heaven, were man / But constant, he were perfect. That one error / Fills him with faults."
   - *What the action does with it:* Julia's disguise is undertaken to keep faith; Proteus's changes are undertaken to break it. The play gives the moral to the man who most needed it and the demonstration to the woman. Lucetta's warning in II.7 ("All these are servants to deceitful men. ... Pray heav'n he prove so when you come to him") against Julia's "His words are bonds, his oaths are oracles" is borne out exactly.

7. **The courtship doctrine that "no" means "yes" is a comic maxim that ends in assault.**
   - *Where spoken:* Julia, I.2, of herself: "maids in modesty say 'No' to that / Which they would have the profferer construe 'Ay'." Valentine, III.1, instructing the Duke: "Take no repulse, whatever she doth say; / For 'Get you gone' she doth not mean 'Away!'"
   - *What the action does with it:* Julia's version is true of Julia, who tears the letter and then kisses the pieces. Proteus applies the same rule to Silvia, who means every refusal ("I despise thee for thy wrongful suit," IV.2), and his conclusion is "I'll woo you like a soldier, at arms' end, / And love you 'gainst the nature of love—force ye" (V.4). The play lets the maxim be funny in Act I and shows where it goes in Act V.

8. **Flattery is the acknowledged tool of courtship, and the women see through it.**
   - *Where spoken:* Valentine, III.1: "Flatter and praise, commend, extol their graces; / Though ne'er so black, say they have angels' faces. / That man that hath a tongue, I say, is no man / If with his tongue he cannot win a woman." Proteus, III.2: "Write till your ink be dry, and with your tears / Moist it again."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Duke uses the pretence of wanting courtship advice to trick Valentine into revealing his own plan. Proteus sings "Who is Silvia?" as Thurio's agent and is answered from the window: "Thou subtle, perjured, false, disloyal man, / Think'st thou I am so shallow, so conceitless, / To be seduced by thy flattery?" (IV.2). Silvia tears his letter unread: "stuffed with protestations / And full of new-found oaths, which he will break / As easily as I do tear his paper" (IV.4). The men's technique works only on the men.

9. **The lover of surfaces cannot tell one woman from another except by details.**
   - *Where spoken:* Proteus, II.4: "'Tis but her picture I have yet beheld, / And that hath dazzled my reason's light." IV.2: "I am but a shadow; / And to your shadow will I make true love." Julia, IV.4, holding Silvia's portrait: "Her hair is auburn, mine is perfect yellow; / If that be all the difference in his love, / I'll get me such a coloured periwig."
   - *What the action does with it:* Julia's inventory of the picture finds no difference that could matter. Proteus's final conversion confirms her: he looks at her face and finds the same thing "more fresh." The play suggests that what the inconstant man loves is a shape, and that shapes are interchangeable.

10. **Loyalty, in its purest form in this play, belongs to a servant and a dog.**
   - *Where spoken:* Lance, IV.4: "I have sat in the stock for puddings he hath stolen ... I have stood on the pillory for geese he hath killed ... How many masters would do this for his servant?" Lance, III.1: "I am but a fool, look you, and yet I have the wit to think my master is a kind of a knave."
   - *What the action does with it:* Lance takes a whipping for Crab, who pissed under the Duke's table. No gentleman in the play does as much for anyone. Julia, serving the man who betrayed her, says: "I am my master's true confirmed love, / But cannot be true servant to my master / Unless I prove false traitor to myself" (IV.4). The clown's speech and the heroine's are the play's two models of fidelity, and both are to the undeserving.

11. **Authority is arbitrary in anger and in pardon alike.**
   - *Where spoken:* Antonio, I.3: "for what I will, I will, and there an end." The Duke, III.1: "I now am full resolved to take a wife / And turn her out to who will take her in." V.4: "I here forget all former griefs, / Cancel all grudge, repeal thee home again."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Duke banishes Valentine on pain of death and then, seeing him face down Thurio, calls him "worthy of an empress' love" and pardons the outlaws too. Nothing has been argued; the Duke has simply changed his mind. The play's happy ending depends on the same caprice that caused its troubles.

12. **Exile strips away the court's valuations, and some of them do not come back.**
   - *Where spoken:* Valentine, V.4: "How use doth breed a habit in a man! / This shadowy desert, unfrequented woods, / I better brook than flourishing peopled towns." The outlaws, IV.1: "some of us are gentlemen, / Such as the fury of ungoverned youth / Thrust from the company of awful men."
   - *What the action does with it:* The banished gentlemen in the forest are, by their own account, murderers and abductors, and they are declared "reformed, civil, full of good" at the end. The forest is where the truth about Proteus comes out and where the court's judgments are reversed. Civil order in this play is whoever holds the key.

## What it teaches

The play's clearest lesson is one it gives to its servants and its women rather than its heroes: that people in love are legible to everyone except themselves, and that the warnings they are given are usually right. Lucetta tells Julia that oaths and tears are "servants to deceitful men"; Speed tells Valentine that his folly shines through him; Lance knows his master is a knave. None of them is listened to, and all of them are vindicated. If you want to know whether a person is to be trusted, the play suggests, ask the people who carry his letters.

Its harder lesson is in the last scene, and it is hard because the play does not seem to know it is hard. Valentine, having said that Silvia is his essence, hands her to the man who has just tried to rape her, in order to prove that a friendship is mended. Proteus, having been inconstant to two women and a friend, is given the play's best lines about constancy. The Duke, who banished on a whim, pardons on a whim. Everything is forgiven and nothing is repaired. A thoughtful reader has to decide whether this is a flaw in the play or a precise picture of how people actually settle things: quickly, in words, with the injured party silent and the apology accepted by someone who was not the one most wronged. Silvia has no line after "O heaven!" The play does not notice her silence. You will.

What remains, and what the play gets right without strain, is the portrait of a mind talking itself into betrayal. Proteus's soliloquies are not the speeches of a villain but of a reasonable man finding reasons; "I to myself am dearer than a friend" sounds like honesty, and it is, and it is the whole problem. The play knows that inconstancy is not usually a lie told to others but a story told to oneself, and that the story is always available.

## Preoccupations

- inconstancy: how quickly desire moves and how easily it justifies itself
- friendship against love, and who gets sacrificed when they conflict
- the gap between what lovers say about the beloved and how they treat her
- servants and women seeing clearly what masters and lovers cannot
- flattery and oaths as tools that work only on the people who use them
- disguise as fidelity (Julia) versus change of mind as betrayal (Proteus)
- forgiveness that is quick, verbal and costs the wrong person
- authority as caprice, in banishment and in pardon
