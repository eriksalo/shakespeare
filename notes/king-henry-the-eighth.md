# King Henry the Eighth

## What happens
The Prologue promises to show the great "followed with the general throng and sweat / Of thousand friends; then, in a moment, see / How soon this mightiness meets misery," and the play delivers three such falls and one narrow escape. The Duke of Buckingham, who hates Cardinal Wolsey, is arrested on the word of his own dismissed surveyor and goes to the block forgiving everyone. Henry, having met Anne Bullen at Wolsey's banquet, discovers a "scruple" about his twenty-year marriage to his brother's widow Katherine; she refuses Wolsey as her judge, appeals to Rome, and is divorced in her absence. Wolsey, who wanted the king to marry a French princess and was secretly writing to the Pope to delay the divorce, accidentally sends the king an inventory of his own wealth along with his letters; he is stripped of everything, discovers "the blessedness of being little," and dies. Katherine, dying at Kimbolton, hears Wolsey's death described, speaks both his faults and his virtues, sees a vision of garlands, and sends the king a letter asking him to care for their daughter and her servants. Anne is crowned. Archbishop Cranmer is set up by Bishop Gardiner to be sent to the Tower, is made to wait at the council door among footboys, and is saved only because the king is watching from a window and has given him a ring. The play ends with the christening of the infant Elizabeth and Cranmer's prophecy of her reign.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Greatness is a thing you have until the moment you do not, and the moment is not of your choosing.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Prologue: "then, in a moment, see / How soon this mightiness meets misery." Buckingham, 1.1, at the instant of arrest: "My life is spanned already. / I am the shadow of poor Buckingham, / Whose figure even this instant cloud puts on / By dark'ning my clear sun." Wolsey, 3.2: "I have touched the highest point of all my greatness, / And from that full meridian of my glory / I haste now to my setting."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play's structure is this observation performed three times. Buckingham goes from "Lord High Constable / And Duke of Buckingham" to "poor Edward Bohun" in a scene; Wolsey from managing the kingdom to "an old man, broken with the storms of state"; Katherine from queen to "Princess Dowager." A gentleman at the coronation looks at the countesses' coronets — "These are stars indeed" — and his companion answers, "And sometimes falling ones."

2. **Those who depend on a prince's favour stand on nothing, and the fallen say so most clearly.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Wolsey, 3.2: "O, how wretched / Is that poor man that hangs on princes' favours! / There is betwixt that smile we would aspire to, / That sweet aspect of princes, and their ruin, / More pangs and fears than wars or women have." Buckingham, 2.1: "those you make friends / And give your hearts to, when they once perceive / The least rub in your fortunes, fall away / Like water from ye, never found again / But where they mean to sink ye."
   - *What the action does with it:* Confirmed by every fall and complicated by two servants. Katherine asks the cardinals, "Can you think, lords, / That any Englishman dare give me counsel?" and no one does. But Cromwell stays with the fallen Wolsey — "my prayers / For ever and for ever shall be yours" — and the play rewards him with office; Griffith and Patience stay with Katherine to the end. Friends fall away like water; a few do not, and the play notices which.

3. **A man does not know himself until he has lost what he was.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Suffolk of Wolsey, 2.2: "The King will know him one day." Norfolk: "Pray God he do! He'll never know himself else." Wolsey after the fall, 3.2: "I know myself now, and I feel within me / A peace above all earthly dignities, / A still and quiet conscience." Griffith, 4.2: "His overthrow heaped happiness upon him, / For then, and not till then, he felt himself, / And found the blessedness of being little."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play gives Wolsey's conversion full weight — it is the longest and most eloquent stretch of the play — and then has Katherine, hearing of his death, recite his vices anyway: "Simony was fair-play. / His own opinion was his law... He was never, / But where he meant to ruin, pitiful." Griffith answers with his virtues. Katherine concedes: "Whom I most hated living, thou hast made me... Now in his ashes honour." Self-knowledge arrives; it does not erase the record.

4. **The wish to be lowly is sincere, easy to say, and abandoned the moment preferment is offered.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Anne, 2.3: "'tis better to be lowly born / And range with humble livers in content / Than to be perked up in a glist'ring grief, / And wear a golden sorrow... By my troth and maidenhead, / I would not be a queen." The Old Lady: "Beshrew me, I would, / And venture maidenhead for't; and so would you, / For all this spice of your hypocrisy."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Lord Chamberlain enters within the same scene to make Anne Marchioness of Pembroke with a thousand a year, and she accepts with "prayers and wishes." The Old Lady: "A very fresh fish here... have your mouth filled up / Before you open it." Anne is never shown choosing; she is shown pitying Katherine and being raised. The play does not call her a hypocrite. It lets the Old Lady do so, and lets the action neither confirm nor deny it.

5. **"Conscience" is a word that serves appetite as readily as it serves God, and the play declines to say which it is serving here.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lord Chamberlain, 2.2: "the marriage with his brother's wife / Has crept too near his conscience." Suffolk: "No, his conscience / Has crept too near another lady." Henry, same scene: "Would it not grieve an able man to leave / So sweet a bedfellow? But, conscience, conscience! / O, 'tis a tender place, and I must leave her." A gentleman at Anne's coronation, 4.1, having described her beauty: "I cannot blame his conscience."
   - *What the action does with it:* Henry's account of his scruple (2.4) is long and sounds sincere — the dead male children, "a judgement on me." The court jokes about it before and after. The play puts both on stage and lets the king praise, in one breath, "the queen of earthly queens" whom he is putting away. Katherine's answer is the only one that steps outside the question: "Heaven is above all yet; there sits a judge / That no king can corrupt."

6. **A person can refuse to be judged by her enemy and lose, and the refusal is still the dignified act.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Katherine, 2.4: "I utterly abhor, yea, from my soul / Refuse you for my judge." To Wolsey: "You're meek and humble-mouthed... but your heart / Is crammed with arrogancy, spleen, and pride." Katherine, 3.1: "Ye have angels' faces, but heaven knows your hearts." "Nothing but death / Shall e'er divorce my dignities."
   - *What the action does with it:* She is divorced anyway, in her absence, "for not appearance." She loses the title, the court, the king, and her health. But she keeps what she said she would keep: she rebukes a messenger for insufficient reverence on her deathbed ("Deserve we no more reverence?") and orders her own burial "like / A queen and daughter to a king." The play does not give her victory. It gives her the thing she said was worth more.

7. **The king's justice is only as good as his attention, and he knows exactly how false accusation works.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Henry to Cranmer, 5.1: "not ever / The justice and the truth o' th' question carries / The due o' th' verdict with it. At what ease / Might corrupt minds procure knaves as corrupt / To swear against you? Such things have been done." Buckingham, 2.1: "I had my trial, / And must needs say a noble one."
   - *What the action does with it:* The speech to Cranmer describes Buckingham's trial precisely — a dismissed surveyor, a confessor, a monk, all swearing — and Henry, who then said "He's traitor to th' height," now applies the lesson only because he likes Cranmer. He saves Cranmer by watching from a window and by a ring; he did not watch Buckingham. The play never settles whether Buckingham was guilty. It shows that the question was never what decided the outcome.

8. **The great forgive at the block, and the play lets forgiveness be real without letting it change anything.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Buckingham, 2.1: "I forgive all. / There cannot be those numberless offences / 'Gainst me that I cannot take peace with." Then: "Yet let 'em look they glory not in mischief, / Nor build their evils on the graves of great men, / For then my guiltless blood must cry against 'em." Katherine, 3.1: "I will not wish ye half my miseries; / I have more charity. But say I warned ye." Wolsey to Cromwell, 3.2: "Love thyself last; cherish those hearts that hate thee."
   - *What the action does with it:* Each act of forgiveness is paired with a warning that carries, in the play's shape, a kind of weight: Wolsey, whom Buckingham forgave and warned, falls; the lords who pressed Wolsey are told "Press not a falling man too far" and Cromwell later tells Gardiner "'Tis a cruelty / To load a falling man." Forgiveness alters the forgiver and leaves the system intact.

9. **Ambition is named as the sin that fell the angels by the one man in the play who had most of it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Wolsey, 3.2: "Cromwell, I charge thee, fling away ambition! / By that sin fell the angels; how can man, then, / The image of his maker, hope to win by it?" "Had I but served my God with half the zeal / I served my king, He would not in mine age / Have left me naked to mine enemies." Norfolk, 1.1, on Wolsey's rise: "spider-like, / Out of his self-drawing web."
   - *What the action does with it:* Wolsey is brought down not by enemies but by his own packet — "O negligence, / Fit for a fool to fall by! What cross devil / Made me put this main secret in the packet / I sent the King?" The man who managed everything mismanages one envelope. The play lets the moral be spoken by the man best placed to speak it and lets the mechanism of his fall be plain accident.

10. **Pomp costs more than it is worth, and the play cannot stop staging it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Norfolk on the Field of the Cloth of Gold, 1.1: "Men might say, / Till this time pomp was single, but now married / To one above itself." Buckingham: "O, many / Have broke their backs with laying manors on 'em / For this great journey. What did this vanity / But minister communication of / A most poor issue?" — a peace that "like a glass / Did break i' th' rinsing."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play then gives us a banquet, a trial in full consistory, a coronation procession listed item by item, a deathbed vision of dancing spirits, and a christening. Every show is admired by someone on stage and paid for by someone off it: the clothiers have "put off / The spinsters, carders, fullers, weavers" (1.2) for the taxes that funded the French interview. The Old Lady: "A threepence bowed would hire me, / Old as I am, to queen it."

11. **The crowd watches both the rising and the falling, and is the same crowd.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Second Gentleman, 2.1: "All the commons / Hate him perniciously and... This duke as much / They love and dote on." Third Gentleman at the coronation, 4.1: "No man living / Could say 'This is my wife' there, all were woven / So strangely in one piece." Porter's Man, 5.3: "How gets the tide in?"
   - *What the action does with it:* The same two Gentlemen stand in the same street for Buckingham's execution and Anne's coronation — "At our last encounter, / The Duke of Buckingham came from his trial... that time offered sorrow, / This, general joy." The porters cannot keep the crowd out of the christening. The play lets the people be a weather, not a judgment.

12. **Comfort that comes late is "like a pardon after execution."**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Katherine, 4.2, when the king sends his commendations to her deathbed: "that comfort comes too late; / 'Tis like a pardon after execution. / That gentle physic given in time had cured me, / But now I am past all comforts here but prayers."
   - *What the action does with it:* Her last letter asks the king for three things — care for their daughter, husbands for her women, wages for her men. She has stopped asking for anything for herself. The play lets her die having blessed him. The king's message is sincere and useless; her dignity is complete and changes nothing. The Epilogue says the play's fate rests on "the merciful construction of good women, / For such a one we showed 'em" — the play's last word points back at the woman it unqueened.

## What it teaches

This play is quieter than the others in its group, and what it teaches is quieter too: that greatness is a position, not a possession, and that the people who hold it are the last to understand this. Buckingham, Katherine, and Wolsey each speak the same discovery at the moment of losing everything, and each speaks it as though it were news. Wolsey's "This is the state of man" is the play's thesis — "The third day comes a frost, a killing frost" — and the play's honesty is that it gives this truth only to the fallen. The ones still rising (Anne, Cromwell, Cranmer) hear it and go on rising. The play does not say they are wrong to. It says they will understand later.

The harder matter is the king. Henry is neither villain nor hero; he is attention. When he looks — from the window at Cranmer, at the inventory in Wolsey's packet — justice follows. When he does not look — at Buckingham's trial, at Katherine's refusal — injustice follows with the same efficiency. The play never tells you whether his conscience about Katherine is real, and lets Suffolk's joke and Henry's long speech stand side by side. What it does show is that the king's own account of how false witness works (5.1) describes exactly what he let happen to Buckingham, and that he applies the lesson only to a man he likes. If you want to know what it is to live under a ruler who is good when he is watching, this play shows you, and it shows you that the people underneath spend their lives trying to be watched.

What is left unresolved is forgiveness. Everyone who falls forgives — Buckingham at the scaffold, Katherine to the cardinals, Wolsey to Cromwell — and the play lets these be real. But each forgiveness comes with a warning, and the warnings come true, and nothing in the machinery of the court is altered by anyone's charity. Katherine's "comfort comes too late; / 'Tis like a pardon after execution" is the play's best line because it describes its own structure: every kindness arrives after the harm. The play ends in a christening and a prophecy of peace, and the Epilogue admits it is counting on the good women in the audience to let it pass — the ones who, like Katherine, have been shown what comes of being good.

## Preoccupations

- the suddenness of a great person's fall
- the worthlessness of a prince's favour as a foundation
- self-knowledge that comes only after loss
- conscience as a word that can serve desire
- forgiveness from the fallen, and what it does and does not change
- justice as a matter of whether the king is looking
- pomp and spectacle versus what they cost
- the crowd as weather, present at every rise and fall
