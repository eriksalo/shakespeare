# Troilus and Cressida

## What happens
The play begins, as its armed Prologue says, "in the middle": seven years into the siege of Troy, with nothing decided and everyone tired. In Troy, Troilus is sick with love for Cressida and can reach her only through her uncle Pandarus; the Trojan council debates whether Helen is worth the war, Hector proves she is not and then votes to keep her for honour's sake. In the Greek camp, Achilles sulks in his tent with Patroclus and mocks the generals; Ulysses diagnoses the sickness as a failure of "degree" and cures it with a trick, rigging a lottery so that the blockish Ajax, not Achilles, answers Hector's challenge. Pandarus brings the lovers together for one night; the next morning Cressida is traded to the Greeks for a captured Trojan. Hector and Ajax fight and embrace as cousins; the Greeks kiss Cressida in turn; Achilles studies Hector "limb by limb" at dinner. Troilus, led by Ulysses, watches Cressida give his sleeve to Diomedes and cannot make what he sees agree with what he believes. Ulysses' policy comes to nothing; Achilles is roused only by Patroclus' death and has his Myrmidons kill Hector unarmed, then drags the body behind his horse. Troy is not taken. Troilus curses Pandarus, who closes the play bequeathing the audience his diseases.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **People argue that a thing is worth only what someone will pay for it, and then discover they cannot live inside that valuation when it is applied to them.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Troilus, 2.2: "What's aught but as 'tis valued?" Hector's answer: "value dwells not in particular will: / It holds his estimate and dignity / As well wherein 'tis precious of itself / As in the prizer. 'Tis mad idolatry / To make the service greater than the god."
   - *What the action does with it:* Troilus wins the argument and keeps Helen. The next morning Cressida is "chang'd for Antenor" (4.2) by "Priam and the general state of Troy," a pure act of pricing, and Troilus says only, "How my achievements mock me!" In the Greek camp she is kissed "in general" and Diomedes tells Troilus, "to her own worth / She shall be priz'd" (4.4), using Troilus's own principle against him. Paris says of Helen, "We'll not commend what we intend to sell" (4.1). The play lets the market win every exchange and shows that no one who proposes the market can bear its verdict on what he loves.

2. **Order among men depends on everyone keeping his place, and the man who says so keeps order by cheating.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Ulysses, 1.3: "Take but degree away, untune that string, / And hark what discord follows!... appetite, an universal wolf... Must make perforce an universal prey, / And last eat up himself."
   - *What the action does with it:* Ulysses's remedy for the breakdown of degree is a rigged lottery and a campaign of flattery to swell Ajax ("Two curs shall tame each other," says Nestor). The policy fails outright: Thersites reports that "now is the cur, Ajax prouder than the cur Achilles, and will not arm today... policy grows into an ill opinion" (5.4). Achilles returns to the field not through degree but through grief for Patroclus, and restores Greek fortune by the most disordered act in the play, a gang killing of an unarmed man. The great speech on order is true as description and useless as cure.

3. **Fame lives only in the present eye; what you did yesterday is already alms for oblivion.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Ulysses, 3.3: "Time hath, my lord, a wallet at his back, / Wherein he puts alms for oblivion... To have done is to hang / Quite out of fashion, like a rusty mail / In monumental mock'ry"; "The present eye praises the present object."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Greeks "clap the lubber Ajax on the shoulder" and pass Achilles "as misers do by beggars." Ulysses is using the truth to manipulate, and it works. The ending confirms it more bitterly than he intended: the soldiers cry "Achilles! Achilles! Hector's slain" for a murder done by Myrmidons, and only Ajax demurs, "yet bragless let it be; / Great Hector was as good a man as he" (5.9). Present fame attaches to the name that is shouted, not to the deed.

4. **No one knows his own worth except by seeing it reflected in others, which makes everyone manageable by a withheld look.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Ulysses, 3.3, reading aloud: "no man is the lord of anything... Till he communicate his parts to others; / Nor doth he of himself know them for aught / Till he behold them formed in the applause." Achilles agrees: "nor doth the eye itself... behold itself, / Not going from itself."
   - *What the action does with it:* The scene is built on this: Agamemnon and the lords "pass strangely by" and Achilles instantly asks, "What, am I poor of late?" Ulysses had said, "Pride hath no other glass / To show itself but pride" (3.3). Ajax, pumped up with praise, "goes up and down the field asking for himself." Pandarus sells Troilus to Cressida by reflection ("mark Troilus above the rest"), and Cressida sells herself by withholding ("Achievement is command; ungain'd, beseech"). Everyone in the play is a mirror for everyone else, and the mirrors are rigged.

5. **In love the desire is infinite and the deed is finite, so lovers always promise more than any act can deliver.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Troilus, 3.2: "This is the monstruosity in love, lady, that the will is infinite, and the execution confin'd; that the desire is boundless, and the act a slave to limit." Cressida: "They that have the voice of lions and the act of hares, are they not monsters?"
   - *What the action does with it:* Before the night, Troilus fears the act itself will destroy him ("I shall lose distinction in my joys"); after it, the morning is a cold leave-taking ("You men will never tarry," 4.2) and the same day she is gone. The vows in 3.2 are oversized and oddly conditional: "As true as Troilus" and "As false as Cressid," each sworn as the name of a future proverb. Both proverbs come true. The play shows the gap Troilus named and shows that naming it does not close it.

6. **A woman who understands exactly how men value her is right, and the understanding does not save her.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cressida, 1.2: "Women are angels, wooing: / Things won are done; joy's soul lies in the doing... Men prize the thing ungain'd more than it is."
   - *What the action does with it:* She breaks her own rule in 3.2 ("Why have I blabb'd? Who shall be true to us, / When we are so unsecret to ourselves?"), is traded within hours, and says so: "O foolish Cressid! I might have still held off, / And then you would have tarried" (4.2). With Diomedes she reverts to the maxim, giving and snatching back the sleeve, and he answers it in kind: "I'll be your fool no more" (5.2). Ulysses reads her as "daughters of the game"; her own last words read herself more honestly: "The error of our eye directs our mind." Her cynicism about men is confirmed; it only tells her what will happen, not how to escape it.

7. **A person can be two people, and the one who loved can watch the other and refuse to believe his eyes.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Troilus, 5.2: "This she? No; this is Diomed's Cressida... this is, and is not, Cressid"; "O madness of discourse, / That cause sets up with and against itself! / Bi-fold authority!" Cressida, 3.2: "I have a kind of self resides with you; / But an unkind self, that itself will leave / To be another's fool."
   - *What the action does with it:* Cressida predicts her own division and then enacts it: "One eye yet looks on thee; / But with my heart the other eye doth see" (5.2). Troilus, who has sworn patience, first denies the evidence ("Was Cressid here?"), then splits her in two to save his idea, then tears her letter: "Words, words, mere words, no matter from the heart" (5.3). The lover who lived on words ends by dismissing them. The play does not resolve whether the Cressida of 3.2 or 5.2 was the true one; it shows that both were present and that Troilus can only survive by denying one.

8. **The go-between is loved for the labour and loathed for the result.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Pandarus, 5.10: "O world! world! Thus is the poor agent despis'd! O traitors and bawds, how earnestly are you set a-work, and how ill requited! Why should our endeavour be so lov'd, and the performance so loathed?"
   - *What the action does with it:* In 3.2 Pandarus asked that "all pitiful goers-between be call'd to the world's end after my name... all constant men be Troiluses, all false women Cressids, and all brokers between Pandars." The play grants the wish exactly, and Troilus's last word to him is "Hence, broker-lackey. Ignominy and shame / Pursue thy life, and live aye with thy name!" Pandarus's aching bones and "diseases" close the play. The man who made the match is the only one punished for it, and he knows it is because the match was wanted.

9. **A man who holds honour above life and keeps his word to the enemy is killed by the enemy who keeps no word at all.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Hector, 5.3: "Life every man holds dear; but the dear man / Holds honour far more precious dear than life"; "I must not break my faith." Cassandra: "It is the purpose that makes strong the vow; / But vows to every purpose must not hold." Andromache: "Do not count it holy / To hurt by being just."
   - *What the action does with it:* Hector spares Thersites ("I do believe thee. Live"), offers Achilles a pause ("Pause, if thou wilt"), and is answered "I do disdain thy courtesy" (5.6). Troilus has already told him, "you have a vice of mercy in you, / Which better fits a lion than a man" (5.3). Hector then chases a Greek for his "goodly armour," unarms to rest over the "putrified core so fair without," and is surrounded: "I am unarm'd; forego this vantage, Greek." "Strike, fellows, strike" (5.8). Achilles has meanwhile dropped his own "major vow" to Polyxena the moment Patroclus died. The honourable code is kept by one side only, and that side's champion dies for keeping it.

10. **Strip the war of its speeches and it is a quarrel over a whore and a cuckold; the man who says so is a coward and is still right.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Thersites, 2.3: "All the argument is a whore and a cuckold—a good quarrel to draw emulous factions and bleed to death upon"; 5.2: "Lechery, lechery! Still wars and lechery! Nothing else holds fashion."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play keeps confirming him. Diomedes tells Paris that Helen's two claimants weigh "each as he, the heavier for a whore" (4.1); Helen's one scene is a song, "Love, love, nothing but love"; Menelaus and Paris fight while Thersites cheers, "The cuckold and the cuckold-maker are at it." But Thersites is beaten by Ajax, begs off Hector as "a very filthy rogue," and dodges Margarelon with "One bear will not bite another." The play makes the truthful voice the vilest one in it, and lets no one refute him.

11. **The generals' wisdom is talk, and the talk changes nothing.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Agamemnon, 1.3: "checks and disasters / Grow in the veins of actions highest rear'd"; Nestor: "In the reproof of chance / Lies the true proof of men." Agamemnon on Achilles, 2.3: "He that is proud eats up himself. Pride is his own glass, his own trumpet, his own chronicle."
   - *What the action does with it:* Achilles and Patroclus perform the generals as a comedy ("He pageants us... 'Tis Agamemnon right!"). The Prologue has already told the audience that the play "leaps o'er the vaunt" and ends by "the chance of war"; Hector's "The end crowns all; / And that old common arbitrator, Time, / Will one day end it" (4.5) is never answered, because the play ends with Troy standing. Agamemnon's own welcome to Hector admits that "what's past and what's to come is strew'd with husks / And formless ruin of oblivion" (4.5). The council scenes diagnose everything and settle nothing.

12. **Reason is admitted to be right and overruled in the same breath, by the very man who stated it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Hector, 2.2, after proving that "these moral laws / Of nature and of nations speak aloud / To have her back return'd": "Yet, ne'ertheless, / My spritely brethren, I propend to you / In resolution to keep Helen still." Troilus: "Reason and respect / Make livers pale and lustihood deject."
   - *What the action does with it:* Hector has said that "pleasure and revenge / Have ears more deaf than adders to the voice / Of any true decision" and then demonstrates the deafness in himself. Cassandra's warning is dismissed as "brain-sick raptures." Cressida says that "to be wise and love / Exceeds man's might" (3.2); the council scene says the same of war. Every figure in the play who can reason chooses against reason for honour, glory, or love, and the play lets the consequences arrive on schedule.

## What it teaches

This is a play in which almost every wise saying is true and almost no wise saying helps. Ulysses is right about degree, right about time, right about the mirror of self-knowledge, right about Cressida; his cleverness produces a prouder Ajax and nothing else. Hector is right about Helen's worth and votes against himself. Cressida is right about men and is traded like Antenor. Thersites is right about the war and is a scab. What you are left with is a world in which understanding and outcome have come apart. If you want a lesson, it is this: the ability to see clearly is not the same as the power to act on what you see, and the play keeps the two in separate hands with deliberate cruelty. Reason, in Hector's phrase, "flies the object of all harm," and the people who do not fly are the ones the play kills.

The love plot teaches something harder than "Cressida was false." The play gives you her reasons from the first scene: things won are done, men prize the ungained, a woman who speaks first has given up her only leverage. It then shows that she is correct, that Troilus does leave her bed at dawn and let her be handed over "at the port" with a sleeve and a warning. Her betrayal in 5.2 is written as half-hearted and self-aware; she calls herself "false wench" before Troilus does. What the play exposes is not simply her inconstancy but the mismatch Troilus named in 3.2: a will that is infinite trying to live in acts that are "slaves to limit." Troilus's reaction, splitting her into "Cressid" and "Diomed's Cressida," shows what love does to protect itself: it would rather divide the beloved than revise the belief. Whether that is fidelity or self-deception, the play will not say.

What is unresolved is the war itself. The Prologue promises "the chance of war" and the play delivers a murder, a dragged corpse, and a city still standing. There is no end in the sense Hector means by "The end crowns all." Instead the last word goes to a sick pander bequeathing diseases to the audience, and the last heroic act is a gang killing praised as single combat. You can read this as the play's judgment that honour, love, and policy are all forms of the same appetite, "an universal wolf" that "must... last eat up himself," which Thersites restates as "lechery eats itself." Or you can read Hector's courtesy and Troilus's "simplicity" as the one real thing in it, destroyed by a world that prices everything. The play supports both and allows neither to win, which is perhaps what it means by beginning and ending in the middle.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between what people say and what they do; wisdom that changes nothing
- worth as a market price versus worth in itself
- time as the thing that undoes fame, love, and vows
- the self as something seen only in other people's eyes
- honour kept by one side only, and what it costs the keeper
- love's infinite promise against its limited act
- war and lust as the same appetite
- a divided self: the beloved who "is, and is not"
