# The First Part of Henry the Sixth

## What happens
The play opens at the funeral of Henry V and never recovers its footing. While the dead king's brothers and uncles quarrel at his coffin, messengers arrive one after another to announce that France is being lost "not by treachery, but want of men and money" — because the lords at home are "disputing of your generals." Two fights then run side by side. In France, Lord Talbot wages a long, personal, nearly single-handed war against the Dauphin Charles and Joan la Pucelle, winning Orleans and Rouen, losing Burgundy to Joan's persuasion, and finally dying with his son outside Bordeaux because the Duke of York and the Duke of Somerset, who hate each other, each withhold the troops that would have saved him. In England, the Protector Gloucester and the Bishop of Winchester brawl in the streets with their liveried servants; Richard Plantagenet and Somerset turn a lawyers' argument into the red and white roses; the dying Mortimer tells Plantagenet that his family's claim to the throne is better than the king's; and the boy-king Henry, pleading for love and amity, is coaxed by Suffolk into breaking a betrothal to marry Margaret of Anjou — a woman Suffolk wants for himself and means to use to "rule both her, the King, and realm." Joan is captured, denies her own father, and is burned; Charles swears a hollow fealty; the play ends not with victory but with a private man's scheme to govern the kingdom through a queen.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Internal quarrel undoes what outward courage wins.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The first Messenger, 1.1: "No treachery, but want of men and money. / Amongst the soldiers this is muttered: / That here you maintain several factions / And whilst a field should be dispatch'd and fought, / You are disputing of your generals." Henry, 3.1: "Civil dissension is a viperous worm / That gnaws the bowels of the commonwealth." Lucy, 4.4: "The fraud of England, not the force of France, / Hath now entrapp'd the noble-minded Talbot."
   - *What the action does with it:* Confirms it with brutal literalness. Talbot is never beaten by the French in the field; he is beaten by York and Somerset refusing to send horsemen to each other's advantage (4.3, 4.4). Somerset even says the quiet part: "York set him on to fight and die in shame / That, Talbot dead, great York might bear the name." The play makes every French victory a consequence of an English quarrel.

2. **A great man's power is not in his body but in what others will do in his name.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Talbot to the Countess of Auvergne, 2.3: "No, no, I am but shadow of myself. / You are deceived, my substance is not here" — then, as his soldiers enter, "These are his substance, sinews, arms and strength." An English soldier, 2.1: "The cry of 'Talbot' serves me for a sword."
   - *What the action does with it:* The idea is first a joke at the Countess's expense, then turns deadly serious. When the "substance" — the armies of York and Somerset — is withheld, the shadow dies. Lucy's long recitation of Talbot's titles over his corpse (4.7) is answered by Joan: "Him that thou magnifiest with all these titles / Stinking and fly-blown lies here at our feet." The name outlives the man; the man was never more than what the name could command.

3. **Reconciliations made under pressure are hollow, and everyone in the room knows it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Gloucester and Winchester shake hands before the king, 3.1, each with an aside: "Ay, but, I fear me, with a hollow heart" / "So help me God, as I intend it not!" Exeter, alone: "This late dissension grown betwixt the peers / Burns under feigned ashes of forged love, / And will at last break out into a flame."
   - *What the action does with it:* Confirmed. Winchester, newly a cardinal in 5.1, resolves "I'll either make thee stoop and bend thy knee, / Or sack this country with a mutiny." Burgundy swears "My vows are equal partners with thy vows" (3.2) and defects in the next scene. Charles gives "tokens of fealty" (5.4) just after Alençon has advised him to "take this compact of a truce, / Although you break it when your pleasure serves." The play contains almost no oath that holds.

4. **Great consequences grow from trivial causes, and the people inside them cannot see the size of what they start.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Warwick in the Temple Garden, 2.4: "this brawl today, / Grown to this faction in the Temple Garden, / Shall send between the Red Rose and the White / A thousand souls to death and deadly night." Henry, 4.1: "Good Lord, what madness rules in brainsick men, / When for so slight and frivolous a cause / Such factious emulations shall arise!"
   - *What the action does with it:* The lawsuit that began the quarrel is never even named — Plantagenet calls it "a case of truth," and nobody states the case. Henry, trying to show impartiality, puts on a red rose as "a toy, a thing of no regard" (4.1), and York's only comment afterward is "I like it not, / In that he wears the badge of Somerset." The gesture meant to be nothing becomes the thing that decides York's hatred. The play keeps showing the gap between how small a thing looks to the one doing it and how large it looks to the one watching.

5. **Honour, for those who hold to it, is worth more than life — and the play asks what that purchase buys.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* John Talbot, 4.5: "Here on my knee I beg mortality, / Rather than life preserved with infamy." Against him, Fastolf, 3.2: "All the Talbots in the world, to save my life."
   - *What the action does with it:* The Talbots die together; Fastolf is stripped of his Garter and banished (4.1). The play honours the choice — Lucy: "His fame lives in the world, his shame in you." But it also shows the price with no discount: Talbot himself calls staying "too much folly... To hazard all our lives in one small boat" (4.6), and Charles ends the scene with "All will be ours, now bloody Talbot's slain." The honourable death saves nothing but the honour.

6. **Reputation runs ahead of the person and is always a disappointment or a shock on meeting.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Countess, 2.3: "I thought I should have seen some Hercules... Alas, this is a child, a silly dwarf!" Talbot of his captivity, 1.4: "Here, said they, is the terror of the French, / The scarecrow that affrights our children so."
   - *What the action does with it:* Both scenes resolve in Talbot's favour — the shadow becomes substance when the horn is winded. But Henry VI himself, on first meeting the man, can only ask "Is this the Lord Talbot?" (3.4), and the great reputation has never earned "so much as thanks" because "till now we never saw your face." A whole kingdom has been running on the strength of a name it had never looked at.

7. **A claim to be chosen by heaven is tested by what the claimant does when heaven is silent.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Joan, 1.2: "God's mother deigned to appear to me... Her aid she promised and assured success." Joan, 5.4: "I never had to do with wicked spirits... chosen from above, / By inspiration of celestial grace."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play refutes her claim as plainly as it can: in 5.3 she summons "ye familiar spirits," offers them "a member," her blood, and her soul, and "they depart." In 5.4 she disowns her father, then claims a child by Charles, then Alençon, then Reignier. But the play does not hand the English heaven either. Talbot's "God is our fortress" (2.1) does not save him, and Bedford's last words — "What is the trust or strength of foolish man?" — are spoken over a French rout he has just watched and will not live past. Both sides invoke God; the action answers neither.

8. **Good intentions in a ruler without force are not just useless but harmful.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Henry, 3.1: "I would prevail, if prayers might prevail, / To join your hearts in love and amity." Henry, 4.1: "Both are my kinsmen, and I love them both." Exeter, 4.1: "'Tis much when scepters are in children's hands; / But more when envy breeds unkind division."
   - *What the action does with it:* Henry's attempt at peacemaking in 4.1 is the direct cause of Talbot's death: he makes York Regent and tells Somerset to "unite / Your troops of horsemen with his bands of foot," thereby splitting the army between two men who hate each other. The play does not mock Henry's goodness — it is the only goodness in the court — but it shows goodness that cannot compel is a kind of abdication.

9. **Appetite dresses itself as policy and argues better than policy does.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Suffolk, holding Margaret prisoner, 5.3: "I'll win this Lady Margaret. For whom? / Why, for my king. Tush, that's a wooden thing!" — and then, in council, 5.5: "Marriage is a matter of more worth / Than to be dealt in by attorneyship... For what is wedlock forced but a hell?"
   - *What the action does with it:* Suffolk's speech for love over bargaining is the most eloquent thing said in the English court, and it is a lie: his last lines are "Margaret shall now be queen, and rule the King; / But I will rule both her, the King, and realm." Henry, who has never felt "any passion of inflaming love," is overthrown by "breath of her renown" and breaks a betrothal on hearsay. The play lets good arguments be made for bad reasons and lets them win.

10. **Men convinced they are wronged will let a third man die rather than yield to each other.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* York, 4.3: "Mad ire and wrathful fury makes me weep / That thus we die while remiss traitors sleep." Somerset, 4.4: "York set him on; York should have sent him aid... I owe him little duty, and less love, / And take foul scorn to fawn on him by sending."
   - *What the action does with it:* Each is sincere; each weeps or protests; each believes the other is the traitor; and the horsemen arrive "too late" (4.4). The play makes the mechanism exact: it is not malice toward Talbot but pride between two others that kills him. Being in the right, or feeling so, is shown to be exactly as deadly as being in the wrong.

11. **Death is the only arbiter that settles anything.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Mortimer, 2.5: "the arbitrator of despairs, / Just Death, kind umpire of men's miseries." Talbot over Bedford, 3.2: "kings and mightiest potentates must die, / For that's the end of human misery."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play's one real resolution of a quarrel is Mortimer's death, which transfers his claim cleanly to Plantagenet. Every living settlement — handshake, truce, treaty, marriage — is undone or about to be. Yet death also settles nothing for the living: Mortimer's death does not end his claim, it passes it on, and Plantagenet leaves resolving "what I do imagine, let that rest."

12. **Faction does not stay with the great; it runs down into the streets.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Mayor, 1.3: "Good God, these nobles should such stomachs bear! / I myself fight not once in forty year." The Mayor again, 3.1: "Our windows are broke down in every street, / And we for fear compell'd to shut our shops." A servingman: "Nay, if we be forbidden stones, we'll fall to it with our teeth."
   - *What the action does with it:* The lords quarrel in verse; their servants pelt each other with pebbles until "many have their giddy brains knock'd out." Vernon and Basset carry their masters' roses across the sea and strike each other over them. The play keeps showing that a quarrel at the top is paid for at the bottom, and that the ones who pay are the ones most eager to fight.

## What it teaches

The play's hard lesson is that a country can be lost without anyone deciding to lose it. No one in this play is a traitor to England in the plain sense — not Gloucester, not Winchester, not York, not Somerset. Each is defending something: his office, his birthright, his honour, his dignity against a rival. And the sum of all these defences is the loss of France and the death of the one man who was actually fighting for it. The text makes this mechanism precise and almost unbearable in 4.3–4.4, where York and Somerset each refuse the other's need with reasons that sound, to each of them, like justice. If you want to know how a group of competent and even loyal people destroys what it is meant to protect, this play shows you: not through villainy but through the refusal of each to be the one who yields.

Against this it sets Talbot, and asks what one honourable man is worth. The answer it gives is double. He is worth a great deal: towns, battles, the terror of a whole nation at a name. And he is worth nothing: "but shadow of myself," dependent entirely on others' willingness to be his substance, and dead the moment they decline. The Talbot scenes with his son are the play's emotional center, and they make the honourable choice as attractive as it can be made — "side by side together live and die." But the play does not pretend the choice accomplishes anything beyond itself. Honour here is real and it is sterile. That is not a contradiction the play resolves; it is one it insists you hold.

What is unresolved is harder. Henry's goodness — his prayers, his tears, his "I love them both" — is the only moral decency in the English court, and it is shown to be the cause of harm. The play does not say he should be crueler. It does not say what he should be. It leaves you with a gentle person in a seat that needs force, and with the knowledge that he will marry a woman chosen by a man who means to rule through her. The last word is Suffolk's, and it is a plan. Everything the play has shown about broken oaths and hollow handshakes tells you how that plan will go.

## Preoccupations

- quarrels among allies as more destructive than enemies
- the gap between a man's name and his body
- oaths and reconciliations that nobody intends to keep
- small causes growing into large harms
- honour chosen over survival, and what that choice is worth
- a good ruler who cannot compel obedience
- desire disguised as reason or policy
- the people below paying for the quarrels above
