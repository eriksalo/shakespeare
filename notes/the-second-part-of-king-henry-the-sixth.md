# The Second Part of King Henry the Sixth

## What happens
Margaret arrives as queen with no dowry and the loss of Anjou and Maine written into her marriage contract, and the court, already divided, finds a common target in the Protector, Duke Humphrey of Gloucester — the one man who is loyal to the king and loved by the commons. His enemies (the Queen, Suffolk, Cardinal Beaufort, Buckingham, Somerset, and York, who wants everyone else out of his way) first bring down his ambitious wife Eleanor through a staged conjuring, then strip him of his office, then arrest him on charges nobody can prove, then have him smothered in his bed. His murder sets the rest of the play loose: the commons rise and force Suffolk's banishment; Suffolk is beheaded by pirates; the Cardinal dies raving; York, sent to Ireland with an army, seeds a rebellion under Jack Cade to "perceive the commons' mind." Cade's rising storms London, hangs a clerk for being able to write, beheads Lord Saye for building a grammar school, and collapses the moment Clifford invokes the name of Henry V. Cade starves in a garden and is killed by its owner. York returns, drops his pretence, claims the crown to the king's face, and wins the first battle at St Albans. The play ends with the Yorkists marching on London, Salisbury warning that "we have not got that which we have," and young Clifford carrying his father's body off the field vowing never again "to have to do with pity."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Innocence is not a shield; those who trust in it die.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Gloucester, 2.4: "I must offend before I be attainted; / And had I twenty times so many foes... All these could not procure me any scathe / So long as I am loyal, true, and crimeless." Henry, 3.2: "What stronger breastplate than a heart untainted? / Thrice is he armed that hath his quarrel just." Lord Saye, 4.4: "The trust I have is in mine innocence, / And therefore am I bold and resolute."
   - *What the action does with it:* Every one of these speakers is proved wrong by the plot. Gloucester is smothered within the act; Saye is beheaded by a mob that hears his defence and kills him "an it be but for pleading so well for his life" (4.7). Suffolk has already explained the mechanism: "do not stand on quillets how to slay him; / Be it by gins, by snares, by subtlety, / Sleeping or waking, 'tis no matter how, / So he be dead" (3.1). The play does not say innocence is worthless; it says innocence is a fact about the victim, not a protection.

2. **Suspicion needs no evidence, because any appearance of goodness can be read as disguise.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Margaret, 3.1: "Seems he a dove? His feathers are but borrowed... Who cannot steal a shape that means deceit?" Suffolk, 3.1: "Smooth runs the water where the brook is deep, / And in his simple show he harbours treason." Buckingham: "Tut, these are petty faults to faults unknown, / Which time will bring to light." Gloucester's own summary: "A staff is quickly found to beat a dog."
   - *What the action does with it:* Gloucester answers every specific charge — he paid garrisons from his own purse, "pity was all the fault that was in me" — and is arrested anyway, because the charges were never the point. The play shows a style of reasoning that cannot be refuted: evidence of innocence is evidence of concealment. It also shows the accusers do not believe it themselves: in the same scene they agree privately that "we have but trivial argument, / More than mistrust."

3. **A good king who will not use force delivers the good into the hands of the bad.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Henry, 3.1: "My conscience tells me you are innocent" — and, as Gloucester is led away, "My lords, what to your wisdoms seemeth best / Do, or undo, as if ourself were here." Then his own simile: "as the butcher takes away the calf... Even so remorseless have they borne him hence; / And as the dam runs lowing up and down... Even so myself bewails good Gloucester's case / With sad unhelpful tears."
   - *What the action does with it:* The murder follows directly on Henry's leaving the room. He knows Gloucester is innocent, says so, and walks out. Later, in 4.9: "Was never subject longed to be a king / As I do long and wish to be a subject," and "Come, wife, let's in, and learn to govern better; / For yet may England curse my wretched reign." The play lets him see it clearly and change nothing. Margaret's "What are you made of? You'll nor fight nor fly" (5.2) is cruel and accurate.

4. **Everyone's politics are private ambition in a public coat, and they know it of each other.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Salisbury, 1.1: "Pride went before; Ambition follows him. / While these do labour for their own preferment, / Behoves it us to labour for the realm." Buckingham in the same scene: "Or thou or I, Somerset, will be Protector." York: "And so says York, [Aside] for he hath greatest cause."
   - *What the action does with it:* Salisbury's "public good" party is, two scenes later, kneeling in York's garden saluting him "our rightful sovereign" (2.2). Eleanor wants the crown, York wants it, Cade wants it, Buckingham wants the protectorship, the Cardinal wants Gloucester dead; even Hume the priest, hired by both sides, concludes "Sort how it will, I shall have gold for all" (1.2). The one person with no private aim, Gloucester, is the one they combine to kill.

5. **The crowd is both right and monstrous, and it turns on a word.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cade, 4.8: "Was ever feather so lightly blown to and fro as this multitude? The name of Henry the Fifth hales them to an hundred mischiefs and makes them leave me desolate." Warwick, 3.2: "The commons, like an angry hive of bees / That want their leader, scatter up and down / And care not who they sting."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play refuses to make the commons simply foolish. Their instinct about Gloucester's murder is correct and their demand for Suffolk's banishment is granted by Henry, who admits "I purpose as they do entreat" (3.2). The petitioners in 1.3 have real grievances — enclosed commons, a lord's man keeping a poor man's "house and lands, and wife" — and the Queen tears the petitions up. But the same commons, under Cade, hang a clerk for writing his name and shout "God save the King!" and "We'll follow Cade!" within ten lines of each other (4.8). What moves them in the end is not argument but the name of a dead king.

6. **Law, learning, and written records are experienced by the powerless as instruments of their ruin — and the play lets this be partly true.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cade, 4.2: "Is not this a lamentable thing, that of the skin of an innocent lamb should be made parchment; that parchment, being scribbled o'er, should undo a man?... I did but seal once to a thing, and I was never mine own man since." Cade to Saye, 4.7: "because they could not read, thou hast hanged them." Saye's answer: "ignorance is the curse of God, / Knowledge the wing wherewith we fly to heaven."
   - *What the action does with it:* The grievance is real — the play has already shown Suffolk "enclosing the commons of Melford" and the Queen tearing supplications. The remedy is grotesque: "Away, burn all the records of the realm. My mouth shall be the parliament of England." Saye, who may be guilty of nothing but speaking Latin, dies; his head and his son-in-law's are made to "kiss" on poles. The play holds both: the law has been a weapon against the poor, and the poor's answer to law is murder.

7. **Prophecy comes true in ways no one can use.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Spirit, 1.4: "By water shall he die and take his end" (Suffolk); "Let him shun castles" (Somerset). York, reading it: "these oracles / Are hardly attained, and hardly understood." Gloucester's dream, 1.2: his staff "broke in twain," with Suffolk's and Somerset's heads on the pieces.
   - *What the action does with it:* Suffolk dies at the hands of Walter Whitmore — "Thy name affrights me, in whose sound is death" (4.1) — and Somerset is killed "underneath an alehouse' paltry sign, / The Castle in Saint Albans" (5.2). Every prophecy is fulfilled by a pun. The play confirms fate and mocks it at once: the future is fixed, and foreknowledge is worthless because the words mean something other than what they say.

8. **Judgment by ordeal is luck dressed as providence.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Henry, 2.3, after Peter kills his drunken master: "by his death we do perceive his guilt. / And God in justice hath revealed to us / The truth and innocence of this poor fellow." York, in the same scene: "Fellow, thank God and the good wine in thy master's way."
   - *What the action does with it:* The audience has watched the neighbours pour sack, charneco, and double beer into Horner until he can barely stand. Henry sees God's hand. York sees the wine. The play puts both readings on stage side by side and gives the facts to York. It does the same with the Simpcox "miracle" (2.1): Henry thanks God "that to believing souls / Gives light in darkness"; Gloucester tests the man and finds "the lyingest knave in Christendom." Faith here is sincere and credulous; scepticism is correct and cruel (the couple is whipped "through every market town"). The wife's "we did it for pure need" goes unanswered.

9. **Conscience exists even in killers and cardinals, but it arrives after the deed.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Second Murderer, 3.2: "O that it were to do! What have we done? / Didst ever hear a man so penitent?" The Cardinal, dying, 3.3: "If thou be'st Death, I'll give thee England's treasure... So thou wilt let me live and feel no pain." "He dies and makes no sign."
   - *What the action does with it:* Warwick draws the moral: "So bad a death argues a monstrous life." Henry refuses it: "Forbear to judge, for we are sinners all." The play gives the Cardinal's terror its full weight — he sees Gloucester's hair "stand upright, / Like lime-twigs set to catch my winged soul" — and gives Henry the last word, but it is a word of mercy over a man who showed none. Remorse is real and it changes nothing for the dead.

10. **Shame, once public, cannot be taken off with the costume.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Eleanor, 2.4: "My shame will not be shifted with my sheet, / No, it will hang upon my richest robes / And show itself, attire me how I can." Earlier: "To think upon my pomp shall be my hell."
   - *What the action does with it:* The woman who dreamed of being gazed at in the coronation chair (1.2) is gazed at barefoot in a white sheet by "every idle rascal follower." The ambition and the punishment are the same spectacle, inverted. Her husband, who told her to "banish the canker of ambitious thoughts," is destroyed by her disgrace exactly as Hume predicted: "her attainture will be Humphrey's fall." The play shows a marriage in which one partner's crime becomes the other's death.

11. **Great men die at the hands of small ones, and die insisting it is impossible.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Suffolk, 4.1: "It is impossible that I should die / By such a lowly vassal as thyself... Great men oft die by vile Bezonians." Cade, 4.10: "I, that never feared any, am vanquished by famine, not by valour."
   - *What the action does with it:* Suffolk, who argued in the previous play that a king should marry for love, is beheaded by a ship's crew on a beach while reminding them he once let them hold his stirrup. Cade, who would have been king, climbs a wall to eat grass and is killed by a country squire. Both die with a sentence of pride in their mouths; the play gives them the dignity of the sentence and the indignity of the death, and does not choose between them.

12. **Oaths bind until someone finds a reason, and both sides find one.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Henry, 5.1: "Canst thou dispense with heaven for such an oath?" Salisbury: "It is great sin to swear unto a sin, / But greater sin to keep a sinful oath." Margaret: "A subtle traitor needs no sophister."
   - *What the action does with it:* York dismisses his army on Buckingham's word, "Upon mine honour, he is prisoner" — and Somerset walks in free with the Queen. York's cry "False king, why hast thou broken faith with me" is justified by the facts, though he was already dissembling ("I must make fair weather yet awhile"). The king's party breaks its word first in this scene; York was always going to. The play does not let either side own honesty.

13. **Grief for a father converts a man into something that cannot pity.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Young Clifford over his father's body, 5.2: "My heart is turned to stone, and while 'tis mine / It shall be stony... Henceforth I will not have to do with pity. / Meet I an infant of the house of York, / Into as many gobbets will I cut it." Richard, in the same scene: "Priests pray for enemies, but princes kill."
   - *What the action does with it:* Old Clifford's last words, "La fin couronne les oeuvres" — the end crowns the work — are refuted immediately: his death is not an end but the beginning of his son's vow. The play closes on York's "glorious day" and Salisbury's correction: "we have not got that which we have; / 'Tis not enough our foes are this time fled." A victory that creates an enemy who has renounced pity is not a victory.

14. **The private garden is offered as a way out, and the play closes the gate.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Iden, 4.10: "Lord, who would live turmoiled in the court / And may enjoy such quiet walks as these? / This small inheritance my father left me / Contenteth me, and worth a monarchy."
   - *What the action does with it:* Within the scene, the contented man has killed a starving one, dragged him "headlong by the heels / Unto a dunghill," and vowed to "thrust thy soul to hell." In the next act he is knighted and told to "henceforth attend on us" (5.1) — pulled into the court he said he never wanted. The play puts the pastoral ideal on stage long enough to show it cannot be kept.

## What it teaches

The play's core demonstration is how a group of intelligent people kill an innocent man while each of them knows he is innocent. Nobody in 3.1 is deceived. Henry says plainly "My conscience tells me you are innocent"; the conspirators admit among themselves that they have "but trivial argument, / More than mistrust." What they have instead is a shared interest and a method — "'tis no matter how, / So he be dead." The lesson is not that evil is cunning; it is that evil needs very little cunning when the good are divided between a king who will not act and a people who can only riot. Gloucester's confidence that loyalty is its own defence is the play's most sympathetic error, and the play corrects it with a corpse whose "eyeballs further out than when he lived" Warwick describes in clinical detail. Innocence does not protect you. Being right does not protect you. Having the commons love you means they will avenge you, not save you.

The second thing the play works through is the relation between the top and the bottom. The Cade scenes are the play's broadest comedy and its most alarming matter, and they are placed exactly where they are so that you cannot miss the rhyme: York, who wants to "pluck the crown from feeble Henry's head," has hired a man to say the same thing in a plasterer's accent. Cade's claim to be a Mortimer is a joke; York's claim to be a Mortimer is the serious plot. Cade says "My mouth shall be the parliament of England"; York says "This hand was made to handle nought but gold." The play does not say the rebels are right. It hangs a clerk for literacy and beheads a man for Latin, and it makes the crowd turn in a breath. But it lets Cade's indictment of law and parchment land, and it shows the Queen tearing up petitions before anyone has raised a staff. Grievance is real; the people who voice it are easily led; the people who lead them are using them. That is the whole picture, and the play declines to simplify it.

What is left unresolved is Henry. He is the only person in the play who consistently sees the truth — about Gloucester, about the Cardinal's death, about his own reign — and he is the only one who does nothing with it. "Can we outrun the heavens?" he asks as the battle is lost, and Margaret's answer is to call him nothing. The play never tells you whether his gentleness is a virtue the world is too corrupt to reward or a failure of nerve dressed as piety. His line "Forbear to judge, for we are sinners all" is both the best and the most useless thing said in the play. You are left holding a king who is good, who knows he is failing, who asks to learn to govern better and will not, and a kingdom in which every other person has already decided that pity is a thing they can no longer afford.

## Preoccupations

- innocence as no protection against power
- accusation without evidence, and reasoning that cannot be refuted
- a good ruler who sees the truth and will not act
- private ambition wearing the language of public good
- the crowd as both justified and dangerous
- law and learning as weapons against the poor
- prophecy fulfilled in ways no one can use
- the conversion of grief into cruelty
