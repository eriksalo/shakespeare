# Venus and Adonis

## What happens
Venus, the goddess of love, pulls the boy Adonis off his horse as he rides to the hunt and spends a day and a night trying to persuade him to make love to her. She argues from her own beauty, from Mars having been her captive, from nature's law that beauty must breed, and from the example of his stallion, which breaks its reins to pursue a mare. Adonis blushes, frowns, calls her immodest, says he is too young to know himself, and tells her that what she calls love is lust. She faints; he revives her with a kiss; she extracts a parting kiss and learns that he means to hunt the boar tomorrow. She describes the boar's savagery, begs him to hunt hares and foxes instead, and prophesies his death. He leaves. She spends the night singing to echoes, finds his bleeding hounds in the morning, rails at Death, retracts when she hears a horn, and then finds the body, gored in the groin by a boar she decides was trying to kiss him. She curses love for all time, plucks the flower that springs from his blood, and flies to Paphos to hide.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Beauty that keeps itself to itself wastes itself; the argument is sound and the arguer wants something.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Venus: "Torches are made to light, jewels to wear, / Dainties to taste, fresh beauty for the use ... Things growing to themselves are growth's abuse, / Seeds spring from seeds, and beauty breedeth beauty; / Thou wast begot; to get it is thy duty" (ll. 163-168). "By law of nature thou art bound to breed, / That thine may live when thou thyself art dead" (ll. 171-172).
   - *What the action does with it:* Adonis names the motive: "You do it for increase: O strange excuse! / When reason is the bawd to lust's abuse" (ll. 791-792). The poem lets both stand. Adonis refuses, dies, and leaves as "posterity" a flower which Venus immediately plucks ("it is as good / To wither in my breast as in his blood", ll. 1181-1182), destroying the one increase the story allowed. The goddess who argued for use ends by consuming.

2. **Desire sees its object and nothing else, and the poem shows this working in a horse and failing in a goddess.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The stallion: "He sees his love, and nothing else he sees, / Nor nothing else with his proud sight agrees" (ll. 287-288). Venus: "The sea hath bounds, but deep desire hath none" (l. 389); "learn of him ... To take advantage on presented joy" (ll. 404-405).
   - *What the action does with it:* The horse gets the mare; the goddess does not get the boy. The example Venus offers refutes her, because the mare was willing ("His love, perceiving how he was enrag'd, / Grew kinder", ll. 317-318). The poem observes that appetite is a complete explanation between beasts and no explanation at all where one party does not want it.

3. **Love and lust are different things, and the one who says so has never felt either.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Adonis: "Call it not, love, for love to heaven is fled, / Since sweating lust on earth usurp'd his name ... Love comforteth like sunshine after rain, / But lust's effect is tempest after sun ... Love surfeits not, lust like a glutton dies; / Love is all truth, lust full of forged lies" (ll. 793-804). He adds: "The text is old, the orator too green" (l. 806).
   - *What the action does with it:* The narrator has already described Venus as the glutton — "glutton-like she feeds, yet never filleth" (l. 548); "like an empty eagle, sharp by fast, / Tires with her beak on feathers, flesh and bone" (ll. 55-56). Adonis' distinction is confirmed by her conduct. But the poem never shows the "love" he describes; it shows only her appetite and his refusal, and then his death. The distinction is right and the thing distinguished is absent.

4. **The goddess of love cannot make herself loved.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "She's love, she loves, and yet she is not lov'd" (l. 610). "Poor queen of love, in thine own law forlorn, / To love a cheek that smiles at thee in scorn!" (ll. 251-252). "Being judge in love, she cannot right her cause" (l. 220).
   - *What the action does with it:* Every argument fails; every kiss is extracted by force, feint, or fee. The one real kiss is payment for letting him go. The poem places at its centre a power that is absolute everywhere except where it is aimed. Mars "hath been my captive and my slave" (l. 101) and a boy with a horse will not be.

5. **The unripe know they are unripe, and are not given time.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Adonis: "Before I know myself, seek not to know me" (l. 525); "The mellow plum doth fall, the green sticks fast, / Or being early pluck'd, is sour to taste" (ll. 527-528); "The colt that's back'd and burden'd being young, / Loseth his pride, and never waxeth strong" (ll. 419-420).
   - *What the action does with it:* He never ripens. The poem grants his argument its full dignity and kills him the next morning, so that the claim "not yet" becomes "never." Venus's line that beauty must be used "in their prime" or "Rot, and consume themselves in little time" (ll. 131-132) is answered not by rot but by a boar.

6. **Love's fears are both ridiculous and right.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Venus: "where love reigns, disturbing jealousy / Doth call himself affection's sentinel; / Gives false alarms" (ll. 649-651); "fear doth teach it divination: / I prophesy thy death, my living sorrow, / If thou encounter with the boar tomorrow" (ll. 670-672). The narrator, on her vacillation in the wood: "O hard-believing love, how strange it seems / Not to believe, and yet too credulous; / Thy weal and woe are both of them extremes; / Despair and hope make thee ridiculous" (ll. 985-988).
   - *What the action does with it:* The prophecy is exact. She then rails at Death, hears a horn, retracts and flatters Death ("I did but jest"), and finds the body. The poem shows that the lover's fear is a true instrument that the lover herself cannot trust, and that hope makes her absurd in the minutes before she is proved right.

7. **Love will use any argument that comes to hand.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Venus, on the hunted hare: "For misery is trodden on by many, / And being low never reliev'd by any ... Unlike myself thou hear'st me moralize, / Applying this to that, and so to so, / For love can comment upon every woe" (ll. 707-714).
   - *What the action does with it:* She moralizes about the hare to keep him from the boar, and says so. The poem shows desire borrowing pity, natural law, astronomy ("Cynthia for shame obscures her silver shine"), and economics ("gold that's put to use more gold begets") in turn, and discarding each when it fails. Adonis: "What have you urg'd that I cannot reprove?" (l. 787).

8. **Grief without an audience repeats itself and is never finished.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Their copious stories oftentimes begun, / End without audience, and are never done" (ll. 845-846). The echoes "would say after her, if she said 'No'" (l. 852).
   - *What the action does with it:* Her night of wailing is answered only by caves; her final curse is spoken over a corpse; she flies to Paphos "not [to] be seen." The poem gives her the last 300 lines and no listener, and observes that this is what grief is like.

9. **Desire and destruction are one motion.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Venus on the boar: "If he did see his face, why then I know / He thought to kiss him, and hath kill'd him so ... Had I been tooth'd like him, I must confess, / With kissing him I should have kill'd him first" (ll. 1109-1118).
   - *What the action does with it:* The poem has been saying this since the eagle simile of line 55; Venus says it herself at the end without irony. Her reading of the boar as a lover is her own confession. The wound is in the groin.

10. **When beauty dies, the world ought to end and does not.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "For he being dead, with him is beauty slain, / And beauty dead, black Chaos comes again" (ll. 1019-1020). "Wonder of time ... this is my spite, / That thou being dead, the day should yet be light" (ll. 1133-1134).
   - *What the action does with it:* The day is light; the lark sang at dawn before she found him; the flower springs up. The poem observes that the mourner's sense of universal loss is real to the mourner and false to the world, and that the gap is itself part of the grief.

11. **Love is disordered because someone once lost the one they loved; the curse describes the poem that contains it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Venus: "Sorrow on love hereafter shall attend ... It shall be fickle, false and full of fraud ... It shall suspect where is no cause of fear, / It shall not fear where it should most mistrust ... And most deceiving when it seems most just ... They that love best their love shall not enjoy" (ll. 1135-1164).
   - *What the action does with it:* Every clause is already true of Venus in this poem: she suspected rightly and feared wrongly, was "raging mad, and silly mild," made "the young old." The curse is offered as the origin of love's misery and reads as its summary. The poem does not say whether love was like this before; it shows that the goddess's explanation is her own portrait.

## What it teaches

The poem is built on a simple refusal, and it does not take sides. Venus has every argument and Adonis has one — not yet — and his one is enough, and then he is dead. If you want the poem to tell you whether he was right to refuse, it will not; it lets his "Before I know myself, seek not to know me" stand as the most reasonable sentence anyone speaks, and then arranges that he never does know himself. What the poem knows is that reasonableness is not protection. The boar does not care about ripeness.

What it knows about desire is less comfortable. The goddess of love is shown as an eagle, a glutton, a vulture, a park with a deer in it, and finally as someone who understands the boar because she would have done the same with teeth. The poem does not punish her for this; it gives her the long, intelligent speeches and the whole last movement of grief. It simply shows that the wish to possess and the wish to consume are not distinct, and that the thing desired knows it — Adonis says "you crush me; let me go" (l. 611) and is right. A reader who has loved someone too much will recognise Venus more than they would like.

The ending is hard. Venus argued for use and increase; she gets a flower and plucks it, and the poem notes that it "withers." Then she prophesies that all love hereafter will be what hers has been. It is possible to read this as the poem's own theory of why love is the way it is, and possible to read it as the self-justification of a creature who could not take no for an answer. The text supports both and says nothing. What it leaves you with is the sight of a day going on being light after the person is dead, which Venus calls spite and which is only the world.

## Preoccupations

- desire as consumption; the lover as predator
- the refusal of the one desired, and whether refusal is wisdom
- beauty as something owed to the world versus something owned
- the lover's fear as both absurd and prophetic
- argument as the instrument of appetite
- grief without audience
- the world going on after the beloved dies
- unripeness: not yet, and never
