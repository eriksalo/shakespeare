# The Tragedy of Romeo and Juliet

## What happens

Two families in Verona have a feud so old nobody remembers its cause, and their servants brawl in the street on "an airy word." A Montague boy, moping over a girl who will not have him, gatecrashes a Capulet feast and meets the Capulet daughter, not yet fourteen; they are married by a friar the next afternoon. Within the hour the boy's friend Mercutio is killed in a street fight the boy tried to stop, the boy kills the killer, and is banished. The girl's father, meaning to cheer her, orders her to marry a count on Thursday; the friar gives her a potion that counterfeits death so she can be carried to the family tomb and collected by her husband. The letter explaining this is held up by a plague quarantine. The boy hears only that she is dead, buys poison, kills the count at her tomb, and drinks; she wakes minutes later, finds him warm, and stabs herself. The parents clasp hands over the bodies. The whole action takes four days, and the Prologue tells you the ending before it begins.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Love and hate are made of the same stuff and are easily confused for one another.**
   - Where it is spoken: Romeo, I.1: "Here's much to do with hate, but more with love: / Why, then, O brawling love! O loving hate!" Juliet, I.5: "My only love sprung from my only hate! / Too early seen unknown, and known too late!" Juliet, III.2, when she thinks Romeo a murderer: "Beautiful tyrant, fiend angelical, / Dove-feather'd raven, wolvish-ravening lamb!"
   - What the action does with it: Romeo's paradoxes in I.1 are a fashionable pose about Rosaline, and the play lets you laugh at them; then it takes the pose and makes it literal. The lovers' first exchange is about pilgrims and saints; the feud's first exchange is about biting thumbs. The same heat that makes Juliet "gallop apace" toward her wedding night (III.2) makes Tybalt draw. The Prince's last word confirms the joke in earnest: "heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (V.3). The play shows the two passions sharing a body temperature and a speed.

2. **A name is nothing and everything: it is "no part of thee" and it is what gets you killed.**
   - Where it is spoken: Juliet, II.2: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet... Romeo, doff thy name, / And for thy name, which is no part of thee, / Take all myself." Romeo, III.3: "In what vile part of this anatomy / Doth my name lodge? Tell me, that I may sack / The hateful mansion."
   - What the action does with it: The play agrees with Juliet philosophically and refutes her practically. Tybalt recognizes Romeo "by his voice" and wants him dead for being "a Montague" (I.5); Juliet learns who Romeo is only after she has fallen in love, from the Nurse ("His name is Romeo, and a Montague," I.5). Romeo's offer to be "new baptis'd" changes nothing; the name follows him to the tomb, and the gold statues at the end are raised to the names. The play shows that what is "no part of thee" is the part the world deals with.

3. **The young live at a speed the old cannot imagine, and the old prescribe a slowness the young cannot use.**
   - Where it is spoken: Friar Lawrence, II.3: "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast." II.6: "These violent delights have violent ends, / And in their triumph die; like fire and powder, / Which as they kiss consume... / Too swift arrives as tardy as too slow." Juliet, II.5: "old folks, many feign as they were dead; / Unwieldy, slow, heavy and pale as lead."
   - What the action does with it: The Friar states the moral and then marries them the same afternoon, and later says "we will make short work" (II.6). Juliet herself knows the danger ("It is too rash, too unadvis'd, too sudden, / Too like the lightning," II.2) and proposes marriage in the next breath. The play's clock is relentless: the Nurse's three hours (II.5), the lark versus the nightingale (III.5), Capulet moving the wedding from Thursday to Wednesday (IV.2), the Friar arriving "some minute ere the time" too late (V.3). The adults who counsel patience are themselves in haste: Capulet "would not for the wealth of all the town" harm Romeo in I.5 and will "drag thee on a hurdle" by III.5. Speed is not a fault the young have; it is the air everyone in the play breathes.

4. **Sorrow has no sense of proportion; one word can weigh more than ten thousand deaths.**
   - Where it is spoken: Juliet, III.2: "That 'banished,' that one word 'banished,' / Hath slain ten thousand Tybalts... There is no end, no limit, measure, bound, / In that word's death, no words can that woe sound." Romeo, III.3: "exile hath more terror in his look, / Much more than death."
   - What the action does with it: Both lovers, separately, say that banishment is worse than death, and the Friar calls this "rude unthankfulness" and offers "adversity's sweet milk, philosophy." Romeo's answer is "Hang up philosophy. / Unless philosophy can make a Juliet... It helps not" (III.3). The play does not decide between them. It does show that the lovers' refusal to accept any middle state (exile, waiting, a second marriage) is what makes every plan fail and every misreading fatal. The feeling that there is "no limit, measure, bound" is both the grandeur of their love and the mechanism of their death.

5. **The counselors who love you best will advise you to betray yourself, and will not understand why you cannot.**
   - Where it is spoken: Nurse, III.5: "I think it best you married with the County... Your first is dead, or 'twere as good he were, / As living here and you no use of him." Juliet, after: "Ancient damnation! O most wicked fiend!... Thou and my bosom henceforth shall be twain."
   - What the action does with it: The Nurse is not a villain; she has carried the ropes, run the messages, and loved Juliet since she was weaned. Her advice is practical and she gives it "from my soul too." The play's observation is that practical love and absolute love cannot speak to each other: from that moment Juliet lies to everyone, including the Nurse, and acts alone ("My dismal scene I needs must act alone," IV.3). Her parents, who mean to cure her grief with a wedding, likewise cannot imagine that she has a grief they do not know about. The play shows a girl surrounded by people who love her, every one of whom is useless to her.

6. **A father's love turns to rage the instant his authority is questioned, and he will not remember having said otherwise.**
   - Where it is spoken: Capulet, I.2: "My will to her consent is but a part; / And she agree, within her scope of choice / Lies my consent." Capulet, III.5: "And you be mine, I'll give you to my friend; / And you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets."
   - What the action does with it: The two speeches are the same man, two days apart. The play gives no explanation for the change except Tybalt's death and the wish to be obeyed; Capulet himself says "I think she will be rul'd / In all respects by me; nay more, I doubt it not" (III.4) before he has asked her. Lady Capulet, who was his age when she had Juliet, says "I would the fool were married to her grave" (III.5) and gets her wish. Over the body, Capulet's grief is real and the play lets it be: "Death is my son-in-law, death is my heir" (IV.5). The observation is that this love and this tyranny are the same thing, not alternating moods.

7. **Comfort offered by someone who is not suffering is an insult to the sufferer.**
   - Where it is spoken: Romeo, III.3: "Thou canst not speak of that thou dost not feel. / Wert thou as young as I, Juliet thy love, / An hour but married, Tybalt murdered, / Doting like me, and like me banished, / Then mightst thou speak." Also I.1, when Benvolio says "Be rul'd by me, forget to think of her": "O teach me how I should forget to think."
   - What the action does with it: The Friar's reply, calling Romeo womanish and a beast, is probably right and does not work; what works is the Nurse's ring from Juliet (III.3). The play keeps showing the same thing: Lady Capulet tells Juliet "much of grief shows still some want of wit" (III.5); the Friar tells the mourning family that "She's best married that dies married young" (IV.5), while knowing she is not dead. Everyone's consolation is either untrue or unfelt. The one thing that consoles is the presence of the beloved, which the plot keeps removing.

8. **Poverty consents to what the will refuses, and the one who pays knows it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Apothecary, V.1: "My poverty, but not my will consents." Romeo: "I pay thy poverty, and not thy will." And: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell."
   - What the action does with it: A short scene, but the play is exact about it: Romeo noticed the man's hunger weeks before he needed him ("this same thought did but forerun my need") and chose him for it. The law against selling poison is a law "to make thee rich" that the world "affords" nobody. Romeo's gift of forty ducats comes with the instruction "buy food, and get thyself in flesh." The play does not moralize beyond Romeo's own line about gold; it shows that the desperate buy from the desperate, and that the richer party sees this clearly and does it anyway.

9. **Death keeps the beauty of the dead a little while, and the living read that as a sign.**
   - Where it is spoken: Romeo, V.3: "Death that hath suck'd the honey of thy breath, / Hath had no power yet upon thy beauty. / Thou art not conquer'd. Beauty's ensign yet / Is crimson in thy lips and in thy cheeks." Capulet, IV.5: "Death lies on her like an untimely frost / Upon the sweetest flower of all the field."
   - What the action does with it: Romeo is right that she is not conquered; she is alive, and her colour is the evidence. He reads it as death being "amorous" and keeping her as a "paramour," and drinks. The play's cruelty is that the truth is in front of him in exactly the terms he uses, and his metaphor eats the fact. Juliet, waking, has the mirror image: "Thy lips are warm!" The observation that the dead look alive is turned into the observation that the living can be mistaken for dead, and that the mistake is final.

10. **Dreams and premonitions are true and useless.**
    - Where it is spoken: Romeo, I.4: "my mind misgives / Some consequence yet hanging in the stars, / Shall bitterly begin his fearful date / With this night's revels; and expire the term / Of a despised life... / By some vile forfeit of untimely death." Juliet, III.5: "Methinks I see thee, now thou art so low, / As one dead in the bottom of a tomb." Romeo, V.1: "I dreamt my lady came and found me dead... / And breath'd such life with kisses in my lips, / That I reviv'd."
    - What the action does with it: Every foreboding comes true, including Romeo's last dream, which he reads as joyful and which is a near-exact picture of the tomb scene with the kiss failing to revive him. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech (I.4) says dreams are "children of an idle brain, / Begot of nothing but vain fantasy," and Mercutio is the first to die. The play lets premonition be accurate and lets no one act on it: Romeo says "he that hath the steerage of my course / Direct my suit. On, lusty gentlemen!" and goes in. Knowing was not the problem.

11. **Blame for a catastrophe is spread so thin that it lands on everyone and no one.**
    - Where it is spoken: Prince, V.3: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love! / And I, for winking at your discords too, / Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punish'd." Friar: "if ought in this / Miscarried by my fault, let my old life / Be sacrific'd." Romeo, III.1: "O, I am fortune's fool!"
    - What the action does with it: The play stacks causes: the feud, Tybalt's temper, Mercutio's pride, Romeo's intervention "all for the best," Capulet's haste, the Nurse's advice, the Friar's scheme, Friar John's quarantine, Balthasar's speed, the Friar's lateness, Romeo's suicide a minute early. Remove any one and the ending changes. The Prologue calls them "star-cross'd" and the Prince calls it a "scourge" from heaven, but the plot is a chain of ordinary decisions. The play offers both readings, fate and fault, and lets the Prince's "Some shall be pardon'd, and some punished" stand without saying which.

12. **Reconciliation comes, and it comes at a price nobody would have paid knowingly.**
    - Where it is spoken: Capulet, V.3: "O brother Montague, give me thy hand. / This is my daughter's jointure, for no more / Can I demand." Montague: "I will raise her statue in pure gold." Capulet: "Poor sacrifices of our enmity."
    - What the action does with it: The Prologue promised that the children's deaths would "bury their parents' strife," and they do. The play is honest that this is what it cost and that the parents understand the exchange rate: a daughter for a handshake. The gold statues are the feud's last competitive gesture, each house outbidding the other. The Prince's "glooming peace" is the only kind the play believes in. It does not say the peace was worth it; it says it happened.

## What it teaches

The play is often remembered as a story about love defeated by hatred, and that is in it, but what the text actually dramatizes is love defeated by speed and by the inability to wait. Juliet sees this on the first night, "too like the lightning," and proposes marriage anyway; the Friar sees it and performs the marriage anyway; Romeo sees the omen and goes to the feast anyway. Nobody is stupid. They all know the thing that would save them, and knowing it does not slow them down, because the thing that would save them, delay, feels like death. Romeo's "Hang up philosophy" is not a boy's tantrum; it is a true statement that no counsel from outside a feeling can govern the feeling. What the play leaves you with is the difficulty of being told, correctly, that you should wait, by someone who is not in your position.

The second thing is how little the adults understand and how much they love. Capulet's rage, the Nurse's pragmatism, Lady Capulet's "I have done with thee," the Friar's complicated plan: all of these come from people trying to help, or trying to govern, a girl whose actual situation they do not know. Juliet's isolation is not that she is unloved; it is that she is loved by people who cannot be told the truth. She ends up with a knife and a vial and the line "My dismal scene I needs must act alone." The play does not blame the adults, exactly; it shows that love without knowledge is as useless as knowledge without love, and that families run on both at once.

What is unresolved is the question the Prince raises and does not answer: who is to be pardoned and who punished. The play has shown you fourteen causes and a quarantine. It calls the lovers "star-cross'd" and then shows no star, only a delayed letter and a man arriving a minute late. If you believe in the stars, the play's chain of accidents is their instrument; if you do not, the chain is just what the world is like. The final tableau offers a reconciliation that was available all along and cost two children to reach. The play says "never was a story of more woe," and it is not asking you to learn anything from it so much as to see that it could not have been otherwise, given who these people were and how fast they moved.

## Preoccupations

- haste, and the impossibility of waiting when feeling is strong
- love and hate as the same heat
- names and family as things that are nothing in themselves and decide everything
- the uselessness of good advice to someone in the grip of a feeling
- the gap between what parents intend and what children need
- foreboding that is accurate and changes nothing
- chance and fault tangled so tightly they cannot be separated
- peace bought at a price no one would have agreed to
