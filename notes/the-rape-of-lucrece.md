# The Rape of Lucrece

## What happens
At the siege of Ardea, Collatine boasts of his wife's chastity; Sextus Tarquinius, the king's son, hears it, rides to Collatium, is welcomed by Lucrece as a guest, and lies awake debating with himself. He lists every reason not to do it — shame, the ruin of his name, the friendship with Collatine, the worthlessness of the prize once had — and goes anyway, reading every obstacle (locked doors, wind, a glove with a needle in it) as a trial that proves the prize worth having. He wakes her, threatens to kill her and a servant and stage it as adultery, ignores her pleading, and rapes her. He leaves at once in self-disgust; she stays and spends the night and morning in a long complaint against Night, Opportunity, and Time, resolves on suicide, writes to Collatine, and studies a painting of the fall of Troy, finding her grief in Hecuba and her deceiver in Sinon. When Collatine, her father, Brutus, and others arrive, she makes them swear revenge, names Tarquin, and stabs herself. Father and husband quarrel over whose loss she is; Brutus, dropping the fool's manner he has worn, turns their grief into an oath, and her body is carried through Rome until the Tarquins are banished.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **To advertise what you value is to invite its theft.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "why is Collatine the publisher / Of that rich jewel he should keep unknown / From thievish ears, because it is his own?" (st. 5). "Honour and beauty in the owner's arms, / Are weakly fortressed from a world of harms" (st. 4). "Beauty itself doth of itself persuade / The eyes of men without an orator" (st. 5).
   - *What the action does with it:* The Argument and the first stanzas make Collatine's boast the cause of everything; the poem offers alternative motives for Tarquin ("Perchance that envy of so rich a thing") and lets the boast stand as the first link. The narrator's rule is confirmed, and the husband whose pride started it never acknowledges it.

2. **Coveting what you do not have costs you what you are.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Those that much covet are with gain so fond / For what they have not, that which they possess / They scatter and unloose it from their bond" (st. 20); "So that in vent'ring ill we leave to be / The things we are, for that which we expect" (st. 22); "for himself himself he must forsake. / Then where is truth, if there be no self-trust?" (st. 23).
   - *What the action does with it:* Confirmed to the letter. Tarquin leaves "a captive victor that hath lost in gain" (st. 106), hating himself, and ends banished with his whole house. The poem's economics of desire — "Make something nothing by augmenting it" — is the plot.

3. **Conscience can see everything and still lose; knowing the cost is no protection.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Tarquin's own debate: "What win I if I gain the thing I seek? / A dream, a breath, a froth of fleeting joy. / Who buys a minute's mirth to wail a week, / Or sells eternity to get a toy?" (st. 31); "I know repentant tears ensue the deed, / Reproach, disdain, and deadly enmity; / Yet strike I to embrace mine infamy" (st. 72). Narrator: "Thus, graceless, holds he disputation / 'Tween frozen conscience and hot-burning will, / And with good thoughts makes dispensation" (st. 35).
   - *What the action does with it:* The poem gives Tarquin a full and accurate account of what he is about to lose, and he proceeds. His method is to re-describe: "Affection is my captain, and he leadeth"; "Respect and reason wait on wrinkled age"; "The blackest sin is cleared with absolution." The work observes that argument is not what stops a man, because he can use argument on the other side.

4. **Desire reads every obstacle as a test that proves the prize worth having.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* The doors, the wind, the glove with the needle: "He in the worst sense construes their denial. / The doors, the wind, the glove that did delay him, / He takes for accidental things of trial" (st. 47). "Pain pays the income of each precious thing: / Huge rocks, high winds, strong pirates, shelves and sands / The merchant fears, ere rich at home he lands" (st. 48).
   - *What the action does with it:* The merchant comparison is Tarquin's, and the poem lets it show him converting hindrance into incentive. Even his prayer is turned: "The powers to whom I pray abhor this fact, / How can they then assist me in the act? / Then Love and Fortune be my gods" (st. 50). Nothing can stop a man who has decided that resistance is encouragement.

5. **Lust, satisfied, turns at once to disdain, and both parties are left poorer.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "But she hath lost a dearer thing than life, / And he hath won what he would lose again ... This hot desire converts to cold disdain. / Pure Chastity is rifled of her store, / And Lust, the thief, far poorer than before" (st. 98). "Drunken desire must vomit his receipt, / Ere he can see his own abomination" (st. 100). "She bears the load of lust he left behind, / And he the burden of a guilty mind" (st. 106).
   - *What the action does with it:* The poem spends almost no time on the act and a great deal on the aftermath: he "like a thievish dog creeps sadly thence," she "like a wearied lamb lies panting there." He gets what he wanted and the poem shows that what he wanted was nothing. His one moment of regret is instant and useless.

6. **Innocence cannot read evil in a face; it learns to afterward, and the learning is the loss.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "unstained thoughts do seldom dream on evil; / Birds never limed no secret bushes fear" (st. 13). "she, that never coped with stranger eyes, / Could pick no meaning from their parling looks" (st. 15). Later, at the Troy painting, on Sinon: "It cannot be, I find, / But such a face should bear a wicked mind" (st. 224).
   - *What the action does with it:* The poem stages her education in one line: she begins to say "It cannot be that so much guile can lurk in such a look," remembers Tarquin, and reverses the sentence mid-utterance. What she now knows is what he took.

7. **Pleading does not reach appetite, but the plea may be right about everything else.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Tears harden lust, though marble wear with raining" (st. 81). She "Pleads in a wilderness where are no laws, / To the rough beast that knows no gentle right" (st. 78). Her argument: "princes are the glass, the school, the book, / Where subjects' eyes do learn, do read, do look" (st. 88); "This deed will make thee only loved for fear, / But happy monarchs still are feared for love."
   - *What the action does with it:* Her speech fails on the night and succeeds in history. The political argument she makes — that a prince's crime teaches his subjects — is the argument Brutus uses to turn the Romans against the Tarquins, and the poem ends with the monarchy gone. The poem lets a woman be right and unheard and then lets her body prove her case.

8. **Opportunity serves the wicked and is never there for the needy; Time unmasks everything, but late.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "O Opportunity, thy guilt is great! / 'Tis thou that execut'st the traitor's treason" (st. 126). "The patient dies while the physician sleeps; / The orphan pines while the oppressor feeds; / Justice is feasting while the widow weeps" (st. 130). "Time's glory is to calm contending kings, / To unmask falsehood and bring truth to light ... To wrong the wronger till he render right" (st. 135).
   - *What the action does with it:* She concludes her own tirade: "In vain I rail at Opportunity, / At Time, at Tarquin, and uncheerful night ... This helpless smoke of words doth me no right" (st. 147). Time does unmask Tarquin, but only because she pays for it: the poem does not let abstract justice do her work.

9. **Shame attaches to the victim, and the poem gives three verdicts on what she should do about it without choosing one.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lucrece: "though my gross blood be stained with this abuse, / Immaculate and spotless is my mind" (st. 240); "No, no, no dame, hereafter living / By my excuse shall claim excuse's giving" (st. 244). The lords: "Her body's stain her mind untainted clears." Brutus: "Thy wretched wife mistook the matter so, / To slay herself, that should have slain her foe" (st. 262).
   - *What the action does with it:* She says "I am the mistress of my fate" (st. 151) and acts; the men say she was clean and need not have; Brutus says she was wrong and should have killed him. The poem lets all three stand. What it insists on is that she decided and they did not, and that her reason — that no later woman should use her as an excuse — is a reason about other people, not herself.

10. **The narrator says women are wax and men marble, and the poem shows the opposite.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "For men have marble, women waxen, minds ... Then call them not the authors of their ill, / No more than wax shall be accounted evil, / Wherein is stamped the semblance of a devil" (st. 178). "Poor women's faces are their own faults' books" (st. 179).
   - *What the action does with it:* Lucrece is the only person in the poem who forms a resolution and carries it out. She stops the men's oaths until she has finished speaking, names the offender, strikes, and leaves them weeping and arguing. Collatine "counterfeits to die with her a space" and must be scolded into action by Brutus. The narrator's theory excuses women by calling them passive; the heroine refuses the excuse and the passivity both.

11. **One man's private pleasure becomes a public plague.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* At the Troy painting: "Why should the private pleasure of some one / Become the public plague of many moe? / Let sin, alone committed, light alone / Upon his head that hath transgressed so" (st. 212). "Had doting Priam checked his son's desire, / Troy had been bright with fame and not with fire" (st. 213). "greatest scandal waits on greatest state" (st. 145).
   - *What the action does with it:* Her question is answered by the ending: Tarquin's private act ends a dynasty and changes a government. The poem also notes the inverse: "The crow may bathe his coal-black wings in mire, / And unperceived fly with the filth away; / But if the like the snow-white swan desire, / The stain upon his silver down will stay" (st. 146). The great cannot sin privately; neither can their victims.

12. **Grief seeks its own likeness and is eased but never cured by finding it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Grief best is pleased with grief's society; / True sorrow then is feelingly sufficed / When with like semblance it is sympathized" (st. 160). "It easeth some, though none it ever cured, / To think their dolour others have endured" (st. 227).
   - *What the action does with it:* She goes to the painting to find "a face where all distress is stelled," finds Hecuba, lends her a voice, tears Sinon's painted face with her nails, and says "Fool, fool! his wounds will not be sore" (st. 226). The poem shows art giving sorrow a shape and no remedy, and shows the mourner knowing it.

13. **The living claim the dead as property, and the clever turn grief into policy.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Father and husband: "The father says 'She's mine.' 'O, mine she is,' / Replies her husband" (st. 257). Brutus: "Why, Collatine, is woe the cure for woe? / Do wounds help wounds, or grief help grievous deeds?" (st. 262).
   - *What the action does with it:* Brutus, who had played the fool, "throws that shallow habit by" and uses her corpse to found a republic. The poem does not say he is wrong; it does show that the woman who said "I am the mistress of my fate" is, within thirty stanzas, an exhibit borne through the streets by men who were quarrelling over her ownership a moment before.

## What it teaches

The poem's most useful knowledge is in Tarquin's night. He knows everything a moralist could tell him and says it to himself in better words — a dream, a breath, a froth of fleeting joy — and then rewrites the argument until desire is the captain and reason is for old men. If you have ever watched yourself do something you had already proved you should not do, you will recognise the mechanism: not the absence of conscience but its defeat in open debate, with every obstacle converted into evidence of the prize's worth. The poem is not interested in whether Tarquin is a monster. It is interested in how a man who can see talks himself blind.

The harder matter is Lucrece's death. The poem gives her the clearest mind in it, lets her argue politics better than the men, and has her kill herself for a stain she and everyone present agree is not hers. Her stated reason is not about herself but about precedent — that no woman after her should be able to say Lucrece lived, so I may. The lords say she need not; Brutus says she mistook the matter; the poem says nothing. A reader is left holding her reasoning and Brutus' together and finding that both are intelligible and neither is comforting. What the poem does make plain is that she was the only person who decided anything, and that as soon as she was dead her body became someone else's argument.

The narrator's maxim about marble and wax is worth keeping for what the poem does to it. It is spoken as an excuse — do not blame women, they are stamped by force — and the heroine is the one unstamped will in the story. If the poem teaches something about the human condition rather than about Rome, it is that the people called passive by the theories around them are often the ones acting, and that the theories survive anyway.

## Preoccupations

- desire that knows its cost and proceeds
- obstacles reinterpreted as encouragement
- the instant collapse of pleasure into self-disgust
- innocence as the inability to read faces, and what it costs to learn
- shame that lands on the wronged rather than the wrongdoer
- the private act with public consequences
- grief seeking its likeness in others' grief
- women called passive by the theory and shown active by the plot
- the dead becoming property and policy
