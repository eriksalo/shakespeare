# The Second Part of King Henry the Fourth

## What happens
Rumour opens the play by bringing false news of victory to Northumberland, who learns within minutes that his son Hotspur is dead and that he himself is next. He promises to join the Archbishop of York's new rising and then, pressed by his wife and daughter-in-law, runs to Scotland instead. The King, sleepless and sick, has not reached his crusade; Prince Hal is still in Eastcheap, now weary of it, while Falstaff dodges the Chief Justice, cheats the Hostess, recruits scarecrows in Gloucestershire, and plans to fleece the ancient Justice Shallow. At Gaultree Forest, Prince John promises the rebels redress of grievances, lets them dismiss their army, and arrests them for treason, keeping the letter of his word. The King, hearing of the victory, collapses; Hal, believing him dead, takes the crown from his pillow, is rebuked, explains himself, and receives his father's last counsel to "busy giddy minds with foreign quarrels." The King dies in a room called Jerusalem. The new King Henry V confirms the Chief Justice who once jailed him, and when Falstaff rides through the night to greet him at his coronation, says "I know thee not, old man," and has him taken to the Fleet.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **People believe the news they want, and fear knows the truth before the messenger speaks.**
   - Spoken: Rumour, Induction: "From Rumour's tongues / They bring smooth comforts false, worse than true wrongs." Northumberland, I.1: "He that but fears the thing he would not know / Hath by instinct knowledge from others' eyes / That what he fear'd is chanced."
   - What the action does: Lord Bardolph gives his barony on the word of a stranger on a stolen horse; Northumberland reads the death in Morton's face before he speaks. Later Warwick says "Rumour doth double, like the voice and echo, / The numbers of the feared" (III.1), and the Archbishop's fifty thousand turn out to be a number nobody counted. The play's whole first movement is built on reports, and the only reliable one is the look on a man's face.

2. **Hope is a way of using "the names of men instead of men."**
   - Spoken: Lord Bardolph, I.3: "We fortify in paper and in figures, / Using the names of men instead of men, / Like one that draws the model of a house / Beyond his power to build it." Hastings: "it never yet did hurt / To lay down likelihoods and forms of hope."
   - What the action does: Hastings's view wins the council and loses the war. Northumberland's "cold intent" arrives at Gaultree: "Thus do the hopes we have in him touch ground / And dash themselves to pieces" (IV.1). The same man who abandoned Hotspur abandons the Archbishop, and the play lets Lady Percy name it beforehand: "The time was, father, that you broke your word" (II.3).

3. **The crowd is sick of whatever it has and hungry for whatever it lost.**
   - Spoken: the Archbishop, I.3: "An habitation giddy and unsure / Hath he that buildeth on the vulgar heart... Past and to come seems best; things present, worst."
   - What the action does: The Archbishop says this and then builds on the vulgar heart himself; his army, told there is peace, scatters "like a school broke up" (IV.2) before anyone can check whether the peace is real. The observation is true and it does not help the man who makes it.

4. **A promise can be kept to the letter and used as a weapon.**
   - Spoken: Lancaster, IV.2: "I pawn'd thee none. / I promised you redress of these same grievances / Whereof you did complain... But for you, rebels, look to taste the due / Meet for rebellion." And then: "God, and not we, hath safely fought today."
   - What the action does: The trick works perfectly and the play passes no sentence on it. Mowbray had felt it coming—"There is a thing within my bosom tells me / That no conditions of our peace can stand" (IV.1)—and was talked out of his instinct by an argument that "a peace is of the nature of a conquest." The only judgement the play offers is Falstaff's, in another key: "this same young sober-blooded boy doth not love me, nor a man cannot make him laugh; but that's no marvel, he drinks no wine" (IV.3). The reader is left to decide whether sobriety and treachery are the same temperament.

5. **The crown is the thing the father stole and the thing that ate him.**
   - Spoken: the King, III.1: "Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown." Hal, IV.5, over the sleeping King: "O polish'd perturbation! golden care!... thou best of gold art worst of gold... Hast eat thy bearer up."
   - What the action does: The King is told the rebellion is over and collapses: "wherefore should these good news make me sick?... Will Fortune never come with both hands full?" (IV.4). He dies in a room named Jerusalem, fulfilling a prophecy he had taken to mean the Holy Land. The crusade he promised at the end of Richard II is never begun; the wish to "wash the blood off" is answered by a pun.

6. **The past, rightly read, predicts the future, and reading it does not change it.**
   - Spoken: Warwick, III.1: "There is a history in all men's lives / Figuring the natures of the times deceased; / The which observed, a man may prophesy." The King: "O God, that one might read the book of fate... The happiest youth... Would shut the book, and sit him down and die."
   - What the action does: Richard II's prophecy about Northumberland the "ladder" is recited and has come true; the King's answer is "Are these things then necessities? / Then let us meet them like necessities." His own last advice to Hal—"busy giddy minds / With foreign quarrels" (IV.5)—is another prediction, and Prince John closes the play saying he hears "a bird" sing of France.

7. **A father can believe his son wants him dead, and the son's explanation can be both true and convenient.**
   - Spoken: the King, IV.5: "Thy wish was father, Harry, to that thought... Thou hid'st a thousand daggers in thy thoughts." Hal: "God witness with me, when I here came in, / And found no course of breath within your Majesty, / How cold it struck my heart!"
   - What the action does: The play shows Hal alone with the crown before anyone else does, and what he says there—"My due from thee is this imperial crown"—is close enough to what he later tells his father that you cannot convict him and not quite the same, so you cannot acquit him. The King accepts it instantly: "God put it in thy mind to take it hence, / That thou mightst win the more thy father's love." Belief, here, is a decision made by a dying man who needs to make it.

8. **A usurped crown becomes a lawful one by being inherited.**
   - Spoken: the King, IV.5: "what in me was purchased, / Falls upon thee in a more fairer sort; / So thou the garland wear'st successively." Hal: "You won it, wore it, kept it, gave it me; / Then plain and right must my possession be."
   - What the action does: Nobody disputes it. The new King is crowned without a rival, the Chief Justice stays, the brothers are reassured—"This is the English, not the Turkish court" (V.2). The soil of the deed does go into the earth with the father, exactly as he said. But the fix is still a policy: the son is told to find a foreign war so that memory will fade.

9. **A king who chooses law over his old friends is right, and the choice is a wound.**
   - Spoken: the Chief Justice, V.2: "I then did use the person of your father; / The image of his power lay then in me." The King: "You did commit me, / For which I do commit into your hand / Th' unstained sword."
   - What the action does: The Justice expected ruin and is made "father to my youth." Then in V.5 the same king says to Falstaff "I know thee not, old man. Fall to thy prayers," and "the grave doth gape / For thee thrice wider than for other men." The play places both scenes within minutes of each other so that you see one act from two sides: the upright judge rewarded, the old companion dropped. Prince John's verdict is "I like this fair proceeding"; the Hostess and Doll are meanwhile dragged to a whipping (V.4).

10. **Company is caught like a disease, and a man can know this and still be the disease someone else is cured of.**
   - Spoken: Falstaff, V.1: "It is certain that either wise bearing or ignorant carriage is caught, as men take diseases, one of another. Therefore let men take heed of their company." Warwick, IV.4: "The prince but studies his companions / Like a strange tongue... which once attained... comes to no further use / But to be known and hated."
   - What the action does: Falstaff says the sentence about Shallow's servants and is at that moment the thing Warwick describes. The King banishes "those that kept me company" with a pension and a promise of advancement "as we hear you do reform yourselves." Falstaff's reply—"I shall be sent for soon at night"—is the last self-deception of a man who has caught his own disease.

11. **Old men lie about their youth, and the lie is the only youth they have left.**
   - Spoken: Falstaff, III.2: "Lord, Lord, how subject we old men are to this vice of lying! This same starved justice hath done nothing but prate to me of the wildness of his youth... and every third word a lie." Shallow: "Jesu, Jesu, the mad days that I have spent! And to see how many of my old acquaintance are dead!"
   - What the action does: Shallow moves from "Death is certain" to "How a good yoke of bullocks at Stamford fair?" in a single breath, and Falstaff tells him "We have heard the chimes at midnight." Falstaff, who sees through Shallow's lies, tells the Chief Justice he is "only old in judgement and understanding" (I.2) and tells Doll "do not bid me remember mine end" (II.4). Poins, watching him with Doll: "Is it not strange that desire should so many years outlive performance?" The play is unsparing about age and does not exempt its wittiest man.

12. **The strong buy their way out of the war; the weak go, and one of them says the bravest thing in the play.**
   - Spoken: Feeble, III.2: "A man can die but once. We owe God a death... he that dies this year is quit for the next." Mouldy: "My old dame will be undone now."
   - What the action does: Mouldy and Bullcalf pay Bardolph three pounds and are released; Feeble, Shadow and Wart are pressed. Falstaff explains his choice as a theory of soldiering ("give me the spare men, and spare me the great ones"). At every level of the play, justice is a favour: Davy asks Shallow to "countenance" a knave because "the knave is mine honest friend" (V.1), and Shallow agrees.

13. **A grief that would look like hypocrisy must be hidden, and so the performance of wildness traps the performer.**
   - Spoken: Hal, II.2: "my heart bleeds inwardly that my father is so sick; and keeping such vile company as thou art hath in reason taken from me all ostentation of sorrow." Poins: "I would think thee a most princely hypocrite."
   - What the action does: The plan announced in the first part—to seem bad so that goodness will shine—has a cost the first part did not show: it makes sincerity unreadable. Hal's "Let the end try the man" is the only answer, and the end, when it comes, is the rejection of Falstaff, which is itself read by Falstaff as a performance: "he must seem thus to the world."

14. **Nobody cares whether the old companion comes back from the war, and the person who says so is the one who does.**
   - Spoken: Doll, II.4: "Thou art going to the wars, and whether I shall ever see thee again or no, there is nobody cares." The Hostess: "I have known thee these twenty-nine years... but an honester and truer-hearted man—well, fare thee well."
   - What the action does: In V.4 Doll and the Hostess are taken by beadles for a killing done "amongst" them and Pistol; Falstaff says "I will deliver her" and is arrested in the next scene. The affection in the tavern is the most unforced thing in the play, and it protects no one.

## What it teaches

This play is about what happens after the victory, and its answer is: sickness, lying, old age, and a king who cannot sleep. The first part let you think honour had been settled at Shrewsbury. The second shows the same king still fighting, by proxy and by trick, and the heir waiting in a tavern he has outgrown, weary enough to want small beer and sorry he wants it. Everything that was promised has to be paid for, and the play's recurring figure is the debt: Falstaff's to the Hostess, Shallow's thousand pounds, the dancer's epilogue "promising you infinitely" like "most debtors do."

The two scenes you will carry away are the ones that most resist each other. At Gaultree a cold young prince keeps the letter of his word, destroys the men who trusted it, and gives God the credit. At Westminster his brother confirms the judge who jailed him and banishes the friend who loved him, and the play calls this "fair proceeding." You may think both acts are necessary, and the text lets you. But it keeps Falstaff's voice in your ear—"he drinks no wine"—and it shows you the Hostess and Doll in the hands of beadles on the morning of the coronation. The order the new king establishes is real, and it is paid for by people who could not pay.

The hardest lesson is about Hal, because the play refuses to tell you whether he is good. He takes the crown from his father's pillow and the text shows you the moment; he explains it, and the text shows you the explanation; they match, mostly. His father believes him because he needs to, and the reader who believes him does the same. What the play does say clearly is that the plan from Part One worked and that it has made its author unreadable, even to himself: "Let the end try the man." The end does, and the end is "I know thee not."

## Preoccupations

- rumour, false report, and hearing what one wants to hear
- age, sickness, and the wearing out of desire
- debt: money owed, promises owed, time owed
- hope built on men who do not come
- policy that keeps the letter of a promise and breaks its meaning
- the crown as a burden that consumes its wearer
- history repeating because people cannot act on what they foresee
- reformation as the casting-off of friends
- the cheapness of the poor and the purchase of exemption
- the father and son who cannot read each other
