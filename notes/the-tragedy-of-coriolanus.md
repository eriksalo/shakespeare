# The Tragedy of Coriolanus

## What happens

Rome's hungry citizens riot against the patricians and win tribunes to speak for them. Caius Martius, the city's greatest soldier and the one who most openly despises the commons, goes to war with the Volsces, takes the city of Corioles almost single-handed, and comes home with a new name, Coriolanus, and the consulship within reach. The custom requires him to stand in the marketplace in a humble gown and ask the people for their voices; he does it with contempt, gets the voices, and loses them within the hour when the tribunes remind the citizens how he asked. His mother persuades him to go back and speak mildly; he lasts one word before he explodes, and is banished. He goes to his old enemy Aufidius, offers his throat or his sword, and is given an army. With Rome at his mercy he refuses his old general, refuses the friend who called him son, and yields only when his mother, wife, and child kneel to him. He makes peace, knowing it will cost him his life, returns to the Volsces, and is killed by Aufidius's faction when the word "boy" makes him boast of his victories in the city whose sons he killed.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A man who will not ask cannot hold power in a city where power must be asked for.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Coriolanus, II.3: "Better it is to die, better to starve, / Than crave the hire which first we do deserve." Menenius, III.1: "His nature is too noble for the world. / He would not flatter Neptune for his trident / Or Jove for's power to thunder. His heart's his mouth." Third Citizen, II.3: "if he would incline to the people, there was never a worthier man."
   - *What the action does with it:* The citizens actually give him their voices; nothing he did had to fail. What undoes him is the manner of asking ("I have your alms. Adieu"), which the tribunes can replay to the crowd. The play does not present the refusal to flatter as simple virtue: the First Officer in II.2 points out that "to seem to affect the malice and displeasure of the people is as bad as that which he dislikes, to flatter them for their love." Coriolanus's plainness is itself a performance of contempt, and it costs exactly what flattery would have bought.

2. **Dissembling is called honor in war and baseness in peace, and the difference may be nothing but custom.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Volumnia, III.2: "If it be honour in your wars to seem / The same you are not... how is it less or worse / That it shall hold companionship in peace / With honour as in war?" Coriolanus's answer: "Must I / With my base tongue give to my noble heart / A lie that it must bear?"; "Lest I surcease to honour mine own truth / And, by my body's action, teach my mind / A most inherent baseness."
   - *What the action does with it:* Both are right and the play lets them be. Volumnia is right that it would have worked: Menenius says the people "have pardons, being asked, as free / As words to little purpose," and the tribunes' whole plan depends on provoking him ("Put him to choler straight"). Coriolanus is right that acting changes the actor: when he does finally bend his nature, for the Volsces, Aufidius observes "He bowed his nature, never known before / But to be rough, unswayable, and free," and uses the softening to kill him. He could not perform a part without becoming it, and the play agrees with him.

3. **The charge of fickleness that the noble lay on the crowd fits the noble just as well.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Martius, I.1: "With every minute you do change a mind / And call him noble that was now your hate, / Him vile that was your garland." Coriolanus, IV.4, on his way to the enemy: "O world, thy slippery turns! Friends now fast sworn... shall within this hour, / On a dissension of a doit, break out / To bitterest enmity... So with me: / My birthplace hate I, and my love's upon / This enemy town."
   - *What the action does with it:* The citizens do change: they elect him, revoke it, banish him, and by IV.6 say "I ever said we were i' th' wrong when we banished him." But the play's largest reversals belong to the great: Coriolanus changes countries, Aufidius goes from "Let me twine / Mine arms about that body" (IV.5) to standing on the corpse (V.6). Coriolanus's speech about slippery turns is delivered in the act of making one. The many-headed beast turns out to be a description of everybody.

4. **"What is the city but the people?" is one answer; the play tests it against the man who says the city is him.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Sicinius, III.1: "What is the city but the people?" Plebeians: "True, / The people are the city." Coriolanus, III.3: "You common cry of curs... I banish you!... There is a world elsewhere." Menenius's fable, I.1: "The senators of Rome are this good belly, / And you the mutinous members."
   - *What the action does with it:* The play rejects both the fable and the slogan. The belly's claim that it distributes to all is contradicted on the spot by the citizen ("Suffer us to famish, and their storehouses crammed with grain") and by Martius's wish to "make a quarry / With thousands of these quartered slaves." But the tribunes' city of the people is shown being managed from behind ("Lay the fault on us... Harp on that still," II.3). As for "a world elsewhere": there is none. Outside Rome Coriolanus becomes "a kind of nothing, titleless" (Cominius, V.1), and inside Antium he is only a weapon until he is a corpse.

5. **No one is the author of himself; the person who made you can unmake you.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Coriolanus, V.3: "I'll never / Be such a gosling to obey instinct, but stand / As if a man were author of himself, / And knew no other kin." Volumnia, I.3: "To a cruel war I sent him." Volumnia, III.2: "Thy valiantness was mine; thou suck'dst it from me, / But owe thy pride thyself." First Citizen, I.1: "he did it to please his mother and to be partly proud."
   - *What the action does with it:* The claim to self-authorship is made in the speech that immediately precedes his surrender to his mother, and he knows what the surrender means: "Most dangerously you have with him prevailed, / If not most mortal to him." The woman who sent him to war and taught him that a bloody brow "more becomes a man / Than gilt his trophy" is the one person who can bring him to his knees, and she does it by kneeling. The play lets her be honored ("the life of Rome!") and lets him die of it, in the same act.

6. **Praise wounds the man who most deserves it, and the virtue that refuses reward is the same trait that refuses to be seen.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Martius, I.9: "I have some wounds upon me, and they smart / To hear themselves remembered"; "When drums and trumpets shall / I' th' field prove flatterers, let courts and cities be / Made all of false-faced soothing!" Coriolanus, II.2: "I had rather have my wounds to heal again / Than hear say how I got them."
   - *What the action does with it:* Refusing the tenth of the spoils (I.9) looks like pure nobility, and Cominius calls his reticence a "concealment / Worse than a theft." But the same refusal, carried into the marketplace, is what makes him unable to show the scars the custom requires, and the citizens notice: "No man saw 'em" (II.3). The play does not separate the admirable from the fatal. He also forgets the name of the poor man who sheltered him, in the one moment he asks for something (I.9). What he cannot bear in public he craves from one person: "My praises made thee first a soldier" (Volumnia, III.2).

7. **Everyone demands gratitude and no one gives it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Third Citizen, II.3: "Ingratitude is monstrous, and for the multitude to be ingrateful were to make a monster of the multitude." Menenius, III.1: "Rome, whose gratitude / Towards her deserved children is enrolled / In Jove's own book, like an unnatural dam / Should now eat up her own." Coriolanus, IV.5: "my thankless country."
   - *What the action does with it:* Rome is ungrateful; the play grants it fully. But Coriolanus's answer is a larger ingratitude: "Wife, mother, child, I know not" (V.2); he tells Menenius, who "godded" him, that pity would be "folly / For one poor grain or two to leave unburnt." Aufidius, who was given Rome by Coriolanus's sword, kills him for it. The only gratitude in the play is the city's to Volumnia at the end, and it is paid to the person whose victory costs her son.

8. **What a virtue is depends on who is interpreting it and when; power leaves no monument but the chair it sat in.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Aufidius, IV.7: "So our virtues / Lie in th' interpretation of the time, / And power, unto itself most commendable, / Hath not a tomb so evident as a chair / T' extol what it hath done. / One fire drives out one fire, one nail one nail." His prediction: "When, Caius, Rome is thine, / Thou art poor'st of all; then shortly art thou mine."
   - *What the action does with it:* The same deeds at Corioles are "the whole name of the war" in II.1, "treason" to the tribunes in III.3, and "blind fortune" in Aufidius's mouth in V.6. Nothing in the deeds changes. Aufidius's prediction comes true even though Rome is not taken: the moment Coriolanus's usefulness ends, so does his life. The chair is Aufidius's; the tomb is "a noble memory," offered by the man who stood on the body.

9. **War makes men need each other and peace makes them hate each other.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Third Servingman, IV.5: peace "makes men hate one another... because they then less need one another." First Servingman: "Peace is a very apoplexy, lethargy." Martius, I.1, on war: "we shall ha' means to vent / Our musty superfluity."
   - *What the action does with it:* The servants' cynicism is borne out by their betters. Aufidius and Martius embrace in the prospect of war ("more dances my rapt heart / Than when I first my wedded mistress saw") and fall out the moment there is a peace to divide. The tribunes thrive on a quiet city and the nobles on an endangered one (Sicinius, IV.6: "Here do we make his friends / Blush that the world goes well"). But the play also counts the cost the servants omit: Antium's "widowed and unchilded many a one," and the crowd in V.6 crying "He killed my son! My daughter!"

10. **Remembering wrongs is not honorable; the merciful act is the right one and the fatal one.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Volumnia, V.3: "Think'st thou it honourable for a noble man / Still to remember wrongs?"; if he burns Rome his chronicle will read "The man was noble, / But with his last attempt he wiped it out." Coriolanus, V.3: "The gods look down, and this unnatural scene / They laugh at."
    - *What the action does with it:* He chooses mercy, calls it "a happy victory to Rome," and goes back to be killed for it as "boy of tears." The play confirms Volumnia's account of what his name would have become, and it also shows that the mercy buys him nothing but a good corpse. The last lines give him "a noble memory" from his murderer, which is exactly the kind of praise he could never stand to hear.

11. **A name is given by others and can be taken by others; a man who thinks he made himself does not even own his name.**
    - *Where it is spoken:* Cominius, I.9: "call him... Caius Martius Coriolanus! Bear / Th' addition nobly ever!" Coriolanus, IV.5: "Only that name remains." Cominius, V.1: "'Coriolanus' / He would not answer to, forbade all names. / He was a kind of nothing, titleless." Aufidius, V.6: "thy stol'n name / Coriolanus, in Corioles"; "Name not the god, thou boy of tears."
    - *What the action does with it:* The name is conferred by a general in a camp, stripped by a city in a forum, declined by its owner in a tent, and finally reduced by a rival to "boy." It is the word "boy" that kills him: it makes him recite "Alone I did it" in a square full of the bereaved. His identity is held together entirely by the words other people use, and he is the last to know it.

## What it teaches

The play is about a man who has exactly one way of being, and a world that needs him to have two. In war his single nature is worth a city; in peace it is unusable, and the people who love him best are the ones who ask him to counterfeit. What is hard about the play is that it refuses to say the counterfeit would have been harmless. Volumnia's argument is sound (you dissemble in battle, why not in the forum?), and his reply is also sound: a body that kneels teaches the mind to kneel. When he finally does learn to bend, at his mother's knee, the bending kills him. A reader who wants the moral to be "he should have been more flexible" has to account for the fact that his one act of flexibility is the one for which he is torn to pieces.

The second difficulty is the people. Coriolanus's contempt for them is ugly and the play shows them deserving some of it: they change their minds on cue, they are steered by two men who tell them to "lay the fault on us," they shout for a banishment and then say it was "against our will." But the play also shows them hungry while the storehouses are full, and shows the belly's fable for the fob-off it is. No faction in the play is right about the city. The nobles think it is the senate, the tribunes say it is the people, Coriolanus believes it is wherever he happens to be standing, and the ending suggests it is the thing that walks in from the gate and kneels: a mother, a wife, a child, the bonds he had sworn to break.

What stays with you is the speech about being "author of himself," because the play builds its whole last movement to refute it as gently and as completely as possible. He says it; his mother kneels; he holds her hand in silence; and then he says "What have you done?" It is the only question in the play he cannot answer with his sword. Nobody made themselves. The person who made you can still move you, and the cost of being moved can be everything, and it is still the right thing to be moved. The play does not pretend that this is a consolation. It gives him a noble memory and a dead march and leaves the word "boy" hanging in the air.

## Preoccupations

- the gap between what people say and what they feel (asking for votes, flattery, performing humility)
- whether a person can be one thing only, and what it costs to bend
- mothers and sons; who made whom
- the crowd's changeability, and the great men's
- the city as a body: who is the belly, who the limbs
- ingratitude demanded and ingratitude given
- names and titles as things conferred and stripped by others
- war as the only condition in which some people can live
- mercy as the right act that gets you killed
